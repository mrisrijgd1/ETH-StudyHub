Why it matters for the exam

Every Linear Algebra I exam has a determinant problem. “State the definition of the determinant” is worth 1–4 points almost every year (HS17, 6 (a); FS18, 6 (a); FS19, problem 3 (a)): today's axioms are one correct answer. Computations of structured determinants by row operations are worth up to 9 points (FS20, problem 6 (a); FS19, problem 3 (d); Exercises 3 and 4); the tridiagonal one of HS17, 6 (c) needs expansion along a row and waits for lesson 20. The rules $\det(AB)=\det A\det B$ and $\det A\ne0\iff A$ invertible decide many true/false items (FS17, (vi), (xxv), (xxvi); FS19, (IX); FS20, (VIII)–(IX); HS17, (xxii)–(xxiii); HS18, (IX); HS20, (13); Worked example 3), HS17, 6 (b) asks to prove $\det(AB^T)=0$ for $m>n$ (Exercise 2), and FS18, 6 (b) asks for $\det(I_m+AB)=\det(I_n+BA)$ (Exercise 5).

Recap of lesson 17 (5 minutes)

Row operations are left multiplications by elementary matrices $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)$ (add $\lambda\cdot L_j$ to $L_i$), $P_{i,j}$ (swap), $S_i(\mu)$ (scale, $\mu\ne0$). Every invertible matrix is a product of elementary matrices; $A$ is invertible iff $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$ iff $A$ is row equivalent to $I_n$. Check yourself:

1. The story: the measure of the ledger

Every ledger of the Free City turns the city's square plot into a parallelogram: its rows are the new edges. The surveyors want one number that says how much land the ledger produces, with a sign that says whether the plot was turned over. For two rows they find it at once: $ad-bc$.

For larger ledgers nobody can draw the picture, so the surveyors write down what any honest measure of land must obey. Stretching one edge stretches the land by the same factor, and the land of two plots glued along an edge adds up. A plot whose two edges coincide is flat and has no land. And the city's own square plot has land $1$. These three rules are the axioms of the determinant.

The surprise of this lesson: the three rules already decide everything. They say how each of the clerks' stamps from lesson 17 changes the measure, so the measure of any ledger can be computed by tidying it. They show that a ledger has nonzero measure exactly when it is reversible, that measures multiply, and that at most one measure can obey the rules. That one exists is lesson 19.

In mathematics: $\det:M_{n\times n}(K)\to K$ is the unique function that is linear in each row, vanishes on matrices with two equal rows, and has $\det I_n=1$.

2. The $2\times2$ case: area with a sign

By lesson 17, Exercise 5, $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ is invertible iff $ad-bc\ne0$ (script Proposition 4.1.1). Write $\det A:=ad-bc$. Over $\mathbb R$ this number has a picture (script Proposition 4.1.2 and Notes 4.1.3–4.1.4, pp. 122–123): $m_A$ maps the unit square to the parallelogram spanned by the columns $(a,c)$ and $(b,d)$, whose area is $|ad-bc|$. The sign records the orientation: $\det A>0$ if the second edge lies to the left of the first, as for $e_1,e_2$; $\det A<0$ if the picture is mirrored. A singular $A$ squashes the square into a segment or a point: area $0$.

Try it: the signed area

Drag the sliders for the columns $(a,c)$ and $(b,d)$ of $A$. The parallelogram is the image of the unit square; its area is $|\det A|$, and its colour shows the sign.

3. The axioms, and what row operations do

Definition (the determinant; script Definition 4.2.1, p. 125). Write a matrix by its rows, $A=(v_1;\dots;v_n)$ with $v_i\in K^n$ as rows. A determinant is a function $\det:M_{n\times n}(K)\to K$ with

  • (D1) multilinear in the rows: for each $i$, (a) $\det(\dots;v_i+w;\dots)=\det(\dots;v_i;\dots)+\det(\dots;w;\dots)$ and (b) $\det(\dots;\lambda v_i;\dots)=\lambda\det(\dots;v_i;\dots)$, the other rows fixed;
  • (D2) alternating: if two rows of $A$ are equal, $\det A=0$;
  • (D3) normalised: $\det I_n=1$.

The script states as a theorem that exactly one such function exists. In this lesson, $\det$ is any function with (D1)–(D3); we derive its properties and prove there is at most one. Existence is lesson 19. We also write $|A|$ for $\det A$.

Proof A: how row operations change the determinant (script Propositions 4.2.2 and 4.2.3, p. 126)

Claim. Let $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ and $\lambda\in K$.

  1. (D4) $\det(\lambda A)=\lambda^n\det A$; (D5) if $A$ has a zero row, $\det A=0$.
  2. Scaling a row by $\lambda$ multiplies $\det$ by $\lambda$: $\det(S_i(\lambda)A)=\lambda\det A$.
  3. (D6) Swapping two rows changes the sign: $\det(P_{i,j}A)=-\det A$.
  4. (D7) Adding a multiple of one row to another does not change $\det$: $\det(Q_{i,j}(\lambda)A)=\det A$ for $i\ne j$.

The idea. (D4), (D5) and scaling are (D1)(b). For the swap, put $v_i+v_j$ into both places: the determinant is $0$ by (D2), and expanding by (D1)(a) gives four terms, two of which vanish; the other two are $\det A$ and the swapped determinant. For (D7), expand the new row $v_i+\lambda v_j$: the extra term has two equal rows.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (D6) First line: “Let $B$ have $v_i+v_j$ in rows $i$ and $j$. By (D2), $\det B=0$.” Last line: “so $\det A+\det(P_{i,j}A)=0$.”
  • (D7) First line: “By (D1), $\det(\dots;v_i+\lambda v_j;\dots)=\det A+\lambda\det(\dots;v_j;\dots;v_j;\dots)$.”
  • The gap: expand with (D1)(a); kill terms with two equal rows by (D2).
The storyWhat you write

Stretch every edge by $\lambda$ and the land grows by $\lambda$ for each edge; an edge of length zero leaves no land.

(1) Apply (D1)(b) to each of the $n$ rows of $\lambda A$: $\det(\lambda A)=\lambda^n\det A$. If row $i$ is $0=0\cdot0$, (D1)(b) gives $\det A=0\cdot\det A=0$. (2) is (D1)(b) for row $i$.

A plot with the same diagonal edge twice is flat; split it, and the two mirror plots must cancel.

(3) Let $B$ have $v_i+v_j$ in rows $i$ and $j$, other rows as in $A$. By (D2), $\det B=0$. Expanding rows $i$ and $j$ by (D1)(a) gives four terms, with rows $(i,j)$ equal to $(v_i,v_i)$, $(v_i,v_j)$, $(v_j,v_i)$, $(v_j,v_j)$. The first and last are $0$ by (D2). So $0=\det A+\det(P_{i,j}A)$.

Sliding one edge along another (a shear) does not change the land.

(4) Row $i$ of $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)A$ is $v_i+\lambda v_j$. By (D1)(a) and (b), $\det(Q_{i,j}(\lambda)A)=\det A+\lambda\det C$, where $C$ has $v_j$ in rows $i$ and $j$. By (D2), $\det C=0$. ∎

Moves used: expand a row by multilinearity compute one element in two ways

The proof in one piece

(D1)(b) row by row gives $\det(\lambda A)=\lambda^n\det A$, and $\det A=0$ for a zero row; scaling one row is (D1)(b). Swap: with $B$ having $v_i+v_j$ in rows $i,j$, (D2) and (D1)(a) give $0=\det B=0+\det A+\det(P_{i,j}A)+0$. Adding: (D1) gives $\det(Q_{i,j}(\lambda)A)=\det A+\lambda\det C$ with $C$ having two equal rows, so $\det C=0$ by (D2). ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Scale: (D1)(b). Zero row: scale it by $0$.
  2. Swap: put the sum into both places, expand, two terms die.
  3. Add: expand, the extra term has two equal rows.

In short: scale ×λ, swap ×(−1), add ×1. So Gaussian elimination computes determinants: eliminate to a triangular matrix and keep track of swaps and scalings.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Is $\det(A+B)=\det A+\det B$?
    Answer

    No: (D1) is linearity in one row at a time. $\det(I_2+I_2)=\det(2I_2)=4\ne2$ (by (D4)).

  2. Rung 2. Show the converse of the swap rule when $1+1\ne0$ in $K$: if $\det$ satisfies (D1) and changes sign under swaps, then (D2) holds.
    Answer

    If rows $i$ and $j$ of $A$ are equal, swapping them does not change $A$, so $\det A=-\det A$, i.e. $2\det A=0$ and $\det A=0$, provided $2\ne0$ in $K$. Over $\mathbb F_2$ this fails: there $-1=1$.

Proof B: the determinant of a triangular matrix (script Proposition 4.2.5, p. 128)

Claim. If $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ is upper triangular with diagonal entries $a_1,\dots,a_n$, then $\det A=a_1a_2\cdots a_n$. The same holds for lower triangular matrices.

The idea. If all $a_i\ne0$, clear the entries above the diagonal from the bottom up, which does not change $\det$ (D7); then pull the diagonal entries out (D1)(b) and use $\det I_n=1$. If some $a_i=0$, clearing upwards from the rows below it produces a zero row.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Case all $a_i\ne0$, first line: “Operations $L_i-\lambda L_j\to L_i$ with $j>i$ turn $A$ into $\mathrm{diag}(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ without changing $\det$.”
  • Case some $a_i=0$, first line: “Let $i_0$ be the largest index with $a_{i_0}=0$.” Last line: “row $i_0$ becomes zero, so $\det A=0=a_1\cdots a_n$.”
  • The gap: Proof A (D7), (D1)(b), (D5), (D3).
The storyWhat you write

A staircase plot can be sheared into a box without changing its land.

Let all $a_i\ne0$. Going from row $n$ up to row $1$, subtract multiples of row $j$ from the rows $i<j$ to clear column $j$ above the diagonal. Rows below $j$ are already zero in column $j$, so nothing else changes, and $\det$ is unchanged (D7). The result is $D=\mathrm{diag}(a_1,\dots,a_n)$.

The land of a box is the product of its edge lengths.

By (D1)(b) in each row, $\det D=a_1\cdots a_n\det I_n=a_1\cdots a_n$ (D3).

A missing step flattens the plot.

If some $a_i=0$, let $i_0$ be the largest such index. The rows $i_0+1,\dots,n$ have nonzero diagonal entries; use them as above to clear row $i_0$ in the columns $>i_0$ (D7). Row $i_0$ was zero in columns $\le i_0$, so it becomes a zero row, and $\det A=0=a_1\cdots a_n$ by (D5). For lower triangular matrices, clear downwards and take the smallest such index. ∎

Moves used: write each row operation as a matrix

The proof in one piece

If all $a_i\ne0$: clearing upwards (D7) gives $\mathrm{diag}(a_1,\dots,a_n)$, and (D1)(b), (D3) give $\det A=a_1\cdots a_n$. If $a_{i_0}=0$ with $i_0$ maximal, clearing row $i_0$ with the rows below (D7) makes it a zero row, so $\det A=0$ (D5), which is the product. Lower triangular: the same downwards. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Nonzero diagonal: shear upwards to a diagonal matrix, pull out the entries.
  2. A zero on the diagonal: the last one becomes a zero row.

In short: triangular $\Rightarrow$ product of the diagonal. Together with Proof A this is the standard way to compute any determinant.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. $\det\begin{pmatrix}2&7&-1\\0&-3&5\\0&0&4\end{pmatrix}$?
    Answer

    $2\cdot(-3)\cdot4=-24$.

  2. Rung 2. Why do we clear upwards starting from the last row?
    Answer

    Row $n$ has only one nonzero entry, $a_n$ in column $n$; subtracting multiples of it from rows above changes only column $n$. Then row $n-1$ has nonzero entries only in column $n-1$ (column $n$ is already cleared), and so on: no step destroys earlier work.

4. Invertible, multiplicative, unique

Proof C: $\det A\ne0$ if and only if $A$ is invertible (script Corollary 4.2.6, p. 128)

Claim. For $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$: $\det A\ne0\iff\mathrm{rank}(A)=n\iff A$ is invertible.

The idea. Row operations multiply $\det$ by nonzero numbers only ($\lambda\ne0$, $-1$, $1$), so they never turn a nonzero determinant into zero or back. An echelon form is triangular; its diagonal has a zero exactly when it has fewer than $n$ pivots.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $B$ be a row echelon form of $A$. By Proof A, $\det B=c\det A$ with $c\ne0$.”
  • Last line: “$\det A\ne0\iff\det B\ne0\iff\mathrm{rank}(B)=n\iff\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$.”
  • The gap: Proof B for the square echelon matrix $B$; lesson 17, Proof C for the second equivalence.
The storyWhat you write

Tidying never creates or destroys land completely.

Let row operations turn $A$ into a row echelon form $B$. Each step multiplies $\det$ by $\lambda\ne0$, by $-1$ or by $1$ (Proof A), so $\det B=c\det A$ with $c\ne0$, and $\det A\ne0\iff\det B\ne0$.

A tidy ledger has land exactly when every step of the staircase is there.

$B$ is square and in echelon form, hence upper triangular. If $\mathrm{rank}(B)=n$, all $n$ diagonal entries are pivots, nonzero, and $\det B\ne0$ (Proof B). If $\mathrm{rank}(B)<n$, the last row is zero and $\det B=0$ (D5).

And full rank is reversibility.

$\mathrm{rank}(B)=\mathrm{rank}(A)$ (lesson 16), and $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n\iff A$ invertible (lesson 17, Proof C). ∎

Moves used: write each row operation as a matrix a chain of named equivalences

The proof in one piece

Row operations multiply $\det$ by nonzero factors (Proof A), so $\det A\ne0$ iff $\det B\ne0$ for a row echelon form $B$. $B$ is upper triangular; it has $n$ nonzero pivots on the diagonal iff $\mathrm{rank}(B)=n$, and then $\det B\ne0$ (Proof B); otherwise its last row is zero and $\det B=0$. Finally $\mathrm{rank}(B)=\mathrm{rank}(A)$, and rank $n$ means invertible (lesson 17). ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Row operations: $\det$ changes by a nonzero factor.
  2. Echelon form is triangular: zero on the diagonal iff rank $<n$.

In short: $\det A\ne0\iff A$ invertible. This is the item that lesson 17's list of equivalences was waiting for.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. For which $t$ is $\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\t&4\end{pmatrix}$ invertible?
    Answer

    $\det=4-t^2\ne0$ iff $t\ne\pm2$.

  2. Rung 2. Why does the proof need that scaling is only by $\lambda\ne0$?
    Answer

    Multiplying a row by $0$ is not a row operation (it is not reversible) and would set $\det$ to $0$; with $\lambda\ne0$ the factor $c$ is a product of nonzero numbers, hence nonzero, which is what “$\det A\ne0\iff\det B\ne0$” needs.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof C from the frame alone.

Proof D: $\det(AB)=\det A\cdot\det B$ (script Lemma 4.2.8 and Proposition 4.2.9, pp. 129–130)

Claim. (1) $\det S_i(\mu)=\mu$, $\det P_{i,j}=-1$, $\det Q_{i,j}(\lambda)=1$, and $\det(TB)=\det T\det B$ for every elementary $T$ and every $B$. (2) For all $A,B\in M_{n\times n}(K)$: $\det(AB)=\det A\det B$.

The idea. Proof A says exactly how an elementary $T$ changes $\det B$; applied to $B=I_n$ it gives $\det T$, and comparing yields (1). An invertible $A$ is a product of elementary matrices (lesson 17, Proof E), so (2) follows by induction on the number of factors. A non-invertible $A$ makes both sides $0$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (1) First line: “By Proof A with $B=I_n$: $\det S_i(\mu)=\mu\det I_n=\mu$.”
  • (2) First line: “Case $\mathrm{rank}(A)<n$.” Then: “Case $A$ invertible: $A=T_1\cdots T_k$.” Last line: “$=\det A\det B$.”
  • The gap: $\mathrm{rank}(AB)\le\mathrm{rank}(A)$ (lesson 15) and induction on $k$.
The storyWhat you write

Each stamp has its own measure: stretch $\mu$, mirror $-1$, shear $1$.

(1) $S_i(\mu)=S_i(\mu)I_n$, so by Proof A $\det S_i(\mu)=\mu\det I_n=\mu$; likewise $\det P_{i,j}=-1$ and $\det Q_{i,j}(\lambda)=1$. Proof A says $\det(TB)=c_T\det B$ with exactly these factors $c_T$, so $\det(TB)=\det T\det B$.

If the first ledger flattens the land, so does the relay.

(2) If $\mathrm{rank}(A)<n$, then $\mathrm{rank}(AB)\le\mathrm{rank}(A)<n$ (lesson 15), so $\det(AB)=0=\det A\det B$ (Proof C).

A reversible ledger is a pile of stamps, and measures of stamps multiply one by one.

If $A$ is invertible, $A=T_1\cdots T_k$ with elementary $T_s$ (lesson 17, Proof E). Induction on $k$: $k=1$ is (1). For $k>1$: $\det(AB)=\det(T_1(T_2\cdots T_kB))=\det T_1\det(T_2\cdots T_kB)$ by (1), $=\det T_1\det(T_2\cdots T_k)\det B$ by the hypothesis, $=\det(T_1\cdots T_k)\det B$ by (1) again. ∎

Moves used: write each row operation as a matrix induction: base, hypothesis, step

The proof in one piece

Proof A applied to $I_n$ gives $\det S_i(\mu)=\mu$, $\det P_{i,j}=-1$, $\det Q_{i,j}(\lambda)=1$, and then Proof A reads $\det(TB)=\det T\det B$ for elementary $T$. If $\mathrm{rank}(A)<n$, then $\mathrm{rank}(AB)<n$ and both sides are $0$ (Proof C). Otherwise $A=T_1\cdots T_k$, and induction on $k$ gives $\det(AB)=\det T_1\det(T_2\cdots T_kB)=\det T_1\det(T_2\cdots T_k)\det B=\det A\det B$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Determinants of the three elementary types.
  2. Singular $A$: both sides $0$.
  3. Invertible $A$: product of elementary matrices, induction.

In short: $\det$ is multiplicative. Consequences you use constantly: $\det(A^{-1})=1/\det A$, $\det(P^{-1}AP)=\det A$ (similar matrices have equal determinants), $\det(AB)=\det(BA)$.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Show $\det(A^{-1})=(\det A)^{-1}$.
    Answer

    $1=\det I_n=\det(AA^{-1})=\det A\det A^{-1}$.

  2. Rung 2. Is $\det(AB)=\det(BA)$ for square $A,B$? Is $\det(A+B)$ determined by $\det A$ and $\det B$?
    Answer

    Yes: both are $\det A\det B$, products in the field commute. No: $\det(I_2+I_2)=4$, but $\det\big(I_2+(-I_2)\big)=0$, although $\det(-I_2)=1=\det I_2$.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof D from the frame alone.

Proof E: at most one function satisfies the axioms (script Corollary 4.2.10, p. 130)

Claim. If $\det$ and $\widetilde{\det}$ both satisfy (D1)–(D3) on $M_{n\times n}(K)$, then $\det=\widetilde{\det}$.

The idea. Everything we proved used only (D1)–(D3), so it holds for both functions. Both vanish on singular matrices, and on an invertible matrix both equal the same product of the values on elementary matrices, which the axioms fix.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Proofs A–D hold for both functions, since they use only (D1)–(D3).”
  • Last line: “so $\det A=\widetilde{\det}A$ for all $A$.”
  • The gap: the values on elementary matrices are forced (Proof D (1)).
The storyWhat you write

Two honest surveyors agree on flat land.

If $\mathrm{rank}(A)<n$: $\det A=0=\widetilde{\det}A$ by Proof C for each function.

And on every stamp, hence on every pile of stamps.

If $A$ is invertible, $A=T_1\cdots T_k$ (lesson 17). By Proof D for each function, $\det A=\det T_1\cdots\det T_k$ and $\widetilde{\det}A=\widetilde{\det}T_1\cdots\widetilde{\det}T_k$, and $\det T_s=\widetilde{\det}T_s\in\{\mu,-1,1\}$ by Proof D (1). ∎

Moves used: two linear maps that agree on a basis are equal write each row operation as a matrix

The proof in one piece

Proofs A–D use only (D1)–(D3), so they hold for $\det$ and $\widetilde{\det}$. For $\mathrm{rank}(A)<n$ both are $0$. Otherwise $A=T_1\cdots T_k$ with elementary $T_s$, and both functions give $\prod_s\det T_s$, where each factor is $\mu$, $-1$ or $1$ according to the type. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Both satisfy Proofs A–D.
  2. Singular: both $0$. Invertible: same product over elementary factors.

In short: the axioms leave no freedom. The pill compares with “agree on a basis”: here the elementary matrices play the role of the generators.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Why does Proof E not prove that a determinant exists?
    Answer

    It only compares two functions that are assumed to exist. Defining $\det A$ as “the product over an elimination” would need a proof that every elimination gives the same number (well-definedness), which is the hard part; lesson 19 avoids it with the Leibniz formula.

  2. Rung 2. Let $f:M_{n\times n}(K)\to K$ satisfy (D1) and (D2), but not necessarily (D3). Show $f(A)=f(I_n)\det A$.
    Answer

    Proofs A–D (except the normalisation) hold for $f$ with $f(I_n)$ in place of $1$: $f(T)=c_Tf(I_n)$ and $f(TB)=c_Tf(B)$. So $f(T_1\cdots T_k)=c_{T_1}\cdots c_{T_k}f(I_n)=\det A\cdot f(I_n)$, and both sides are $0$ for singular $A$. If $f(I_n)\ne0$, then $f/f(I_n)$ satisfies (D1)–(D3) and Proof E applies.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof E from the frame alone.

Worked examples: a mixed set before the exercises

Worked example 1: a determinant by elimination (script Example 4.2.7)

Compute $\det A$ for $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&2\\3&-2&0\\4&1&4\end{pmatrix}$ using only Proofs A and B.

  1. The $(1,1)$ entry is $0$. What is the first step, and what does it cost?
    Answer

    Swap $L_1\leftrightarrow L_2$. A swap multiplies the determinant by $-1$ (Proof A), so keep a running sign $-1$. Rows now: $(3,-2,0)$, $(0,1,2)$, $(4,1,4)$.

  2. Clear the rest of the first column and the second column.
    Answer

    $L_3-\tfrac43L_1$: $(0,\tfrac{11}3,4)$. $L_3-\tfrac{11}3L_2$: $(0,0,4-\tfrac{22}3)=(0,0,-\tfrac{10}3)$. Adding a multiple of another row changes nothing (Proof A).

  3. Read off the determinant, and check it.
    Answer

    The result is upper triangular with diagonal $3,1,-\tfrac{10}3$, so by Proof B its determinant is $-10$, and $\det A=(-1)\cdot(-10)=10$. Check with the expansion formula for $3\times3$ matrices (proved in lesson 20): $0\cdot(-8-0)-1\cdot(12-0)+2\cdot(3+8)=-12+22=10$ ✓. The explorer above starts with this matrix.

Worked example 2: a multiple-choice determinant (FS20, (IX))

The determinant of $\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-3\\-1&2&3\\1&-2&3\end{pmatrix}$ is (a) $0$; (b) $2$; (c) $12$; (d) $24$.

  1. Eliminate.
    Answer

    $L_2+L_1$: $(0,4,0)$. $L_3-L_1$: $(0,-4,6)$. $L_3+L_2$: $(0,0,6)$. No swaps, no scalings.

  2. Answer.
    Answer

    Diagonal $1,4,6$, so $\det=24$: (d). Under exam pressure the elimination is safer than the formula with six products, because each step is one subtraction you can check.

Worked example 3: quick exam items (FS17, FS19, FS20, HS18, HS20)

  1. FS19, (IX): which rule fails in general for $A,B\in M_{n\times n}$? (a) $\det(2A)=2\det A$; (b) $\det A\det B=\det(AB)$; (c) $\det(AB)=\det(BA)$; (d) $\det I_n=1$.
    Answer

    (a). $2A$ scales each of the $n$ rows by $2$, so $\det(2A)=2^n\det A$ (Proof A, $n$ times); for $n=2$, $\det(2I_2)=4\ne2$. (b) is Proof D, (c) follows from (b) since $\det A\det B=\det B\det A$, and (d) is (D3).

  2. FS17, (vi): $\{A\in M_{n\times n}(K)\mid\det A=0\}$ is a subspace of $M_{n\times n}(K)$.
    Answer

    False for $n\ge2$: $E_{11}$ and $I_n-E_{11}$ both have a zero row, so determinant $0$, but their sum $I_n$ has determinant $1$. (For $n=1$ the set is $\{0\}$, which is a subspace.)

  3. FS17, (xxvi), and FS20, (VIII): is $\det:M_{n\times n}(K)\to K$ linear? Is it surjective?
    Answer

    Not linear for $n\ge2$: $\det(2I_n)=2^n\ne2\det I_n$. It is linear in each row separately (D1), which is a different statement. Surjective: yes, $\det S_1(t)=t$ for every $t\ne0$ and $\det 0=0$. So in FS20, (VIII) the false statement is (c), linearity; (a) is Proof C and (b) is Proof B.

  4. HS18, (IX): which property does the determinant not have? (a) invariance under swapping two columns; (b) $\det(AB)=\det A\det B$; (c) linearity in each column; (d) invariance under adding a column to another column.
    Answer

    (a): a swap changes the sign. Our axioms speak about rows; the column versions of (c) and (d) follow from $\det A^T=\det A$ (lesson 20), and (b) is Proof D.

  5. HS20, (13): for all $A,B\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb R)$, $\det(AB)-\det A-\det B+\det(A+B)=0$.
    Answer

    False. $n=2$, $A=B=I_2$: $1-1-1+4=3\ne0$. A test with the identity matrix is the fastest way to kill a false determinant identity.

Why it's cool

The determinant is the factor by which a linear map scales volumes, with a sign that says whether it flips orientation. In Analysis II this number returns as the Jacobian determinant in the change of variables formula: polar coordinates give $dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta$ because $\det\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}=r$. Computer graphics uses the sign every frame: a triangle whose $2\times2$ determinant turns negative has been flipped and faces away from the camera, so it is not drawn.

For wonder: elimination against $n!$ (optional, 5 min)

Lesson 19 writes the determinant as a sum over all permutations: $n!$ products of $n$ entries. For $n=20$ that is $20!\approx2.4\cdot10^{18}$ products; a computer doing a billion per second needs about 77 years. Elimination needs roughly $\tfrac13n^3\approx2700$ operations for the same matrix, a few microseconds. Same number, two routes: the formula is the one that proves existence, and the elimination of Proofs A and B is the one anybody actually uses.

Exercise 1 (HS17, 6 (a); FS18, 6 (a); HS20, problem 2 (c))

(a) State the definition of the determinant of a matrix $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$. (b) Compute $\det\begin{pmatrix}7&6&1\\5&6&6\\2&4&5\end{pmatrix}$ (in HS20 only the final number is marked).

Hint

(a) Name the map, then the three properties. (b) First make a small pivot: $L_1-L_2$ gives $(2,0,-5)$.

Solution

Frame: (a) the axioms; (b) elimination with Proofs A and B, then a check. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

(a) The determinant is the unique map $\det:M_{n\times n}(K)\to K$ such that (D1) $\det$ is linear in each row when the other rows are fixed, (D2) $\det A=0$ whenever two rows of $A$ are equal, and (D3) $\det I_n=1$. (Uniqueness is Proof E; existence is lesson 19.)

(b) $L_1-L_2$: $(2,0,-5)$; determinant unchanged. $L_3-L_1$: $(0,4,10)$. $L_2-\tfrac52L_1$: $(0,6,\tfrac{37}2)$. $L_3-\tfrac23L_2$: $(0,0,10-\tfrac{37}3)=(0,0,-\tfrac73)$. Diagonal $2,6,-\tfrac73$, so $\det=-28$.

Check with the $3\times3$ formula: $7(30-24)-6(25-12)+1(20-12)=42-78+8=-28$ ✓.

Exercise 2 (HS17, 6 (b), 3 points; FS17, (xxv))

Let $A,B\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ with $m>n$. Show that $\det(AB^T)=0$.

Hint

$AB^T$ is $m\times m$. Bound its rank by the rank of $A$, then use Proof C.

Solution

Frame: rank of a product, then Proof C. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

$AB^T\in M_{m\times m}(K)$. Every column of $AB^T$ is $A$ times a column of $B^T$, so it lies in the column space of $A$. Hence $\mathrm{rank}(AB^T)\le\mathrm{rank}(A)\le n<m$ (the rank is at most the number of columns, lesson 16). So $AB^T$ is not invertible, and $\det(AB^T)=0$ by Proof C. $\blacksquare$

The same argument shows: a product of an $m\times n$ and an $n\times m$ matrix with $m>n$ is never invertible. So FS17, (xxv) is true.

Exercise 3 (FS20, problem 6 (a), 9 points)

For $n\ge1$ and $a\ne1$ let $A_n\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb R)$ have entries $a$ on the diagonal and $1$ everywhere else. Show that $\det A_n=(a-1)^{n-1}(a+n-1)$.

Hint

All row sums are equal. Add all rows to the first row, take the common factor out of row 1, then subtract row 1 from every other row.

Solution

Frame: three row operations reach a triangular matrix; Proofs A and B. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

  1. Add $L_2,\dots,L_n$ to $L_1$. Each step adds another row, so the determinant does not change (Proof A). Every entry of the new first row is $a+(n-1)$.
  2. By linearity in row 1 (D1), $\det A_n=(a+n-1)\det A_n'$, where $A_n'$ has first row $(1,1,\dots,1)$ and the other rows of $A_n$. This uses only (D1), so it is valid even if $a+n-1=0$.
  3. For $i=2,\dots,n$ replace $L_i$ by $L_i-L_1$ (determinant unchanged). Row $i$ of $A_n$ is $(1,\dots,1,a,1,\dots,1)$ with $a$ in place $i$, so it becomes $(0,\dots,0,a-1,0,\dots,0)$.
  4. The result is upper triangular with diagonal $1,a-1,\dots,a-1$, so $\det A_n'=(a-1)^{n-1}$ (Proof B).

Hence $\det A_n=(a-1)^{n-1}(a+n-1)$. $\blacksquare$ Check: $n=2$: $a^2-1=(a-1)(a+1)$ ✓; $n=3$, $a=2$: the explorer's third preset gives $4=1^2\cdot4$ ✓. (The assumption $a\ne1$ is not needed for the formula; for $a=1$ both sides are $0$ when $n\ge2$.)

Exercise 4 (FS19, problem 3 (b)–(d), 14 points)

For $n\ge1$ let $M_n=\big(2^{i+j}\big)_{i,j=1,\dots,n}\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb R)$. (b) Write out $M_n$. (c) Compute $\det M_n$ for $n=1,2,3$. (d) Compute $\det M_n$ and $\mathrm{rank}\,M_n$ for all $n\ge1$.

Hint

$2^{i+j}=2^i\cdot2^j$. Compare row $i$ with row $1$.

Solution

Frame: every row is a multiple of the first; Proof A (or Proof C). (Checked twice, no official solution.)

(b) $M_n=\begin{pmatrix}4&8&\cdots&2^{n+1}\\8&16&\cdots&2^{n+2}\\\vdots&&&\vdots\\2^{n+1}&2^{n+2}&\cdots&2^{2n}\end{pmatrix}$, row $i$ equal to $2^{i-1}$ times row $1$.

(c) $\det M_1=4$. $\det M_2=4\cdot16-8\cdot8=0$. For $n=3$: $L_2-2L_1$ is a zero row, so $\det M_3=0$ (a zero row gives determinant $0$ by (D1)).

(d) Row $i=2^{i-1}\cdot$ row $1$, and row $1\ne0$, so the row space is spanned by row $1$: $\mathrm{rank}\,M_n=1$ for all $n$. Hence $\det M_1=4$ and $\det M_n=0$ for $n\ge2$ (Proof C, since the rank is $1<n$).

Exercise 5 (FS18, problem 6 (b), 6 points: Sylvester's identity)

Show that $\det(I_m+AB)=\det(I_n+BA)$ for all $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$, $B\in M_{n\times m}(K)$. You may use without proof that a block triangular matrix $\begin{pmatrix}M&U\\0&N\end{pmatrix}$ or $\begin{pmatrix}M&0\\V&N\end{pmatrix}$ with square blocks $M,N$ has determinant $\det M\det N$.

Hint

Let $X=\begin{pmatrix}I_m&-A\\B&I_n\end{pmatrix}$ and $Y=\begin{pmatrix}I_m&A\\0&I_n\end{pmatrix}$. Compute $YX$ and $XY$ and use Proof D.

Solution

Frame: one matrix $X$, two block triangular factorisations, Proof D. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

Block multiplication gives

$$YX=\begin{pmatrix}I_m+AB&0\\B&I_n\end{pmatrix},$$

$$XY=\begin{pmatrix}I_m&0\\B&I_n+BA\end{pmatrix}.$$

(Top right of $YX$: $-A+A=0$; top right of $XY$: $A-A=0$; bottom right of $XY$: $BA+I_n$.) By the hint, $\det Y=\det I_m\det I_n=1$. By Proof D, $\det(I_m+AB)=\det(YX)$ $=\det Y\det X=\det X$, and likewise $\det(I_n+BA)=\det(XY)$ $=\det X\det Y=\det X$. Both equal $\det X$, so $\det(I_m+AB)=\det(I_n+BA)$. $\blacksquare$

Note that $YX\ne XY$ in general; only their determinants agree, because $\det$ turns products into products of numbers, and numbers commute.

Exercise 6 (the $3\times3$ Vandermonde determinant)

Show that

$$\begin{aligned}&\det\begin{pmatrix}1&a&a^2\\1&b&b^2\\1&c&c^2\end{pmatrix}\\&\quad=(b-a)(c-a)(c-b).\end{aligned}$$

Conclude: if $a,b,c$ are pairwise different, then for all $y_1,y_2,y_3$ there is exactly one polynomial $p$ of degree at most $2$ with $p(a)=y_1$, $p(b)=y_2$, $p(c)=y_3$.

Hint

$L_2-L_1$ and $L_3-L_1$, then take $b-a$ out of row 2 and $c-a$ out of row 3. For the conclusion, write $p=x_0+x_1t+x_2t^2$: the conditions are a linear system for $(x_0,x_1,x_2)$.

Solution

Frame: elimination with factors taken out by (D1); then Proof C on a linear system.

$L_2-L_1$: $(0,\,b-a,\,b^2-a^2)$; $L_3-L_1$: $(0,\,c-a,\,c^2-a^2)$. Since $b^2-a^2=(b-a)(b+a)$, (D1) in row 2 takes out $b-a$, leaving $(0,1,b+a)$; likewise row 3 gives the factor $c-a$ and $(0,1,c+a)$. Then $L_3-L_2$: $(0,0,c-b)$. Proof B: the determinant is $(b-a)(c-a)\cdot1\cdot1\cdot(c-b)$. $\blacksquare$

The conditions read $V(x_0,x_1,x_2)^T=(y_1,y_2,y_3)^T$ with $V$ the matrix above. If $a,b,c$ are pairwise different, $\det V\ne0$, so $V$ is invertible (Proof C) and the system has exactly one solution (lesson 17, Proof C).

Exercise 7 (HS17, (xxii) and (xxiii): inverses and similar matrices)

(a) Show $\det(A^{-1})=\det(A)^{-1}$ for $A\in GL_n(K)$; is $\det(A^{-1})=-\det A$ (HS17, (xxii))? (b) Show that similar matrices ($B=S^{-1}AS$) have the same determinant. (c) HS17, (xxiii): two matrices are similar if and only if they have the same determinant. True or false?

Hint

(a), (b): apply $\det$ to $AA^{-1}=I_n$ and to $S^{-1}AS$. (c) Which matrices are similar to $I_n$?

Solution

Frame: Proof D three times; a counterexample with the identity.

(a) $1=\det I_n=\det(AA^{-1})=\det A\det(A^{-1})$, so $\det(A^{-1})=\det(A)^{-1}$. HS17, (xxii) is false: for $A=I_n$, $\det(A^{-1})=1\ne-1$.

(b) $\det(S^{-1}AS)=\det(S^{-1})\det A\det S=\det(S)^{-1}\det A\det S=\det A$.

(c) False. The direction “similar $\Rightarrow$ same determinant” is (b), but the converse fails: $S^{-1}I_2S=I_2$ for every $S$, so $I_2$ is similar only to itself, while $\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}\ne I_2$ also has determinant $1$.
