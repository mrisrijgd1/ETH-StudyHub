1. The story: the arrows of the Free City

Until now the Free City only had numbers: the guild of a field, where you can add, subtract, multiply and divide. Now caravans arrive, and with them a second kind of citizen: arrows, journeys across the land. Two journeys can be joined end to end (adding arrows). A journey can be stretched by a number: twice as far, half as far, backwards (multiplying an arrow by a number). But two arrows cannot be multiplied with each other, and an arrow cannot be divided by an arrow.

The lawbook of lesson 4 gets a new chapter: the laws that link the numbers and the arrows. There are eight of them. And the first thing the clerk notices is how many different things obey them: not only journeys on a map, but lists of numbers, infinite sequences, functions, polynomials, even sets. Whatever obeys the eight laws, every rule of the chapter applies to it.

In mathematics: the arrows are vectors, the numbers are scalars from a field $K$, and a set of vectors with addition and scalar multiplication obeying the axioms is a vector space. Halbeisen's one-line summary: a vector space is a field acting on an abelian group. This is the central object of the course. Everything that follows (bases, dimension, linear maps, matrices) is about vector spaces.

2. The definition

Definition (vector space; Definition 2.0.1, p. 33). Let $K$ be a field. A vector space over $K$ is a set $V$ with two operations

  • addition $+:V\times V\to V$, $(v_1,v_2)\mapsto v_1+v_2$,
  • scalar multiplication $\cdot:K\times V\to V$, $(a,v)\mapsto a\cdot v=av$,

such that for all $a,b\in K$ and $v,v_1,v_2,v_3\in V$:

axiomformula
V1addition is associative$v_1+(v_2+v_3)=(v_1+v_2)+v_3$
V2zero vector$\exists\,0_V\in V\ \forall v:$ $0_V+v=v$
V3additive inverses$\forall v\ \exists v'$: $v+v'=0_V$
V4addition is commutative$v_1+v_2=v_2+v_1$
V5compatibility$a\cdot(b\cdot v)=(ab)\cdot v$
V6one acts trivially$1\cdot v=v$
V7distributivity I$a(v_1+v_2)=av_1+av_2$
V8distributivity II$(a_1+a_2)v=a_1v+a_2v$

Elements of $V$ are vectors, elements of $K$ are scalars. For $K=\mathbb R$ (or $\mathbb C$) one says real (or complex) vector space.

The lecture's version (Definition 1 of §3) packs V1–V4 into one phrase: $(V,+_V)$ is an abelian group (lesson 1). The remaining four are Halbeisen's (a)–(d): (a) is V6, (b) is V8, (c) is V5, (d) is V7. So: a vector space is an abelian group $(V,+_V,0_V)$, a field $K$, and a map $K\times V\to V$ satisfying V5–V8. When you write a definition in the exam, either version earns the points, as long as every axiom is there with its quantifiers.

Read the axioms with types in mind. Each symbol has a type, and the same sign means different things:

  • In V8, $(a_1+a_2)v=a_1v+a_2v$: the left $+$ is addition in $K$, the right $+$ is addition in $V$.
  • In V5, $a\cdot(b\cdot v)=(ab)\cdot v$: $ab$ is multiplication in $K$; both other dots are scalar multiplication.
  • There are two zeros, $0=0_K\in K$ and $0_V\in V$, and two “minus”: $-a$ in $K$ and $-v$ in $V$.

The lecture writes $+_V$ and $0_V$ at first to keep them apart; the script deliberately doesn't, “to force the reader to keep wondering what exactly means what” (p. 34). Do it yourself in your head, every time. Note what is not there: no product of two vectors, and no division by a vector.

3. Calculation rules from the axioms

Proof A: $0\cdot v=0_V$, $a\cdot0_V=0_V$, $(-1)v=-v$, no “zero divisors” (Lemma 2.0.2 (c)–(g), p. 33; lecture Lemma 3.0 = Serie 2, exercise 10)

Claim. Let $V$ be a vector space over $K$. For all $a\in K$, $v\in V$:

  1. $0\cdot v=0_V$;
  2. $a\cdot0_V=0_V$;
  3. $(-1)\cdot v=-v$;
  4. if $a\cdot v=0_V$, then $a=0$ or $v=0_V$.

The idea. Exactly lesson 4, Proof B, with the two distributive laws V8 and V7 in place of the ring's. The official solution first isolates one small fact, the helper lemma: if $u+u=u$ in $V$, then $u=0_V$ (add $-u$ on both sides). Then (a): $0\cdot v=(0+0)v=0v+0v$. (b): $a0_V=a(0_V+0_V)=a0_V+a0_V$. (c): show that $(-1)v$ adds to $v$ to give $0_V$. (d): if $a\ne0$, multiply by $a^{-1}$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (a), (b): V8 and V7 are the only axioms that mix $+$ and $\cdot$; the proof must produce a sum. Last line: “by the helper lemma, $\dots=0_V$.”
  • (c): “$(-1)v=-v$” means “$(-1)v$ is the additive inverse of $v$”. Last line: “$v+(-1)v=0_V$, so $(-1)v=-v$ by uniqueness.”
  • (d): an “or”: case split. “If $a=0$, done. Otherwise $a\ne0$, and we show $v=0_V$.”
The storyWhat you write

A journey that, joined to itself, is still the same journey, goes nowhere.

Helper. If $u+u=u$, then $0_V=u+(-u)$ $=(u+u)+(-u)$ $=u+(u+(-u))=u+0_V=u$ (V3, V1, V3, V2 with V4).

Stretch any journey by the number nothing. “Nothing” is “nothing plus nothing”, and stretching shares out over a sum of numbers.

(a) $0\cdot v=(0+0)\cdot v=0\cdot v+0\cdot v$ (V8); by the helper with $u=0\cdot v$, $0\cdot v=0_V$.

Stretch the empty journey by any number: same trick, with the other sharing law.

(b) $a\cdot0_V=a\cdot(0_V+0_V)=a\cdot0_V+a\cdot0_V$ (V2, V7); by the helper, $a\cdot0_V=0_V$.

The journey stretched by minus one, joined to the original, goes nowhere. So it is the way back.

(c) $v+(-1)v=1v+(-1)v$ $=(1+(-1))v=0\cdot v=0_V$ (V6, V8, (a)). Inverses in the group $(V,+)$ are unique, so $(-1)v=-v$.

A stretched journey goes nowhere. Either the stretch was by nothing, or the stretch can be undone, and then the journey went nowhere to begin with.

(d) Let $av=0_V$. If $a=0$, done. If $a\ne0$: $v=1v=(a^{-1}a)v$ $=a^{-1}(av)=a^{-1}0_V=0_V$ (V6, V5, (b)). ∎

Moves used: write $0$ as $0+0$ and distribute show it is the inverse, then use uniqueness cancel with an inverse

The proof in one piece

Helper: if $u+u=u$, adding $-u$ gives $u=0_V$. (a) $0v=(0+0)v=0v+0v$, so $0v=0_V$. (b) $a0_V=a(0_V+0_V)=a0_V+a0_V$, so $a0_V=0_V$. (c) $v+(-1)v=(1+(-1))v=0v=0_V$, so $(-1)v=-v$ by uniqueness of inverses. (d) If $av=0_V$ and $a\ne0$, then $v=(a^{-1}a)v=a^{-1}(av)=a^{-1}0_V=0_V$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. State and prove the helper first: “if $u+u=u$, then $u=0_V$” (add $-u$, regroup).
  2. $0\cdot v$: write $0=0+0$ in $K$ and use V8. $a\cdot0_V$: write $0_V=0_V+0_V$ in $V$ and use V7.
  3. $(-1)v=-v$: show $v+(-1)v=0_V$ (V6, V8, step 2) and cite uniqueness of the additive inverse.
  4. $av=0_V\Rightarrow a=0$ or $v=0_V$: case split; if $a\ne0$, multiply by $a^{-1}$ and use V5, V6.
  5. Name the axiom after every equals sign, and index every zero ($0$ or $0_V$).

In short: lesson 4's two tricks (zero is zero plus zero; “minus” is tested by adding to zero), once in $K$ and once in $V$. This is Serie 2, exercise 10, and exactly how a first exam proof about vector spaces is marked: one point per justified step.

The script's Lemma 2.0.2 also lists: the zero vector and the additive inverses are unique (lesson 1, since $(V,+)$ is a group), $-(-v)=v$ (lesson 4, Proof A, Rung 2), and (h) sums of $n$ vectors need no brackets (associativity, by induction). From now on we write $w-v:=w+(-v)$ and use all these rules freely.

4. The standard examples

A definition with eight axioms is only useful if many things satisfy it. The script's motto: always test a new definition on the examples (“your computer compiler”, p. 42). Here is the list. For each, the vector space structure is inherited from $K$: operations are defined entry by entry (componentwise, termwise, pointwise), so each axiom is the corresponding rule of $K$, applied in each entry.

0. The zero space $\{0_V\}$, over every field: $0_V+0_V:=0_V$, $a\cdot0_V:=0_V$.

1. A field over a subfield (lecture). If $K\subseteq L$ is a subfield (a subset that is a field with the same operations), then $L$ is a vector space over $K$, with scalar multiplication the multiplication of $L$. Examples: $\mathbb R$ over $\mathbb Q$, $\mathbb C$ over $\mathbb R$, $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ over $\mathbb Q$, and $K$ over itself.

2. The coordinate space $K^n$ (Example 2.0.3, p. 34): $K^n=\{(a_1,\dots,a_n)\mid a_i\in K\}$ with

$$\begin{aligned}&(a_1,\dots,a_n)+(b_1,\dots,b_n)\\ &\quad:=(a_1+b_1,\dots,a_n+b_n),\\ &a\cdot(a_1,\dots,a_n):=(aa_1,\dots,aa_n).\end{aligned}$$

Zero vector $(0,\dots,0)$. $K^0=\{0\}$ is the zero space, $K^1=K$. Note 2.0.4: one writes the vectors as rows or as columns; later, for matrix products, columns ($K^n_{\text{col}}$) matter.

3. Matrices $M_{m\times n}(K)$ (p. 40–41): rectangular arrays with $m$ rows and $n$ columns, added and scaled entry by entry. As a vector space this is $K^{mn}$ written in a rectangle.

4. Sequences $K^\infty=\{(a_1,a_2,\dots)\mid a_i\in K\}$ (Claim 2.1.10, p. 37; lecture $S(K)$), added and scaled term by term.

5. Functions $K^S=\{f:S\to K\}$ for a set $S\ne\emptyset$ (p. 38): $(f+g)(x):=f(x)+g(x)$, $(af)(x):=a\cdot f(x)$. Zero vector: the zero function. For $S=\{1,\dots,n\}$ this is $K^n$ in disguise, for $S=\mathbb N$ it is $K^\infty$ (Note 2.1.19). Serie 2, exercise 14 is the case $\mathbb R^{\mathbb R}$.

6. Polynomials $K[x]$ (p. 40; Serie 2, exercise 12 for $\mathbb Q[X]$): $a\cdot(a_0+a_1x+\dots+a_nx^n)$ $:=aa_0+aa_1x+\dots+aa_nx^n$.

Proof B: $K^n$ is a vector space (Example 2.0.3, “verify some/all!”)

Claim. With componentwise operations, $K^n$ is a vector space over $K$.

The idea. Two $n$-tuples are equal exactly when they agree in each component. So each axiom is a statement about $n$ components, and in each component it is a field axiom.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $v=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$, $w$, $u\in K^n$ and $a,b\in K$. Two tuples are equal if and only if they agree in every component.”
  • What to show: the operations land in $K^n$, and V1–V8 hold. V2 and V3 are “there exists” axioms: name the zero vector and the negative.
  • Last line: “So all eight axioms hold, and $K^n$ is a vector space over $K$.”
  • The gap: for each axiom, compare the $i$-th components of both sides and cite the field rule that makes them equal.
The storyWhat you write

A caravan list has $n$ entries. Two lists are the same if every entry is the same.

Let $v=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$, $w=(w_1,\dots,w_n)$, $u\in K^n$ and $a,b\in K$. Tuples are equal iff all components are equal.

Each law of the arrows, read entry by entry, is a law of numbers.

V8: the $i$-th component of $(a+b)v$ is $(a+b)v_i=av_i+bv_i$ (distributivity in $K$), which is the $i$-th component of $av+bv$. V5: $a(bv_i)=(ab)v_i$. V6: $1\cdot v_i=v_i$. V7: $a(v_i+w_i)=av_i+aw_i$. V1, V4: associativity and commutativity of $+$ in $K$.

The empty list and the reversed list complete the laws.

V2: $0_V:=(0,\dots,0)$, since $0+v_i=v_i$. V3: $-v:=(-v_1,\dots,-v_n)$, since $v_i+(-v_i)=0$. So $K^n$ is a vector space. ∎

Moves used: check the axioms entry by entry name a witness

The proof in one piece

Let $v,w,u\in K^n$ and $a,b\in K$; tuples are equal when all components agree. The operations are maps $K^n\times K^n\to K^n$ and $K\times K^n\to K^n$, since sums and products of field elements lie in $K$. For each $i$: $(v_i+w_i)+u_i=v_i+(w_i+u_i)$ and $v_i+w_i=w_i+v_i$ (V1, V4); $0+v_i=v_i$, so $0_V:=(0,\dots,0)$ satisfies V2; $v_i+(-v_i)=0$, so $-v:=(-v_1,\dots,-v_n)$ satisfies V3; $a(bv_i)=(ab)v_i$ (V5); $1\cdot v_i=v_i$ (V6); $a(v_i+w_i)=av_i+aw_i$ (V7); $(a+b)v_i=av_i+bv_i$ (V8). Each equality is a field axiom of $K$, and it holds in every component, so V1–V8 hold in $K^n$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Write V5 for $K^n$ in one line, with the field rule you use.
    Answer

    The $i$-th component of $a(bv)$ is $a(bv_i)$, that of $(ab)v$ is $(ab)v_i$, and $a(bv_i)=(ab)v_i$ by associativity of multiplication in $K$. So $a(bv)=(ab)v$.

  2. Rung 2. Do the same proof for the function space $K^S$: which words change, and which extra check appears?
    Answer

    “Component $i$” becomes “value at $x\in S$”: two functions are equal when they agree at every $x$. For example V8: $\big((a+b)f\big)(x)=(a+b)f(x)$ $=af(x)+bf(x)$ $=(af+bf)(x)$. The zero vector is the zero function, $-f$ is $x\mapsto-f(x)$. The extra check is only that $f+g$ and $af$ are again functions $S\to K$, which they are by definition.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof B from the frame alone, with all eight axioms named.
How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Say how equality works in the set (componentwise, termwise, pointwise).
  2. Check that the operations stay in the set.
  3. Name $0_V$ and $-v$ explicitly (V2, V3).
  4. For the six “for all” axioms, write one line each: “in each component it is [field rule]”. Write out at least V5 and V8 in full.

In short: equality entry by entry, then each axiom is a field rule in each entry. If the set sits inside a known vector space, the subspace test (Proof D) is shorter: use it.

The same proof, with “component $i$” replaced by “term $i$”, “entry $(i,j)$”, “value at $x$” or “coefficient of $x^i$”, proves examples 3–6. For sequences and functions you also check that the operations stay inside the set (they obviously do: the sum of two sequences is a sequence). For polynomials, check that $a\cdot p$ is again a polynomial: only finitely many coefficients are nonzero, since $a\cdot0=0$.

Worked example 1: how many vectors does $\mathbb F_p^n$ have? Compute in $\mathbb F_{11}^{11}$ (Serie 2, exercise 13)

  1. (a) $|\mathbb F_p^n|$?
    Answer

    Each of the $n$ components independently takes one of the $p$ values $\bar0,\dots,\overline{p-1}$, so $|\mathbb F_p^n|=p\cdot p\cdots p=p^n$. A vector space need not be infinite: $\mathbb F_2^3$ has $8$ vectors.

  2. (b) $\bar7\cdot(\bar0,\bar1,\bar2,\dots,\overline{10})$ in $\mathbb F_{11}^{11}$?
    Answer

    Componentwise, $7k$ modulo $11$ for $k=0,\dots,10$: $(\bar0,\bar7,\bar3,\overline{10},\bar6,\bar2,\bar9,\bar5,\bar1,\bar8,\bar4)$. Every class appears exactly once: multiplication by $\bar7\ne\bar0$ is injective, exactly the map $m_a$ of lesson 4.

Worked example 2: the power set as a vector space over $\mathbb F_2$ (Example 2.1.23, p. 41; Quiz 2, item 4)

Let $X$ be a set. Make $\mathcal P(X)$ a vector space over $\mathbb F_2=\{0,1\}$ with addition the symmetric difference $A\triangle B$.

  1. What must the zero vector be, and how must scalar multiplication be defined?
    Answer

    $A\triangle\emptyset=A$, so $0_V=\emptyset$. There is no choice for scalar multiplication: V6 forces $1\cdot A=A$, and Proof A (a) forces $0\cdot A=0_V=\emptyset$.

  2. Check V8 for $a=b=1$, the only case where the field's addition is not obvious.
    Answer

    In $\mathbb F_2$, $1+1=0$, so the left side is $0\cdot A=\emptyset$. The right side is $A\triangle A=\emptyset$. Equal. (In lesson 1 you proved that $(\mathcal P(X),\triangle)$ is an abelian group in which every element is its own inverse; that is exactly what $1+1=0$ needs.) The other cases: $a=0$ or $b=0$ make both sides equal to $1\cdot A$ or $\emptyset$; V5–V7 likewise by cases. So the answer to Quiz 2, item 4 is yes.

5. Non-examples: when no scalar multiplication works

Quiz 2 asks “is $V$ a vector space over $K$?” for pairs where only the group and the field are given. The convention (official solution): if nothing else is said, scalar multiplication is the obvious one. But the solution goes further and shows that for the “no” cases no scalar multiplication works. The tool is Proof A (d).

Proof C: $(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is not a vector space over $\mathbb Q$, whatever the scalar multiplication (Quiz 2, item 3)

Claim. There is no map $\mathbb Q\times(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\})\to\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$ that makes the abelian group $V=(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ a vector space over $\mathbb Q$.

The idea. Careful with types: here the “vector addition” $+_V$ is the multiplication of real numbers, and the zero vector is $0_V=1$. The element $-1$ satisfies $(-1)+_V(-1)=(-1)(-1)=1=0_V$: twice the vector $-1$ is the zero vector. In a vector space over $\mathbb Q$, $2\cdot x=x+_Vx$, and “$2\cdot x=0_V$ with $2\ne0$” forces $x=0_V$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • “Whatever the scalar multiplication”: a statement about all maps $\ast$, so the proof is by contradiction. First line: “Assume some $\ast:\mathbb Q\times V\to V$ makes $V$ a vector space over $\mathbb Q$.”
  • Translate the types: $x+_Vy:=xy$, $0_V=1$ (since $1\cdot x=x$), and the negative of $x$ is $x^{-1}$.
  • Last line: “… so $-1=0_V=1$, a contradiction.”
  • The gap: find a vector $x\ne0_V$ and a scalar $\lambda\ne0$ with $\lambda\ast x=0_V$, using only the axioms (not a formula for $\ast$).
The storyWhat you write

Suppose some clerk found a way to stretch these “arrows” by rational numbers, obeying all laws.

Assume, to the contrary, that some $\ast:\mathbb Q\times V\to V$ satisfies V5–V8. Here $x+_Vy=xy$ and $0_V=1$.

Stretching by $2$ means joining the arrow to itself.

By V8 and V6, for every $x$: $2\ast x=(1+1)\ast x$ $=1\ast x+_V1\ast x=x+_Vx$.

The arrow $-1$, joined to itself, is the empty arrow.

For $x=-1$: $2\ast(-1)=(-1)\cdot(-1)=1=0_V$.

A stretch by a nonzero number that gives nothing means the arrow was nothing. But $-1$ is not the empty arrow.

As $2\ne0$ in $\mathbb Q$, Proof A (d) gives $-1=0_V=1$, a contradiction. So no such $\ast$ exists. ∎

The proof in one piece

6. Subspaces

Most vector spaces in practice live inside a bigger one: the solutions of an equation inside $K^n$, the Fibonacci sequences inside all sequences, the continuous functions inside all functions. The script defines this right after the axioms.

Definition (subspace; Definition 2.0.5, p. 34). Let $V$ be a vector space over $K$. A subset $W\subseteq V$ is a subspace of $V$ if

  • (LSS1) $W\ne\emptyset$;
  • (LSS2) for all $w_1,w_2\in W$: $w_1+w_2\in W$;
  • (LSS3) for all $a\in K$, $w\in W$: $aw\in W$.

Exercise 2.0.6 (p. 35). (a) (LSS1) may be replaced by (LSS1′): $0_V\in W$. (b) (LSS2) and (LSS3) together are equivalent to: $\forall a_1,a_2\in K\ \forall w_1,w_2\in W:$ $a_1w_1+a_2w_2\in W$.

Proof D: a subspace is a vector space; and the two tests (Lemma 2.0.7 and Exercise 2.0.6, p. 35)

Claim. (i) If $W$ satisfies (LSS1)–(LSS3), then $0_V\in W$, and $W$ with the operations of $V$ is a vector space over $K$. (ii) (LSS1′)+(LSS2)+(LSS3) $\iff$ (LSS1)+(LSS2)+(LSS3).

The idea. V1, V4–V8 are “for all” statements; they hold in $V$, so in particular for the elements of $W$. Only the “there exists” axioms V2 and V3 need care: the zero vector and the negatives must lie in $W$. They do, by Proof A: $0_V=0\cdot w$ and $-w=(-1)\cdot w$, and $W$ is closed under scalar multiplication.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (i), first line: “Let $W$ satisfy (LSS1)–(LSS3). By (LSS2) and (LSS3), addition and scalar multiplication restrict to $W$.” Last line: “So $W$ is a vector space over $K$.”
  • Sort the axioms: the “for all” axioms (V1, V4–V8) are inherited; the “there exists” axioms (V2, V3) need a witness inside $W$.
  • (ii): two implications between the first conditions; (LSS2), (LSS3) are the same on both sides.
  • The gap: produce $0_V$ and $-w$ from elements of $W$ using only scalar multiplication.
The storyWhat you write

A district of arrows: not empty, closed under joining and under stretching.

(i) By (LSS2), (LSS3), $+$ and $\cdot$ restrict to maps $W\times W\to W$ and $K\times W\to W$.

The two versions of the first condition: one implies the other.

(ii) (LSS1′) $\Rightarrow$ (LSS1): $0_V\in W$, so $W\ne\emptyset$. (LSS1)+(LSS3) $\Rightarrow$ (LSS1′): shown in (i). ∎

Moves used: inherit from the bigger structure the subspace test: zero, sums, multiples

The proof in one piece

(i) By (LSS2) and (LSS3), $+$ and $\cdot$ restrict to maps $W\times W\to W$ and $K\times W\to W$. The axioms V1, V4, V5, V6, V7, V8 hold for all vectors of $V$, so in particular for all vectors of $W$. By (LSS1) there is some $u\in W$; then $0_V=0\cdot u\in W$ by the rule $0\cdot v=0_V$ and (LSS3), so V2 holds in $W$. For $w\in W$, $-w=(-1)w\in W$ by the rule $(-1)v=-v$ and (LSS3), so V3 holds in $W$. Hence $W$ is a vector space over $K$, with $0_V\in W$. (ii) If $0_V\in W$, then $W\ne\emptyset$; conversely, (i) shows that (LSS1) and (LSS3) give $0_V\in W$. ∎

  1. Rung 1. Why does V4 not need to be checked for $W$, but V2 does?
    Answer

    V4 says “for all $v_1,v_2$: $v_1+v_2=v_2+v_1$”; it holds for all vectors of $V$, so for those of $W$. V2 says “there is a zero vector in the space”; the zero of $V$ must be shown to lie in $W$, otherwise $W$ has no zero vector at all.

  2. Rung 2. Prove Exercise 2.0.6 (b): (LSS2) and (LSS3) together $\iff$ $a_1w_1+a_2w_2\in W$ for all $a_1,a_2\in K$, $w_1,w_2\in W$.

    “$\Rightarrow$”: $a_1w_1\in W$ and $a_2w_2\in W$ by (LSS3), so their sum lies in $W$ by (LSS2). “$\Leftarrow$”: $a_1=a_2=1$ gives $w_1+w_2\in W$ (with V6); $a_2=0$ gives $a_1w_1+0\cdot w_2=a_1w_1+0_V=a_1w_1\in W$.

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. For “show that $W$ is a vector space” when $W$ lies in a known vector space $V$: do not check eight axioms; say “we show $W$ is a subspace of $V$”.
  2. Check $0_V\in W$ (one line, with the condition that defines $W$).
  3. Take $w_1,w_2\in W$, show $w_1+w_2$ satisfies the condition.
  4. Take $a\in K$, $w\in W$, show $aw$ satisfies the condition.
  5. Close with “so $W$ is a subspace, hence a vector space with the operations of $V$”.

In short: zero in, closed under sums, closed under multiples. To refute: one witness.

The subspace test you will use. To show $W\subseteq V$ is a subspace: (1) $0_V\in W$; (2) $w_1,w_2\in W\Rightarrow w_1+w_2\in W$; (3) $a\in K$, $w\in W\Rightarrow aw\in W$. To show it is not: give one witness: $0_V\notin W$, or two elements whose sum leaves $W$, or one element and one scalar whose product leaves $W$. Checking $0_V\in W$ first is the fastest filter: a set without $0_V$ is never a subspace.

Examples from the script.

  • $\{0_V\}$ and $V$ are subspaces of $V$ (Example 2.1.1). So every vector space has a subspace, but the zero space has only one: “every vector space has two different subspaces” is false (the zero space is the exception).
  • $U_b=\{x\in K^3\mid$ $x_1-x_2+x_3=b\}$ is a subspace iff $b=0$ (Example 2.1.3, p. 36). For $b\ne0$: the sum of two elements has $z_1-z_2+z_3=b+b=2b$, and $2b\ne b$ since $b\ne0$. (Even quicker: $0_V\notin U_b$.)
  • More generally, the solutions of $Ax=0$ form a subspace of $K^n$, and of $Ax=b$ with $b\ne0$ do not (Example 2.1.7, p. 37). This is the most important example: later in the course you will see that every subspace of $K^n$ is of this form.
  • Fibonacci sequences $\{(a_i)\mid a_n=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}\ \forall n\ge3\}$ form a subspace of $K^\infty$ (Example 2.1.12, p. 38), and so does every linear recursion $a_n=\alpha a_{n-1}+\beta a_{n-2}$. Serie 2, exercise 11.
  • $\{f\in K^S\mid f(s_0)=0\}$ is a subspace of $K^S$ (Example 2.1.17, p. 39); so is $\{f:[0,1]\to\mathbb R\mid f\text{ continuous}\}$ (Example 2.1.18).
  • $K[x]_h=\{p\in K[x]\mid\deg p\le h\}$ is a subspace (Example 2.1.21, p. 40). Is $\{p\mid\deg p=h\}$? Worked example 5.

Worked example 3: which subset is not a subspace of $\mathbb R^3$? (HS21, question VI)

(a) $\{x\mid x_1=2x_2=3x_3\}$. (b) $\{x\mid x_1x_2=0\}$. (c) $\{x\mid x_1^2+x_2^2=0\}$. (d) $\{(0,0,x^2-y^2)\mid x,y\in\mathbb R\}$.

    Answer

    (a) $x_1-2x_2=0$ and $2x_2-3x_3=0$: solutions of $Ax=0$, a line. (c) Over $\mathbb R$, $x_1^2+x_2^2=0\iff x_1=x_2=0$: the $x_3$-axis. (d) $x^2-y^2$ takes every real value ($x^2-0$ for $t\ge0$, $0-y^2$ for $t\le0$): the $x_3$-axis again. All three are subspaces.

Worked example 4: “if $W_1,W_2$ are subspaces of $V$, then $W_1\cap W_2$ and $W_1\cup W_2$ are subspaces.” True or false? (FS17, v; FS18, (v))

  1. The intersection.
    Answer

    A subspace: $0_V\in W_1$ and $0_V\in W_2$; if $w,w'\in W_1\cap W_2$, then $w+w'$ lies in $W_1$ (closed) and in $W_2$ (closed); same for $aw$. (Lesson 6, Proof A.)

    Answer

    In general not: in $\mathbb R^2$, the two axes are subspaces, but their union is not (the explorer above, $xy=0$). So the statement is false. (The union is a subspace only if $W_1\subseteq W_2$ or $W_2\subseteq W_1$: Exercise 6.)

Worked example 5: polynomials of degree exactly $h$; “$\deg f=\deg g=n\Rightarrow\deg(f+g)=n$” (Example 2.1.21; HS17, (vii))

  1. Is $\{p\in K[x]\mid\deg p=2\}$ a subspace of $K[x]$?
    Answer

    No: the zero polynomial has degree $-\infty$ (or “no degree”), not $2$, so $0_V$ is missing. Also $x^2+(-x^2+1)=1$ has degree $0$. Whereas $\{\deg p\le2\}$ is a subspace: the sum and scalar multiples of polynomials of degree $\le2$ have degree $\le2$.

  2. And the HS17 statement?
    Answer

    False, with the same witness: $f=x^2$, $g=-x^2+1$, $\deg(f+g)=0$. Leading terms can cancel. True is only $\deg(f+g)\le\max(\deg f,\deg g)$.

Why it's cool

The eight axioms never say what a vector is. That is the point: a theorem proved once from the axioms holds at the same time for arrows in space, for lists of numbers, for sound signals (functions of time), for images (matrices of pixel values), for polynomials and for error-correcting codes over $\mathbb F_2$. When your phone compresses a photo, it treats the picture as a vector in a space of dimension several million and changes the basis (lesson 13). When Netflix recommends a film, your taste is a vector. One definition, and every later lesson pays out in all these worlds at once.

For wonder: the positive reals are a vector space, with multiplication as addition

Proof C showed that $(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ cannot be a vector space, and the culprit was the single element $-1$. Remove the negatives: on $\mathbb R_{>0}$ define “addition” $x\oplus y:=xy$ and “scalar multiplication” $\lambda\odot x:=x^\lambda$ for $\lambda\in\mathbb R$. All axioms hold, since $(xy)^\lambda=x^\lambda y^\lambda$, $x^{\lambda+\mu}=x^\lambda x^\mu$, $x^{\lambda\mu}=(x^\mu)^\lambda$, $x^1=x$. The zero vector is the number $1$. In fact $\log$ translates this strange space into the ordinary $\mathbb R$: $\log(xy)=\log x+\log y$ and $\log(x^\lambda)=\lambda\log x$. Lesson 12 calls such a translation an isomorphism. A slide rule is a machine built on it.

Worked example 6: “the matrices $A$ with $A^2=0$ form a subspace of $M_{2\times2}(\mathbb R)$.” True or false? (mock exam HS20)

Product rule: $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}^2$ $=\begin{pmatrix}a^2+bc&ab+bd\\ca+dc&cb+d^2\end{pmatrix}$.

  1. Run the subspace test in order: is $0$ in the set? Is it closed under scaling?
    Answer

    $0^2=0$, so the zero matrix is in. If $A^2=0$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb R$, then $(\lambda A)^2=\lambda^2A^2=0$. Both checks pass, so the decision falls on addition.

  2. Find two matrices in the set whose sum is not.
    Answer

    $E=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $F=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ satisfy $E^2=F^2=0$. But $E+F=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has square $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\ne0$. False. (The set contains $0$ and is closed under scaling; only addition fails. Worked example 7: subspace or not? (a) $\{(a_i)\in\mathbb R^\infty\mid a_1=0\}$; (b) $\{(a_i)\mid a_i\to0\}$; (c) $\{(a_i)\mid a_{n+1}=a_n+1\ \forall n\}$; (d) $\{f\in\mathbb R^{\mathbb R}\mid f(1)=f(2)\}$.

  1. (a) and (c): which one fails the fastest filter?
    Answer

    (c): the zero sequence would need $0=0+1$, so $0_V\notin W$. Not a subspace. (a) passes: the zero sequence has $a_1=0$; if $a_1=0$ and $b_1=0$, then $a_1+b_1=0$ and $\lambda a_1=0$. A subspace.

  2. (b): which known facts give the three checks?
    Answer

    The limit rules from Analysis: the zero sequence tends to $0$; if $a_i\to0$ and $b_i\to0$, then $a_i+b_i\to0+0=0$; and $\lambda a_i\to\lambda\cdot0=0$. A subspace (the space $c_0$ of null sequences).

  3. (d): is the condition linear?
    Answer

    Yes: $f(1)=f(2)$ is $f(1)-f(2)=0$. The zero function satisfies it; if $f(1)=f(2)$ and $g(1)=g(2)$, then $(f+g)(1)=f(1)+g(1)=f(2)+g(2)=(f+g)(2)$, and $(\lambda f)(1)=\lambda f(2)=(\lambda f)(2)$. A subspace, by the same test as the functions vanishing at a point.

Worked example 8: periodic functions (exam style: “show that … is a vector space”)

Let $P=\{f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\mid f(x+1)=f(x)\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb R\}$, the functions with period $1$, and $Q=\{f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\mid f(x+1)=f(x)+1\text{ for all }x\}$. Which one is a vector space with the pointwise operations?

  1. Which bigger vector space do both live in, and what does that save?
    Answer

    Both are subsets of $\mathbb R^{\mathbb R}$, the space of all functions $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ (section 4, example 5). So instead of eight axioms it suffices to run the subspace test (Proof D).

  2. Run the test for $P$.
    Answer

    Zero: the zero function satisfies $0=0$. Sums: if $f,g\in P$, then $(f+g)(x+1)=f(x+1)+g(x+1)$ $=f(x)+g(x)=(f+g)(x)$. Multiples: $(\lambda f)(x+1)=\lambda f(x+1)=\lambda f(x)=(\lambda f)(x)$. So $P$ is a subspace of $\mathbb R^{\mathbb R}$, hence a vector space. Examples in $P$: $\sin(2\pi x)$, $\cos(2\pi x)$, every constant.

  3. And $Q$? Give the shortest refutation.
    Answer

    The zero function would need $0=0+1$: $0_V\notin Q$. Not a subspace. (Also: $f(x)=x$ lies in $Q$, but $2f$ satisfies $2(x+1)=2x+2\ne2x+1$.) Note $Q=x+P$: every $f\in Q$ is $x$ plus a periodic function. That is a parallel copy of $P$, an affine subspace (lesson 9).

Exercise 1 (FS19, problem 2 (a), 3 points)

Let $V$ be the vector space of all real sequences $(x_n)_{n\ge0}$. Show that the sequences that take only finitely many different values form a subspace $W$ of $V$.

Hint

If $(x_n)$ takes values in a finite set $A$ and $(y_n)$ in a finite set $B$, in which set does $(x_n+y_n)$ take its values? How big is it?

Solution

Frame: the subspace test. Moves: unfold the definition; three checks.

Write $\operatorname{val}(x):=\{x_n\mid n\ge0\}$; then $W=\{x\in V\mid\operatorname{val}(x)\text{ finite}\}$.

(0) The zero sequence has $\operatorname{val}=\{0\}$, which is finite, so $0_V\in W$.

(+) Let $x,y\in W$. For every $n$, $x_n+y_n$ lies in $\{a+b\mid a\in\operatorname{val}(x),$ $b\in\operatorname{val}(y)\}$, a set with at most $|\operatorname{val}(x)|\cdot|\operatorname{val}(y)|$ elements. So $\operatorname{val}(x+y)$ is finite, and $x+y\in W$.

(·) Let $\lambda\in\mathbb R$, $x\in W$. Then $\operatorname{val}(\lambda x)=\{\lambda a\mid a\in\operatorname{val}(x)\}$ has at most $|\operatorname{val}(x)|$ elements, so $\lambda x\in W$.

Hence $W$ is a subspace of $V$.

Exercise 3 (Exercise 2.1.13, p. 38: “the answer depends on something”)

Is $W=\{(a_i)\in K^\infty\mid a_n=a_{n-1}^2\ \forall n\ge2\}$ a subspace of $K^\infty$?

Hint

Which constant sequences lie in $W$? Scale one of them by $\lambda$ and ask for which $\lambda$ it stays in $W$. The field matters.

Solution

Frame: test the simplest element of $W$ against scaling. Moves: one counterexample is enough.

The constant sequence $(1,1,1,\dots)$ lies in $W$ in every field, since $1=1^2$. For $\lambda\in K$, $\lambda\cdot(1,1,\dots)=(\lambda,\lambda,\dots)$ lies in $W$ iff $\lambda=\lambda^2$, i.e. $\lambda(\lambda-1)=0$, i.e. $\lambda\in\{0,1\}$ (no zero divisors).

Frame: obvious scalar multiplication first; for “no”, a structural obstruction. Moves: “$\lambda v=0_V$ with $\lambda\ne0$ forces $v=0_V$”; inherit from the bigger structure.

(1) Yes: $\mathbb Q$ is a subfield of $\mathbb R$ (section 4, example 1). (2) No: $\sqrt2\cdot1\notin\mathbb Q$, and no other scalar multiplication works either (Proof C, Rung 2: it would give an injective map $\mathbb R\to\mathbb Q$). (3) No: $-1$ is its own negative ($(-1)(-1)=1=0_V$), which no vector space over $\mathbb Q$ allows (Proof C). (4) Yes: Worked example 2. The quiz gives the point only if all four are right: “1 yes, 2 no, 3 no, 4 yes”.

Exercise 5 (calculation rules)

Let $V$ be a vector space over $K$, $a,b\in K$, $v,w\in V$. Prove: (a) $a(v-w)=av-aw$; (b) if $av=bv$ and $v\ne0_V$, then $a=b$; (c) if $av=aw$ and $a\ne0$, then $v=w$.

Hint

Bring everything to one side and write it as one scalar times one vector. Then use: $\lambda v=0_V$ forces $\lambda=0$ or $v=0_V$.

Solution

Frame: move everything to one side and use “$\lambda v=0_V\Rightarrow\lambda=0$ or $v=0_V$”. Moves: show it is the inverse, then use uniqueness.

(a) $a(v-w)=a(v+(-1)w)$ $=av+a((-1)w)$ $=av+(-a)w$ $=av+(-1)(aw)=av-aw$ (V7, V5 twice, and $(-1)u=-u$).

(b) $0_V=av-bv=av+(-b)v=(a-b)v$ (the rule $(-1)u=-u$ with V5, then V8). As $v\ne0_V$ and $\lambda v=0_V$ forces $\lambda=0$ or $v=0_V$, we get $a-b=0$, so $a=b$.

(c) $0_V=av-aw=a(v-w)$ by (a). As $a\ne0$, the same rule gives $v-w=0_V$, so $v=w$.

Exercise 6 (unions of subspaces)

Let $W_1,W_2$ be subspaces of $V$. Show: $W_1\cup W_2$ is a subspace if and only if $W_1\subseteq W_2$ or $W_2\subseteq W_1$.

Hint

“$\Rightarrow$” by contraposition: take $w_1\in W_1\setminus W_2$ and $w_2\in W_2\setminus W_1$. Where can $w_1+w_2$ lie?

Solution

Frame: contrapositive for “$\Rightarrow$”. Moves: prove the contrapositive; assume the opposite.

“$\Leftarrow$”: then $W_1\cup W_2$ is $W_2$ or $W_1$, a subspace. “$\Rightarrow$”: assume neither inclusion holds; choose $w_1\in W_1\setminus W_2$ and $w_2\in W_2\setminus W_1$. If $w_1+w_2\in W_1$, then $w_2=(w_1+w_2)-w_1\in W_1$, a contradiction; if $w_1+w_2\in W_2$, then $w_1=(w_1+w_2)-w_2\in W_2$, a contradiction. So $w_1+w_2\notin W_1\cup W_2$, and the union is not closed under addition.

Exercise 7 (true or false, old exam style)

(a) Every vector space has at least two elements. (b) Every field has at least two elements. (c) $\{x\in\mathbb R^3\mid x_1+x_2+x_3=1\}$ is a subspace. (d) $\mathbb C$ is a vector space over $\mathbb R$. (e) $\mathbb R$ is a vector space over $\mathbb C$ with the multiplication of $\mathbb C$. (f) If $W\subseteq V$ satisfies $0_V\in W$ and is closed under addition, then $W$ is a subspace.

Hint

For subsets, test $0_V$ first. For “a vector space over”, check that scalar multiplication stays in the set.

Solution

Frame: a “true” needs a reason that works in every case; a “false” needs one concrete counterexample.

  1. (a) False. The zero space $V=\{0\}$ over any field, with $0+0=0$ and $a\cdot0=0$, satisfies every axiom: each axiom is an equation between elements of $V$, and $V$ has only one element, so both sides are always $0$ (the zero space).
  2. (b) True. The field axioms demand $0\ne1$, so $0$ and $1$ are two different elements.
  3. (c) False. Every subspace contains the zero vector, but $0+0+0=0\ne1$, so $(0,0,0)$ is not in the set. (Geometrically: a plane that misses the origin.)
  4. (d) True. The axioms of a vector space over $\mathbb R$ are, for $a,b\in\mathbb R$ and $u,v\in\mathbb C$, field laws of $\mathbb C$: associativity and distributivity of multiplication, $1\cdot v=v$. They hold because they hold for all complex numbers, in particular when $a,b$ are real.
  5. (e) False. Scalar multiplication must be a map $K\times V\to V$. Here $i\in\mathbb C$ and $1\in\mathbb R$, but $i\cdot1=i\notin\mathbb R$: the product leaves $V$, so there is no scalar multiplication at all.
  6. (f) False. $W=\mathbb Z^2\subseteq\mathbb R^2$ contains $(0,0)$ and the sum of two integer vectors is an integer vector, but $\tfrac12\cdot(1,0)=(\tfrac12,0)\notin W$: closure under scalars fails. (Also $\mathbb N_0\subseteq\mathbb R$, where even $-1\cdot1$ leaves the set.) Closure under scalar multiplication is a separate condition and cannot be dropped.

Exercise 8 (even and odd functions: a first look at splitting a space)

In $V=\mathbb R^{\mathbb R}$ (all functions $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, pointwise operations) let $E=\{f\mid f(-x)=f(x)\ \forall x\}$ (even functions) and $O=\{f\mid f(-x)=-f(x)\ \forall x\}$ (odd functions). (a) Show that $E$ and $O$ are subspaces. (b) Show $E\cap O=\{0\}$. (c) Show that every $f\in V$ can be written as $f=g+h$ with $g\in E$, $h\in O$. (d) Is $\{f\mid f(0)=1\}$ a subspace?

Hint

(a) Use the one-line test $a_1f_1+a_2f_2\in W$ and evaluate at $-x$. (c) Try $g(x)=\tfrac12\big(f(x)+f(-x)\big)$ and guess $h$ from $f=g+h$.

Solution

Frame: for (a), first line: $f_1,f_2\in E$, $a_1,a_2\in\mathbb R$; last line: $(a_1f_1+a_2f_2)(-x)=(a_1f_1+a_2f_2)(x)$ for all $x$. For (c), we must produce $g$ and $h$ and check three things: $g$ even, $h$ odd, $g+h=f$.

  1. (a) $E$ contains the zero function, since $0(-x)=0=0(x)$. For $f_1,f_2\in E$ and $a_1,a_2\in\mathbb R$ and every $x$: $(a_1f_1+a_2f_2)(-x)=a_1f_1(-x)+a_2f_2(-x)$ (definition of the pointwise operations) $=a_1f_1(x)+a_2f_2(x)$ (both $f_i$ are even) $=(a_1f_1+a_2f_2)(x)$. So $E$ is non-empty and closed under linear combinations of two elements, hence a subspace. For $O$ the same computation gives $a_1(-f_1(x))+a_2(-f_2(x))=-(a_1f_1+a_2f_2)(x)$, and the zero function is odd since $0=-0$.
  2. (b) If $f$ is even and odd, then for every $x$: $f(x)=f(-x)=-f(x)$, so $2f(x)=0$ and, dividing by $2$ in $\mathbb R$, $f(x)=0$. So $f$ is the zero function. (Over $\mathbb F_2$ this step would fail, because $2=0$ there.)
  3. (c) Put $g(x)=\tfrac12\big(f(x)+f(-x)\big)$ and $h(x)=\tfrac12\big(f(x)-f(-x)\big)$. Then $g(-x)=\tfrac12\big(f(-x)+f(x)\big)=g(x)$, so $g\in E$; $h(-x)=\tfrac12\big(f(-x)-f(x)\big)=-h(x)$, so $h\in O$; and $g(x)+h(x)=\tfrac12\cdot2f(x)=f(x)$. Example: $e^x=\cosh x+\sinh x$.
  4. (d) No. The zero function has value $0\ne1$ at $0$, so it is not in the set, and every subspace contains the zero vector.

Together, (b) and (c) say that every function splits into an even and an odd part in exactly one way (if $g+h=g'+h'$, then $g-g'=h'-h$ lies in $E\cap O=\{0\}$). This is the first example of a direct sum $V=E\oplus O$, which comes back in lesson 8.
