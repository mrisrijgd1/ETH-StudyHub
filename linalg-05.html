StudyHub · Linear Algebra I · Lesson 5

The realm of arrows: vector spaces

A vector space is an abelian group that a field can act on. You'll learn the eight axioms, prove the arithmetic rules that follow from them, and meet the standard examples: $K^n$, functions, sequences, polynomials, matrices, and even a power set. Serie 2 and Quiz 2 are exactly this lesson.

Script EN 2023, start of Chapter 2 (pp. 33–34: Definition 2.0.1, Lemma 2.0.2, Example 2.0.3, Note 2.0.4) and the example zoo of §2.1 (pp. 37–41: sequence spaces, Example 2.1.12, function spaces $K^S$, polynomial spaces, matrices, Example 2.1.23).

About 75 minutes.
You're done when

Script
Main: Script EN 2023 (pages as above). Lecture: Halbeisen's notes Part II, §3 “Definitionen und Beispiele” (pp. 14–17), where the rules of Lemma 2.0.2 are Lemma 3.0 and the axioms are labelled differently (see section 1).
Why it matters for the exam

Every later topic (span, bases, dimension, linear maps) lives inside a vector space, and “show that $V$ is a $K$-vector space” is a classic first exam part: HS18, Aufgabe 2(a) gives 6 points for showing that all maps $M\to K$ form a $K$-vector space. It is Exercise 2 below. Quiz 2 (four pairs $V,K$: vector space or not?) is Exercise 1, with the official reasoning.

Recap of lesson 4 (5 min)

In a ring, $0\cdot a=0$, because $0\cdot a=(0+0)\cdot a=0\cdot a+0\cdot a$, and then you cancel. Check: which axiom lets you “cancel” $0\cdot a$ from $0\cdot a=0\cdot a+0\cdot a$?

Answer

Add the additive inverse $-(0\cdot a)$ on both sides and use associativity of $+$: $0=0\cdot a-0\cdot a=(0\cdot a+0\cdot a)-0\cdot a=0\cdot a+(0\cdot a-0\cdot a)=0\cdot a$. Section 2 does the same thing for vectors.

The story: the realm of arrows

Beyond Numeria lies the realm of arrows. Arrows can be laid end to end (that is their guild ritual, an abelian group), and the wizards of a field can stretch any arrow by a number. The realm has a charter of eight rules saying how stretching and laying end to end get along. Arrows need not look like arrows: in this realm a function, a sequence, a polynomial or even a set of numbers can be an arrow, as long as the charter holds.

1. The axioms

Definition 2.0.1 (vector space, p. 33). A vector space over a field $K$ is a set $V$ with two operations

$+:V\times V\to V$, $(v_1,v_2)\mapsto v_1+v_2$ and $\cdot:K\times V\to V$, $(a,v)\mapsto a\cdot v=av$,

such that for all $a,b\in K$ and $v,v_1,v_2,v_3\in V$:

ScriptAxiomLecture
V1$v_1+(v_2+v_3)=(v_1+v_2)+v_3$$(V,+)$ abelian group
V2there is $0=0_V\in V$ with $0+v=v$
V3for every $v$ there is $v'$ with $v+v'=0$
V4$v_1+v_2=v_2+v_1$
V5$a\cdot(b\cdot v)=(a\cdot b)\cdot v$(c)
V6$1\cdot v=v$(a)
V7$a\cdot(v_1+v_2)=a\cdot v_1+a\cdot v_2$(d)
V8$(a_1+a_2)\cdot v=a_1\cdot v+a_2\cdot v$(b)

Elements of $V$ are vectors, elements of $K$ are scalars. For $K=\mathbb R$ (or $\mathbb C$) we say real (or complex) vector space.

Two additions, two multiplications. In V8, the $+$ on the left is addition in $K$; the $+$ on the right is addition in $V$. In V5, $a\cdot b$ is multiplication in $K$, while $b\cdot v$ is scalar multiplication (footnote 1, p. 33). Reading these correctly is the first exam skill here. When in doubt, write $+_K$, $+_V$, $0_K$, $0_V$.

In the lecture. Halbeisen states the definition as “$(V,\circ)$ an abelian group, $(K,+,\cdot,0,1)$ a field, and $*:K\times V\to V$ with (a) $1*x=x$, (b) $(\lambda+\mu)*x=(\lambda*x)\circ(\mu*x)$, (c) $(\lambda\cdot\mu)*x=\lambda*(\mu*x)$, (d) $\lambda*(x\circ y)=(\lambda*x)\circ(\lambda*y)$” (p. 14). The Serie 2 solutions label these four as (V1)–(V4) in yet another order: (V1) $=$ script V7, (V2) $=$ V8, (V3) $=$ V5, (V4) $=$ V6. When you read a Musterlösung, check which numbering it uses.

2. Arithmetic rules (Lemma 2.0.2)

Lemma 2.0.2 (properties of vector spaces, p. 33). Let $V$ be a vector space over $K$. Then:

  1. the zero vector in V2 is unique;
  2. the additive inverse in V3 is unique, written $-v$, and $w-v:=w+(-v)$;
  3. $0_K\cdot v=0_V$ for all $v\in V$;
  4. $a\cdot0_V=0_V$ for all $a\in K$;
  5. $(-1)\cdot v=-v$ for all $v\in V$;
  6. $-(-v)=v$ for all $v\in V$;
  7. if $a\cdot v=0_V$, then $a=0$ or $v=0_V$;
  8. associativity also holds for sums of $n$ vectors.

The script states these without proof. Serie 2, Aufgabe 10 asks for (c), (d), (e), (g), and its official solution is the model for the proofs below. Parts (a), (b), (f) are lesson 1's Proofs A and B and lesson 4's “inverse of the inverse”, applied to the group $(V,+)$.

Proof A: $0_K\cdot v=0_V$ and $a\cdot0_V=0_V$ (parts (c), (d))

Helper (from the Serie 2 solution). If $u\in V$ satisfies $u+u=u$, then $u=0_V$.

Claim. $0_K\cdot v=0_V$ for all $v$, and $a\cdot0_V=0_V$ for all $a$.

The idea. Zero is the only vector that doesn't change when you add it to itself. And $0_K\cdot v$ has that property, because $0_K=0_K+0_K$ and V8 splits it.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • To show: an equation $u=0_V$ for the specific vector $u=0_K\cdot v$.
  • Tools: V8 (split a sum of scalars), V7 (split a sum of vectors), and the group $(V,+)$ to cancel.
  • The gap: produce an equation $u+u=u$, then cancel.
The storyWhat you write

First the helper: an arrow that, laid end to end with itself, gives itself back.

Helper. Let $u+u=u$. Then $0_V=u+(-u)=(u+u)+(-u)=u+(u+(-u))=u+0_V=u$, using V3, the assumption, V1, V3 and V2.

Stretch any arrow by zero. Zero is zero plus zero, so the stretched arrow equals itself plus itself.

(c) Since $0_K+0_K=0_K$ in $K$, V8 gives $0_K\cdot v=(0_K+0_K)\cdot v=0_K\cdot v+0_K\cdot v$.

By the helper, it must be the zero arrow.

The helper with $u:=0_K\cdot v$ gives $0_K\cdot v=0_V$.

The same trick works for stretching the zero arrow, splitting the arrow instead of the number.

(d) Since $0_V+0_V=0_V$, V7 gives $a\cdot0_V=a\cdot(0_V+0_V)=a\cdot0_V+a\cdot0_V$, and the helper with $u:=a\cdot0_V$ gives $a\cdot0_V=0_V$. ∎

Moves used: write $0=0+0$ and distribute; cancel with the helper “$u+u=u\Rightarrow u=0$”.

The proof in one piece

Helper: if $u+u=u$, then $0_V=u+(-u)=(u+u)+(-u)=u+(u+(-u))=u+0_V=u$. (c) By V8, $0_K\cdot v=(0_K+0_K)\cdot v=0_K\cdot v+0_K\cdot v$, so $0_K\cdot v=0_V$ by the helper. (d) By V7, $a\cdot0_V=a\cdot(0_V+0_V)=a\cdot0_V+a\cdot0_V$, so $a\cdot0_V=0_V$ by the helper. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1. In (c), which “$+$” is used in $0_K+0_K=0_K$, and which in $0_K\cdot v+0_K\cdot v$?

    Answer

    The first is addition in $K$, the second is addition in $V$. V8 is the bridge between them.

  2. Rung 2. Compare with lesson 4: which ring proof is this, almost word for word?

    Answer

    Lemma 1.3.4, $0\cdot a=0$ in a ring: $0\cdot a=(0+0)\cdot a=0\cdot a+0\cdot a$, then cancel.

  3. Rung 3. Write the helper and (c) from the frame alone.

Proof B: $(-1)\cdot v=-v$ (part (e))

Claim. For all $v\in V$, $(-1)\cdot v=-v$.

The idea. $-v$ is the vector that gives $0_V$ when added to $v$. So show that $(-1)\cdot v$ does that, and let uniqueness of inverses finish.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • To show: $(-1)\cdot v$ is an additive inverse of $v$, i.e. $v+(-1)\cdot v=0_V$.
  • Then: inverses in a group are unique (lesson 1, Proof B), so it equals $-v$.
  • The gap: turn $v$ into a scalar multiple ($v=1\cdot v$) so V8 can combine the two terms.
The storyWhat you write

Write the arrow as “stretched by one”, so both arrows are stretchings of the same arrow.

$v+(-1)\cdot v=1\cdot v+(-1)\cdot v$ (V6)

Combine the stretchings: one plus minus one is zero.

$\phantom{v+(-1)\cdot v}=(1+(-1))\cdot v=0_K\cdot v$ (V8)

Stretching by zero gives the zero arrow, so $(-1)\cdot v$ undoes $v$. There is only one undoer.

$\phantom{v+(-1)\cdot v}=0_V$ (part (c)). So $(-1)\cdot v$ is an additive inverse of $v$; by uniqueness of inverses, $(-1)\cdot v=-v$. ∎

Moves used: insert a middle point (here $v=1\cdot v$); “is an inverse” + uniqueness.

The proof in one piece

Let $v\in V$. By V6, V8 and (c), $v+(-1)\cdot v=1\cdot v+(-1)\cdot v=(1+(-1))\cdot v=0_K\cdot v=0_V$. So $(-1)\cdot v$ is an additive inverse of $v$. Since inverses in the group $(V,+)$ are unique, $(-1)\cdot v=-v$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1. Why can't we just “cancel $v$”?

    Answer

    There is no division by vectors. The only tools are the axioms, and uniqueness of inverses is how we identify $-v$.

  2. Rung 2. Write the chain of equalities with the reason for each step.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof B from the frame alone.

Proof C: $a\cdot v=0_V$ forces $a=0$ or $v=0_V$ (part (g))

Claim. For all $a\in K$ and $v\in V$: if $a\cdot v=0_V$, then $a=0$ or $v=0_V$.

The idea. An “or” conclusion: assume the first part fails ($a\ne0$) and prove the second. Then $a$ has an inverse in the field, and we can undo the stretching.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $a\cdot v=0_V$. If $a=0$ we are done; so let $a\ne0$.”
  • Last line: “Hence $v=0_V$.”
  • The gap: use $a^{-1}$ (it exists because $K$ is a field) and V5, V6.
The storyWhat you write

A wizard stretched an arrow and got the zero arrow. Either the wizard used zero, or…

Let $a\cdot v=0_V$. If $a=0$, done. Otherwise $a\ne0$, so $a^{-1}\in K$ exists (field).

…the wizard can shrink it back. Shrinking the zero arrow gives zero.

$v=1\cdot v=(a^{-1}a)\cdot v=a^{-1}\cdot(a\cdot v)=a^{-1}\cdot0_V=0_V$, by V6, V5 and part (d). ∎

Moves used: case split for an “or” goal; multiply by the inverse.

The proof in one piece

Let $a\in K$, $v\in V$ with $a\cdot v=0_V$. If $a=0$ the claim holds. Let $a\ne0$. Since $K$ is a field, $a^{-1}$ exists, and by V6, V5 and (d), $v=1\cdot v=(a^{-1}a)\cdot v=a^{-1}\cdot(a\cdot v)=a^{-1}\cdot0_V=0_V$. In each case $a=0$ or $v=0_V$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1. Where is it used that $K$ is a field and not just a ring?

    Answer

    In “$a^{-1}$ exists for $a\ne0$”. Over a ring like $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$, “$\bar2\cdot\bar3=\bar0$” shows the analogue fails.

  2. Rung 2. Write the contrapositive form: if $a\ne0$ and $v\ne0_V$, then …?

    Answer

    … $a\cdot v\ne0_V$.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof C from the frame alone.

How to reproduce Lemma 2.0.2 under exam pressure
  1. (c), (d): write $0=0+0$ (in $K$ or in $V$), distribute with V8 or V7, cancel with “$u+u=u\Rightarrow u=0_V$”.
  2. (e): $v+(-1)v=1v+(-1)v=(1-1)v=0v=0_V$, then uniqueness of inverses.
  3. (g): if $a\ne0$, $v=1v=(a^{-1}a)v=a^{-1}(av)=a^{-1}0_V=0_V$.

In short: “zero splits, minus one undoes, a nonzero scalar can be undone.” Name the axiom at every equals sign: that is where the points are.

3. The coordinate space $K^n$

Example 2.0.3 (p. 34). For a field $K$ and $n\in\mathbb N$, $K^n=\{(a_1,\dots,a_n):a_i\in K\}$ with componentwise operations

$(a_1,\dots,a_n)+(b_1,\dots,b_n):=(a_1+b_1,\dots,a_n+b_n)$, $\quad a\cdot(a_1,\dots,a_n):=(aa_1,\dots,aa_n)$.

All axioms follow from the field axioms of $K$, one component at a time. The zero vector is $(0,\dots,0)$.

Note 2.0.4 (p. 34). Rows $(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ and columns $\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\ \vdots\\ x_n\end{pmatrix}$ are strictly different sets, but we usually don't care and write rows because they're shorter. Columns matter once we multiply matrices by vectors.

Finite example (Serie 2, Aufgabe 13). Over $\mathbb F_p$, each of the $n$ components takes one of $p$ values, so $|\mathbb F_p^n|=p^n$. In $\mathbb F_2^3$ there are $8$ vectors, and $v+v=(1+1)v=0\cdot v=0_V$ for each: every vector is its own inverse.

Why it's cool. The script promises (p. 34) that every $n$-dimensional vector space over $K$ “is” $K^n$ (isomorphic to it). So this plain example is, in a precise sense, all finite-dimensional vector spaces at once. That will be proved in Chapter 3.

4. The zoo: functions, sequences, polynomials, matrices, sets

Function spaces (p. 38). For a nonempty set $S$, $K^S=\{f:S\to K\}$ with pointwise operations $(f+g)(x):=f(x)+g(x)$ and $(a\cdot f)(x):=a\cdot f(x)$ is a vector space over $K$. The zero vector is the zero function.

Every other example in this section is secretly a function space:

  • Sequences (p. 37). $K^\infty=\{(a_1,a_2,\dots):a_i\in K\}$, added and scaled termwise. A sequence is a function $\mathbb N\to K$ (Note 2.1.19, p. 39).
  • Fibonacci sequences (Example 2.1.12, p. 38). $\mathrm{Fib}=\{(a_i):a_n=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}\text{ for all }n\ge3\}$ is a vector space inside $K^\infty$, since sums and multiples of solutions are solutions. This is how the script opens the whole course (Chapter 0).
  • Polynomials (p. 40). $K[x]$ with $a\cdot(a_0+a_1x+\dots+a_nx^n):=aa_0+aa_1x+\dots+aa_nx^n$. Also $K[x]_h$, the polynomials of degree at most $h$ (Example 2.1.21).
  • Matrices (p. 40). $M_{m\times n}(K)$, added and scaled entry by entry. This is $K^{mn}$ written as a rectangle.
  • The power set (Example 2.1.23, p. 41). $\mathcal P(X)$ over $\mathbb F_2$ with symmetric difference $\triangle$ as addition, $\emptyset$ as zero vector, $1\cdot A=A$ and $0\cdot A=\emptyset$. Worked example 3 checks it.

In the lecture. Halbeisen calls $K^\infty$ the sequence space $S(K)$ and starts sequences at $a_0$. Matrices are written with the column as upper index, $a^j_i$ for row $i$, column $j$; the script writes $a_{ij}$.

5. Fields as vector spaces over smaller fields

The lecture (pp. 15–16) adds one more family, and Quiz 2 tests it: if $K\subseteq L$ are fields with the same operations, then $L$ is a vector space over $K$, with scalar multiplication the multiplication in $L$. All eight axioms are field axioms of $L$. Examples: $\mathbb R$ over $\mathbb Q$, $\mathbb C$ over $\mathbb R$, $K$ over $K$ (that is $K^1$), and $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ over $\mathbb Q$.

Claim (lecture, pp. 15–16). $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2):=\{a+b\sqrt2:a,b\in\mathbb Q\}$, with the operations of $\mathbb R$, is a field.

Proof (only closure needs checking)

$\mathbb R$ is a field, so associativity, commutativity and distributivity hold automatically. Sums: $(a_1+b_1\sqrt2)+(a_2+b_2\sqrt2)=(a_1+a_2)+(b_1+b_2)\sqrt2$. Products: $(a_1+b_1\sqrt2)(a_2+b_2\sqrt2)=(a_1a_2+2b_1b_2)+(a_1b_2+a_2b_1)\sqrt2$. $0=0+0\sqrt2$, $1=1+0\sqrt2$, $-(a+b\sqrt2)=(-a)+(-b)\sqrt2$. Inverses: if $(a,b)\ne(0,0)$ then $a^2-2b^2\ne0$ (otherwise $b\ne0$ and $(a/b)^2=2$, contradicting $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$ from lesson 3), and $$(a+b\sqrt2)\left(\tfrac{a}{a^2-2b^2}-\tfrac{b}{a^2-2b^2}\sqrt2\right)=\tfrac{a^2-2b^2}{a^2-2b^2}=1.$$ ∎

Why it's cool. $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ over $\mathbb Q$ is a tiny two-dimensional world with “basis” $1,\sqrt2$, while $\mathbb R$ over $\mathbb Q$ is infinite-dimensional and strange. One charter covers both, and also the finite worlds $\mathbb F_2^n$ used in error-correcting codes.

Worked examples

Try each step in your head before opening the answer.

Worked example 1: all functions $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ form a real vector space (Serie 2, Aufgabe 14)

  1. What must you check first, before any axiom?

    Answer

    That the operations land in $V$: $f+g$ and $a\cdot f$ are again functions $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, since $f(x)+g(x)\in\mathbb R$ and $a\cdot f(x)\in\mathbb R$ for every $x$.

  2. When are two functions equal, and why does that make every axiom easy?

    Answer

    $f=g$ iff $f(x)=g(x)$ for all $x$. So each axiom only has to hold at each point $x$, where it is an axiom of the field $\mathbb R$. Example V7: $(a\cdot(f+g))(x)=a(f(x)+g(x))=af(x)+ag(x)=(a\cdot f+a\cdot g)(x)$.

  3. Name the zero vector and the inverse of $f$.

    Answer

    The zero function $x\mapsto0$, and $-f:x\mapsto-f(x)$. With these, V1–V4 hold pointwise, and V5–V8 hold pointwise by associativity and distributivity in $\mathbb R$ and $1\cdot f(x)=f(x)$.

Worked example 2: a scalar multiple in $\mathbb F_{11}^{11}$ (Serie 2, Aufgabe 13(b))

Compute $\bar7\cdot(\bar0,\bar1,\bar2,\bar3,\bar4,\bar5,\bar6,\bar7,\bar8,\bar9,\overline{10})$.

  1. What does the scalar multiplication do?

    Answer

    It multiplies each component by $\bar7$ in $\mathbb F_{11}$: compute $7k$ and take the remainder mod $11$.

  2. The result?

    Answer

    $7k$ for $k=0,\dots,10$: $0,7,14,21,28,35,42,49,56,63,70$, remainders $0,7,3,10,6,2,9,5,1,8,4$. So the result is $(\bar0,\bar7,\bar3,\overline{10},\bar6,\bar2,\bar9,\bar5,\bar1,\bar8,\bar4)$. Every class appears exactly once: multiplication by $\bar7$ is the bijection $m_{\bar7}$ from lesson 3, Proof D.

Worked example 3: $\mathcal P(X)$ is a vector space over $\mathbb F_2$ (Example 2.1.23; Quiz 2, part 4)

  1. Addition is $\triangle$. What are the zero vector and $-A$?

    Answer

    $\emptyset$, and $-A=A$, since $A\triangle A=\emptyset$ (lesson 1, worked example 2).

  2. Why are $1\cdot A=A$ and $0\cdot A=\emptyset$ forced?

    Answer

    V6 forces $1\cdot A=A$; Lemma 2.0.2 (c) forces $0\cdot A=0_V=\emptyset$.

  3. Check V8 for $a_1=a_2=1$.

    Answer

    Left: $(1+1)\cdot A=0\cdot A=\emptyset$. Right: $1\cdot A\triangle1\cdot A=A\triangle A=\emptyset$. ✓ The other cases ($a_i\in\{0,1\}$) are just as short.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (Quiz 2, with the official solution)

For which pairs is $V$ a vector space over $K$ (with the natural scalar multiplication)? (1) $(\mathbb R,+)$ over $\mathbb Q$. (2) $(\mathbb Q,+)$ over $\mathbb R$. (3) $(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ over $\mathbb Q$. (4) $(\mathcal P(\mathbb N),\triangle)$ over $\mathbb F_2$.

Solution

1 yes, 2 no, 3 no, 4 yes.

(1) $\mathbb Q\subseteq\mathbb R$ is a subfield, so $\mathbb R$ is a $\mathbb Q$-vector space (section 5). (2) The natural multiplication leaves $\mathbb Q$: $\sqrt2\cdot1=\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$. The official solution even shows no scalar multiplication works: $\lambda\mapsto\lambda\cdot1$ would be an injective map $\mathbb R\to\mathbb Q$ (by Lemma 2.0.2 (g)), impossible since $\mathbb Q$ is countable and $\mathbb R$ is not (lesson 3). (3) Here “vector addition” is multiplication and $0_V=1$. Take $x=-1$: $2\cdot x=x+_Vx=(-1)(-1)=1=0_V$, but $2\ne0$ and $x\ne0_V$, contradicting Lemma 2.0.2 (g). (4) Worked example 3.

Exercise 2 (LinAlg I exam HS18, Aufgabe 2(a), 6 points)

Let $M$ be a finite set and $V:=\{f:M\to K\}$. Show that $V$ with $(f+g)(x):=f(x)+g(x)$ and $(af)(x):=af(x)$ is a $K$-vector space.

Solution

Frame: operations land in $V$; two functions are equal iff equal at every point; then every axiom is a field axiom at each point.

For $f,g\in V$, $a\in K$, the rules $x\mapsto f(x)+g(x)$ and $x\mapsto af(x)$ assign to each $x\in M$ one element of $K$, so $f+g,af\in V$. For all $x\in M$: V1 and V4 hold since $(f+(g+h))(x)=f(x)+(g(x)+h(x))=(f(x)+g(x))+h(x)$ and $f(x)+g(x)=g(x)+f(x)$ in $K$. V2: the zero map $0_V(x)=0$ satisfies $(0_V+f)(x)=f(x)$. V3: $-f:x\mapsto-f(x)$ satisfies $(f+(-f))(x)=0$. V5–V8: $a(bf(x))=(ab)f(x)$, $1f(x)=f(x)$, $a(f(x)+g(x))=af(x)+ag(x)$, $(a+b)f(x)=af(x)+bf(x)$, by the field axioms of $K$. Since all these hold at every $x$, they hold as equalities of functions. So $V$ is a $K$-vector space. ∎ (Finiteness of $M$ is not needed here; it matters in part (b), the dimension.)

Exercise 3 (Serie 2, Aufgabe 11)

Show that $\mathrm{Fib}=\{(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}:f_{i+2}=f_{i+1}+f_i\text{ for all }i\}$, with termwise addition, is a vector space over $\mathbb R$. (Lesson 1, Exercise 5 showed it is an abelian group.)

Solution

Define $\lambda\cdot(f_n):=(\lambda f_n)$. It lands in $\mathrm{Fib}$: $\lambda f_{i+2}=\lambda(f_{i+1}+f_i)=\lambda f_{i+1}+\lambda f_i$. Two sequences are equal iff equal in every term, and termwise V5–V8 are associativity of multiplication, $1\cdot f_n=f_n$ and the two distributive laws in $\mathbb R$. So $\mathrm{Fib}$ is a real vector space. (Same calculation for all sequences $K^\infty$; $\mathrm{Fib}$ is closed under both operations inside it.)

Exercise 4 (Serie 2, Aufgabe 12)

Show that $\mathbb Q[X]$ with coefficientwise addition and $\lambda\cdot\sum a_iX^i:=\sum(\lambda a_i)X^i$ is a vector space over $\mathbb Q$.

Solution

Closure: if $a_i=0$ for $i>n$, then $\lambda a_i=\lambda\cdot0=0$ for $i>n$, so $\lambda p$ is again a polynomial with rational coefficients. Two polynomials are equal iff all coefficients agree, so the axioms can be checked coefficient by coefficient, where they are field axioms of $\mathbb Q$. ($\lambda\cdot p$ is also just the ring product of the constant polynomial $\lambda$ with $p$; that makes $\mathbb Q[X]$ an algebra over $\mathbb Q$.)

Exercise 5 (find the broken axiom)

On $\mathbb R^2$ with the usual addition, define $a*(x_1,x_2):=(ax_1,0)$. Which axiom fails?

Solution

V6: $1*(0,1)=(0,0)\ne(0,1)$. V5, V7 and V8 all hold, because both sides only involve the first coordinate, scaled as usual. So V6 is the only axiom that fails, which shows it can't be dropped.

Exercise 6 (an inverse in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$)

Compute $(1+\sqrt2)^{-1}$ in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ with the formula of section 5, and check it.

Solution

$a=b=1$, $a^2-2b^2=-1$, so the inverse is $\frac1{-1}-\frac1{-1}\sqrt2=-1+\sqrt2$. Check: $(1+\sqrt2)(-1+\sqrt2)=-1+\sqrt2-\sqrt2+2=1$. ✓
