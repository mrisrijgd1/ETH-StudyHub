Recap of lesson 15 and lesson 16 (5 minutes)

Rank–nullity: $\dim\mathrm{Ker}(m_A)+\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$. Row operations keep the row space and the solutions of $Ax=0$; the pivot columns of $A$ are a basis of $\mathrm{ColS}(A)$; row rank $=$ column rank. A square matrix with a one-sided inverse is invertible (lesson 15, Proof D). Check yourself:

  1. $A\in M_{3\times5}(K)$ has rank $3$. How many free variables does $Ax=0$ have?
  2. $Ax=b$ has a solution if and only if $b$ lies in…?
  3. Is $(AB)^{-1}=A^{-1}B^{-1}$?
  4. Which row operation undoes $L_2-3L_1\to L_2$?
Answers

1. $5-3=2$. 2. $\mathrm{ColS}(A)$, because $Ax=\sum_jx_ja_j$. 3. No: $(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}$ (lesson 12; socks and shoes). 4. $L_2+3L_1\to L_2$. Today each row operation becomes a matrix, and “undo” becomes “inverse matrix”.

1. The story: the clerks' stamps

Since lesson 16, the clerks of the Free City tidy their ledgers with three moves: swap two lines, rescale a line, add a multiple of one line to another. The council now asks them to stamp every move into the ledger's margin, so that an auditor can check the work later. The guild of mapmakers notices that each stamp is itself a small ledger: the identity with one tiny change. Pressing a stamp onto a ledger is multiplying by that matrix.

Two discoveries follow. First, every stamp can be cancelled by another stamp: the moves are reversible. Second, when the clerks manage to tidy a ledger all the way to the identity, the pile of stamps they used, multiplied together, is the inverse of the ledger. So the clerks can compute inverses without any new idea: they tidy the ledger and, on a second page beside it, they let the same stamps act on the identity. What appears on the second page is the inverse.

Before that, the council's old question: when a ledger is a system of equations, what does the set of all its solutions look like?

In mathematics: elementary matrices $E$ satisfy $EA=$ “$A$ after one row operation”; every $A\in\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$ is a product of them; and row reducing $(A\,|\,I_n)$ to $(I_n\,|\,B)$ gives $B=A^{-1}$.

2. Solution sets of linear systems

Definition (solution sets; script, p. 100). For $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ and $b\in K^m$ let $\mathrm{Sol}(A,b):=\{x\in K^n\mid Ax=b\}$. The system is homogeneous if $b=0$; then $\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)=\mathrm{Ker}(m_A)$ is a subspace. If $A'$ is a reduced row echelon form of $A$ with $r=\mathrm{rank}(A)$ pivots in columns $j_1<\dots<j_r$, the variables $x_{j_1},\dots,x_{j_r}$ are the pivot variables and the other $l=n-r$ variables $x_{i_1},\dots,x_{i_l}$ are the free variables.

Proof A: the free variables parametrise the solutions (script Corollaries “$\Psi$ is an isomorphism” and 3.6.1, pp. 100–101)

Claim. Let $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ have rank $r$, with free variables $x_{i_1},\dots,x_{i_l}$, $l=n-r$. For every $\lambda\in K^l$ there is exactly one $x\in\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)$ with $x_{i_1}=\lambda_1,\dots,x_{i_l}=\lambda_l$; call it $\Psi(\lambda)$. Then $\Psi:K^l\to\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)$ is a linear isomorphism. In particular $\dim\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)=n-\mathrm{rank}(A)$, and $\Psi(e_1),\dots,\Psi(e_l)$ is a basis of $\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)$.

The idea. In reduced form, row $k$ reads “$x_{j_k}$ $+$ (a combination of free variables) $=0$”. So the pivot variables are forced, and forced linearly, by the free ones. Reading off the free entries undoes $\Psi$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “$\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)=\mathrm{Sol}(A',0)$ for the reduced form $A'$ (lesson 16).”
  • Last line: “$\Psi$ is linear and bijective, so $\dim\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)=l=n-r$.”
  • The gap: the equations of $A'$ solved for the pivot variables: $x_{j_k}=-\sum_qa'_{k,i_q}x_{i_q}$.
The storyWhat you write

Tidying does not change which recipes balance the ledger.

Row operations do not change the solutions of $Ax=0$ (lesson 16), so $\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)=\mathrm{Sol}(A',0)$ for the reduced row echelon form $A'=(a'_{kj})$.

Each tidy line says how one forced quantity depends on the free ones.

Row $k$ of $A'x=0$ ($k\le r$) reads $x_{j_k}+\sum_{q}a'_{k,i_q}x_{i_q}=0$, since the pivot columns of $A'$ are standard vectors; rows $k>r$ are zero. So $x\in\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)$ iff $x_{j_k}=-\sum_qa'_{k,i_q}x_{i_q}$ for all $k\le r$.

Choose the free quantities, and everything else is decided, linearly.

Hence for $\lambda\in K^l$ the unique solution with free entries $\lambda$ is $\Psi(\lambda)$ with $\Psi(\lambda)_{i_q}=\lambda_q$ and $\Psi(\lambda)_{j_k}=-\sum_qa'_{k,i_q}\lambda_q$. Every entry is a linear expression in $\lambda$, so $\Psi$ is linear.

Reading the free quantities off a solution undoes the choice.

The map $x\mapsto(x_{i_1},\dots,x_{i_l})$ is inverse to $\Psi$: it recovers $\lambda$ from $\Psi(\lambda)$, and $\Psi$ applied to the free entries of a solution $x$ gives $x$ back, by uniqueness. So $\Psi$ is an isomorphism, $\dim\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)=l=n-r$, and the images of the standard basis form a basis (lesson 12). ∎

Moves used: parametrise by the free variables show it is the inverse, then use uniqueness

The proof in one piece

$\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)=\mathrm{Sol}(A',0)$ for the reduced form $A'$. Its nonzero rows say $x_{j_k}=-\sum_qa'_{k,i_q}x_{i_q}$ ($k\le r$), so each choice $\lambda$ of the free variables extends to exactly one solution $\Psi(\lambda)$, whose entries are linear in $\lambda$. Reading off the free entries is an inverse of $\Psi$. So $\Psi:K^{n-r}\to\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)$ is a linear isomorphism and $\dim\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)=n-\mathrm{rank}(A)$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Reduce; solve each nonzero row for its pivot variable.
  2. Free variables arbitrary, pivot variables linear in them: $\Psi$ linear.
  3. Inverse: read off the free entries.

In short: one basis vector of the kernel per free variable: set that free variable $1$, the others $0$, and solve. This is rank–nullity again, now with an explicit basis.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. For $A'=\begin{pmatrix}1&3&0&2\\0&0&1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, write $\Psi(\lambda_1,\lambda_2)$ and a basis of the kernel.
    Answer

    Free: $x_2,x_4$. $x_1=-3\lambda_1-2\lambda_2$, $x_3=\lambda_2$. $\Psi(\lambda)=\lambda_1(-3,1,0,0)+\lambda_2(-2,0,1,1)$; basis $(-3,1,0,0),(-2,0,1,1)$.

  2. Rung 2. Why does the proof need the reduced form, not just any echelon form?
    Answer

    In the reduced form each equation contains exactly one pivot variable, so it can be solved directly. With a plain echelon form you would solve from the bottom up (back substitution); the result is the same map $\Psi$, only the bookkeeping is longer.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof A from the frame alone.

Proof B: all solutions = one solution + the homogeneous solutions (script Corollary 3.6.2, p. 101)

Claim. Let $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ and $b\in K^m$. (1) $\mathrm{Sol}(A,b)\ne\varnothing$ if and only if $b\in\mathrm{ColS}(A)$. (2) If $y\in\mathrm{Sol}(A,b)$, then $\mathrm{Sol}(A,b)=y+\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)=\{y+x\mid Ax=0\}$.

The idea. $Ax$ is the combination of the columns with coefficients $x$, which gives (1). For (2): two solutions of $Ax=b$ differ by a solution of $Ax=0$, because $A$ is linear. This is lesson 11's “fibres are cosets of the kernel” in the language of systems.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (2) “⊇”, first line: “Let $x\in\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)$.” Last line: “so $y+x\in\mathrm{Sol}(A,b)$.”
  • (2) “⊆”, first line: “Let $z\in\mathrm{Sol}(A,b)$, and put $x:=z-y$.” Last line: “so $z=y+x$ with $x\in\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)$.”
  • The gap: $A(u\pm v)=Au\pm Av$.
The storyWhat you write

The ledger can produce exactly the mixtures of its columns.

(1) $Ax=x_1a_1+\dots+x_na_n$ for the columns $a_j$. So some $x$ has $Ax=b$ iff $b\in\mathrm{Sp}(a_1,\dots,a_n)=\mathrm{ColS}(A)$.

Adding a recipe that balances to zero does not change the result.

(2) “⊇”: if $Ax=0$, then $A(y+x)=Ay+Ax=b+0=b$.

Two recipes with the same result differ by one that balances to zero.

“⊆”: if $Az=b$, put $x:=z-y$. Then $Ax=Az-Ay=b-b=0$, so $z=y+x\in y+\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)$. ∎

Moves used: two names, subtract the matrix column by column

The proof in one piece

(1) $Ax=\sum_jx_ja_j$, so $Ax=b$ is solvable iff $b\in\mathrm{ColS}(A)$. (2) If $Ax=0$ then $A(y+x)=b$; if $Az=b$ then $A(z-y)=0$ and $z=y+(z-y)$. Hence $\mathrm{Sol}(A,b)=y+\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Solvable iff $b\in\mathrm{ColS}(A)$ iff $\mathrm{rank}(A\,|\,b)=\mathrm{rank}(A)$ (lesson 16, Exercise 5).
  2. Two inclusions, each one line of linearity.

In short: general solution $=$ particular solution $+$ general homogeneous solution. So the answer to “give all solutions” always has the shape $y+\lambda_1u_1+\dots+\lambda_lu_l$: an affine subspace (lesson 9) of dimension $n-\mathrm{rank}(A)$, or the empty set.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Is $\mathrm{Sol}(A,b)$ a subspace when $b\ne0$?
    Answer

    No: $0\notin\mathrm{Sol}(A,b)$, since $A0=0\ne b$. It is an affine subspace (a translate of one), or empty.

  2. Rung 2. Show: if $Ax=b$ has exactly one solution for one $b$, then $Ax=c$ has at most one solution for every $c$.
    Answer

    Exactly one solution means $\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)=\{0\}$ by (2). Then by (2) again every nonempty $\mathrm{Sol}(A,c)$ is a single point $y+\{0\}$.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof B from the frame alone.

3. When is a square matrix invertible? Many equivalent answers

Proof C: the equivalences for invertible matrices (script Proposition 3.6.8, pp. 103–104)

Claim. For $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ the following are equivalent:

  1. $A$ is invertible.
  2. $m_A$ is injective; equivalently, $Ax=0$ only has the solution $x=0$; equivalently, the columns of $A$ are independent.
  3. $m_A$ is surjective; equivalently, $Ax=b$ is solvable for every $b$; equivalently, the columns of $A$ span $K^n$.
  4. $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$; equivalently, the rows of $A$ are a basis of $K^n$.
  5. $Ax=b$ has exactly one solution for every $b\in K^n$.
  6. $A^T$ is invertible.
  7. $A$ is row equivalent to $I_n$: row operations turn $A$ into $I_n$.

The idea. Almost everything is already proved: a square matrix is invertible iff $m_A$ is bijective (lesson 12), and for a map $K^n\to K^n$ injective, surjective and bijective coincide (lesson 15). The ranks connect columns and rows (lesson 16). The one new item is (7), and it is just Gaussian elimination run to the end.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “We show (1)⇔(2)⇔(3)⇔(4)⇔(5), then (1)⇔(6) and (4)⇔(7).”
  • Last line: “so all seven statements are equivalent.”
  • The gap: lesson 15, Proof C (equal dimensions), and for (7) that row operations keep the row space.
The storyWhat you write

An invertible ledger is a courier that can be sent back.

(1)⇔ $m_A$ bijective: if $AB=BA=I_n$ then $m_B$ is inverse to $m_A$; conversely the inverse of a bijective $m_A$ is linear, so equal to $m_B$ for $B=[m_A^{-1}]$, and $AB=BA=I_n$ (lesson 12).

Between equal cities, losing nothing, reaching everything and being reversible are the same.

(1)⇔(2)⇔(3): $m_A:K^n\to K^n$ is injective iff surjective iff bijective (lesson 15, Proof C). The reformulations: $\mathrm{Ker}(m_A)=\{0\}$ is injectivity, and $\mathrm{Im}(m_A)=\mathrm{ColS}(A)$ (lesson 15, Proof A).

Reaching everything means rank $n$; the row clerk counts the same.

(3)⇔(4): surjective iff $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$; and $\mathrm{rank}(A)=\mathrm{row\,rank}(A)$ (lesson 16), and $n$ rows spanning an $n$-dimensional space form a basis (lesson 7). (5)⇔[(2) and (3)] by Proof B, so (5)⇔(1).

The transposed ledger is reversible too.

(1)⇒(6): $(A^{-1})^TA^T=(AA^{-1})^T=I_n$ and likewise $A^T(A^{-1})^T=I_n$. (6)⇒(1): apply this to $A^T$, since $(A^T)^T=A$.

Full rank means the tidying ends at the identity, and a ledger that tidies to the identity has full rank.

(4)⇒(7): a reduced row echelon form $A'$ of $A$ has $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$ pivots in $n$ columns, so every column is a pivot column and $A'=I_n$. (7)⇒(4): row operations keep the row space (lesson 16), so $\mathrm{RowS}(A)=\mathrm{RowS}(I_n)=K^n$. ∎

Moves used: a chain of named equivalences count with rank–nullity swap rows and columns with the transpose

The proof in one piece

$A$ is invertible iff $m_A$ is bijective (lesson 12), iff injective, iff surjective (equal dimensions, lesson 15). Injective means $Ax=0\Rightarrow x=0$, i.e. independent columns; surjective means $\mathrm{ColS}(A)=K^n$, i.e. $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$, i.e. (row rank $=$ column rank) the $n$ rows are a basis. Unique solvability for all $b$ is injective plus surjective (Proof B). $A^T$ is invertible with inverse $(A^{-1})^T$, and conversely by $(A^T)^T=A$. Finally, if $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$, the reduced echelon form has $n$ pivots in $n$ columns and equals $I_n$; and if $A$ is row equivalent to $I_n$, then $\mathrm{RowS}(A)=K^n$ and $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Invertible $\iff m_A$ bijective.
  2. Square: injective $\iff$ surjective $\iff$ bijective.
  3. Translate: kernel, columns, rank, rows.
  4. Row equivalent to $I_n$: reduced form with $n$ pivots.

In short: for square matrices, one good property gives all of them. In an exam, pick the one that is easiest to check (often: “$Ax=0$ has only the trivial solution”), and say “so $A$ is invertible”. Lesson 18 adds one more item to the list: $\det A\ne0$.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. $A\in M_{4\times4}(\mathbb R)$ has two equal rows. Is $A$ invertible?
    Answer

    No: the rows are dependent, so (4) fails, so (1) fails.

  2. Rung 2. Which of the seven items fail for a non-square $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$, $m\ne n$, even if $\mathrm{rank}(A)=\min(m,n)$?
    Answer

    Injective and surjective cannot both hold (lesson 15, Proof C: too small or too big), so (1), (5), (6), (7) fail, and exactly one of (2), (3) can hold.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof C from the frame alone.

4. Elementary matrices: row operations as multiplication

Definition (elementary matrices; script Definition 3.6.12, p. 105). Let $E_{ij}\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ be the matrix with $1$ in position $(i,j)$ and $0$ elsewhere. For $i\ne j$, $\lambda\in K$ and $\mu\in K\setminus\{0\}$:

  • $Q_{i,j}(\lambda):=I_n+\lambda E_{ij}$ (the identity with an extra $\lambda$ in position $(i,j)$);
  • $P_{i,j}:=I_n-E_{ii}-E_{jj}+E_{ij}+E_{ji}$ (the identity with rows $i$ and $j$ swapped; a permutation matrix);
  • $S_i(\mu):=I_n+(\mu-1)E_{ii}$ (the identity with $\mu$ in position $(i,i)$).

These are the elementary matrices. Example in $M_{3\times3}(K)$: $Q_{2,1}(-2)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\-2&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $P_{1,3}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&1&0\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}$, $S_2(5)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&5&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$.

Proof D: multiplying by an elementary matrix performs a row operation, and elementary matrices are invertible (script Lemmas 3.6.14 and 3.6.15, pp. 106–107)

Claim. Let $A\in M_{n\times p}(K)$ with rows $u_1,\dots,u_n$. (1) $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)A$ is $A$ after $L_i+\lambda L_j\to L_i$; $P_{i,j}A$ is $A$ after $L_i\leftrightarrow L_j$; $S_i(\mu)A$ is $A$ after $\mu L_i\to L_i$. (2) $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)^{-1}=Q_{i,j}(-\lambda)$, $P_{i,j}^{-1}=P_{i,j}$ and $S_i(\mu)^{-1}=S_i(\mu^{-1})$. In particular every elementary matrix is invertible, with an elementary inverse.

The idea. Row $k$ of a product $EA$ is (row $k$ of $E$) times $A$, a combination of the rows of $A$ with the entries of row $k$ of $E$ as coefficients. An elementary matrix differs from $I_n$ in one or two rows, so only those rows of $A$ change, in exactly the prescribed way. For (2), apply (1) with $A$ the matrix of the undoing operation.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (1) First line: “Row $k$ of $EA$ is $\sum_le_{kl}u_l$.”
  • (2) First line: “By (1), $Q_{i,j}(-\lambda)Q_{i,j}(\lambda)$ is $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)$ after $L_i-\lambda L_j\to L_i$.” Last line: “$=I_n$, and the same in the other order.”
  • The gap: the rows of $E$ are standard rows $e_k^T$, except one or two.
The storyWhat you write

Each line of a stamp says which lines of the ledger to mix into the new line.

(1) For any $E=(e_{kl})$, row $k$ of $EA$ is $\sum_le_{kl}u_l$. If row $k$ of $E$ is $e_k^T$, row $k$ of $EA$ is $u_k$: unchanged.

Each stamp changes only the lines it names.

$Q_{i,j}(\lambda)$: only row $i$ differs from $I_n$, it is $e_i^T+\lambda e_j^T$, so row $i$ of the product is $u_i+\lambda u_j$. $P_{i,j}$: rows $i$ and $j$ are $e_j^T$ and $e_i^T$, giving $u_j$ and $u_i$. $S_i(\mu)$: row $i$ is $\mu e_i^T$, giving $\mu u_i$.

Every stamp has a cancelling stamp.

(2) By (1) with $A=Q_{i,j}(\lambda)$: $Q_{i,j}(-\lambda)Q_{i,j}(\lambda)$ is $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)$ after $L_i-\lambda L_j\to L_i$. Row $j$ of $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)$ is $e_j^T$ ($j\ne i$), so row $i$ becomes $e_i^T+\lambda e_j^T-\lambda e_j^T=e_i^T$: the product is $I_n$. Exchanging $\lambda$ and $-\lambda$ gives the other order. Likewise swapping twice and scaling by $\mu$ then $\mu^{-1}$ give $I_n$. ∎

Moves used: write each row operation as a matrix undo the row operation

The proof in one piece

(1) Row $k$ of $EA$ is $\sum_le_{kl}u_l$. Rows of $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)$, $P_{i,j}$, $S_i(\mu)$ equal those of $I_n$ except row $i$ ($e_i^T+\lambda e_j^T$, resp. $e_j^T$, resp. $\mu e_i^T$) and, for $P_{i,j}$, row $j$ ($e_i^T$). So only these rows of $A$ change, into $u_i+\lambda u_j$, resp. $u_j$ and $u_i$, resp. $\mu u_i$. (2) By (1), $Q_{i,j}(-\lambda)Q_{i,j}(\lambda)$ is $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)$ with $\lambda e_j^T$ subtracted from row $i$, i.e. $I_n$; similarly in the other order and for $P_{i,j}P_{i,j}$ and $S_i(\mu^{-1})S_i(\mu)$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Row $k$ of $EA$ $=$ combination of the rows of $A$ with coefficients from row $k$ of $E$.
  2. Elementary: only one or two rows differ from $I_n$.
  3. Inverse $=$ the matrix of the undoing operation.

In short: left multiplication acts on rows, right multiplication on columns: $AQ_{i,j}(\lambda)$ is $A$ after $C_j+\lambda C_i\to C_j$ (Exercise 4). To find an elementary matrix, apply the operation to $I_n$: $E=E\cdot I_n$.

  1. Rung 1. Write the $3\times3$ elementary matrix that performs $L_3+4L_1\to L_3$, and its inverse.
    Answer

    $Q_{3,1}(4)$: $I_3$ with $4$ in position $(3,1)$. Inverse $Q_{3,1}(-4)$.

  2. Rung 2. Is the product of two elementary matrices elementary? (FS18, (xviii) asks whether they form a group.)
    Answer

    Not in general: $Q_{1,2}(1)Q_{2,1}(1)=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ differs from $I_2$ in both rows and in more than a swap, so it is none of the three types. So the elementary matrices are not a group (false); they generate the group $\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$ (Proof E).

Proof E: every invertible matrix is a product of elementary matrices (script Satz 3.6.16 and Corollary 3.6.18, p. 107)

Claim. Let $A\in\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$. There are elementary matrices $T_1,\dots,T_k$ with $T_k\cdots T_1A=I_n$. Then $A^{-1}=T_k\cdots T_1$ and $A=T_1^{-1}\cdots T_k^{-1}$ is a product of elementary matrices.

The idea. Gaussian elimination brings an invertible $A$ to $I_n$ (Proof C (7)); each step is a left multiplication (Proof D (1)). A product that sends $A$ to $I_n$ is a left inverse of $A$, hence the inverse. Undo the steps one by one to write $A$ itself.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “By Proof C, $A$ is row equivalent to $I_n$: row operations $\rho_1,\dots,\rho_k$ turn $A$ into $I_n$.”
  • Last line: “$A=T_1^{-1}\cdots T_k^{-1}$, a product of elementary matrices.”
  • The gap: Proof D, and $(XY)^{-1}=Y^{-1}X^{-1}$.
The storyWhat you write

The clerks tidy the reversible ledger down to the identity, stamping every move.

By Proof C (7), row operations turn $A$ into $I_n$. By Proof D (1), the $s$-th operation is left multiplication by an elementary $T_s$. So $T_k\cdots T_2T_1A=I_n$.

The pile of stamps is the way back.

$B:=T_k\cdots T_1$ satisfies $BA=I_n$; multiplying by $A^{-1}$ from the right gives $B=A^{-1}$.

Undo the stamps in reverse order, and the original ledger reappears.

Hence $A=B^{-1}=(T_k\cdots T_1)^{-1}=T_1^{-1}T_2^{-1}\cdots T_k^{-1}$, and each $T_s^{-1}$ is elementary by Proof D (2). ∎

Moves used: write each row operation as a matrix show it is the inverse, then use uniqueness

The proof in one piece

By Proof C, $A$ is row equivalent to $I_n$, and by Proof D each row operation is a left multiplication by an elementary matrix: $T_k\cdots T_1A=I_n$. So $T_k\cdots T_1$ is a left inverse, hence $A^{-1}=T_k\cdots T_1$, and $A=T_1^{-1}\cdots T_k^{-1}$ with every $T_s^{-1}$ elementary (Proof D). ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Invertible ⇒ row equivalent to $I_n$.
  2. Each step is a left multiplication.
  3. Product of the steps $=A^{-1}$; reverse and invert for $A$.

In short: $\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$ is generated by elementary matrices. Mind the order: the first operation stands on the right, next to $A$.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Why is $BA=I_n$ enough to conclude $B=A^{-1}$ here?
    Answer

    Because $A$ is already known to be invertible: $B=B(AA^{-1})=(BA)A^{-1}=A^{-1}$. (Even without that, lesson 15, Proof D says a one-sided inverse of a square matrix is two-sided.)

  2. Rung 2. Write $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\3&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ as $T_1^{-1}T_2^{-1}$: which operations turn the product into $I_2$, in which order?
    Answer

    The product is $\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\6&1\end{pmatrix}=Q_{2,1}(3)S_1(2)$. To reach $I_2$: first undo $Q_{2,1}(3)$ by $L_2-3L_1$ (giving $S_1(2)$), then $\tfrac12L_1$. So $T_1=Q_{2,1}(-3)$, $T_2=S_1(\tfrac12)$, and indeed $T_1^{-1}T_2^{-1}=Q_{2,1}(3)S_1(2)$.

Proof F: the Gauss–Jordan method computes the inverse (script Lemma 3.6.19 and Satz 3.6.20, pp. 107–108)

Claim. Let $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$. Bring the $n\times2n$ matrix $(A\,|\,I_n)$ by row operations as far as possible towards reduced row echelon form. If the left half becomes $I_n$, so that the result is $(I_n\,|\,B)$, then $A$ is invertible and $A^{-1}=B$. If the left half does not become $I_n$, then $A$ is not invertible.

The idea. Row operations on the wide matrix act on both halves at once, as one left multiplication: $T(A\,|\,I_n)=(TA\,|\,T)$. When the left half is $I_n$, the right half is the product $T$ of the steps, which is $A^{-1}$ by Proof E.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “For $T\in M_{n\times n}(K)$, $T(C\,|\,D)=(TC\,|\,TD)$ (multiply column by column).”
  • Last line: “so $B=T=A^{-1}$; and if the left half does not reach $I_n$, $A$ is not row equivalent to $I_n$.”
  • The gap: Proofs C (7), D and E.
The storyWhat you write

A stamp pressed on the double page acts on both pages.

Column $c$ of $T(C\,|\,D)$ is $T$ times column $c$ of $(C\,|\,D)$, so $T(C\,|\,D)=(TC\,|\,TD)$.

The second page records the product of all stamps.

If the operations, i.e. elementary $T_1,\dots,T_k$ (Proof D), give $(I_n\,|\,B)$, then with $T:=T_k\cdots T_1$: $(I_n\,|\,B)=T(A\,|\,I_n)=(TA\,|\,T)$. So $TA=I_n$ and $B=T$.

A ledger that tidies to the identity is reversible, and the record is its inverse.

$TA=I_n$ with $T$ invertible (a product of invertible matrices) gives $A=T^{-1}$, so $A$ is invertible and $A^{-1}=T=B$.

If the tidying gets stuck, the ledger was never reversible.

If no sequence of row operations turns the left half into $I_n$, then $A$ is not row equivalent to $I_n$, so $A$ is not invertible by Proof C (7). In practice: the reduced form of $A$ has fewer than $n$ pivots, i.e. $\mathrm{rank}(A)<n$. ∎

Moves used: write each row operation as a matrix the matrix column by column

The proof in one piece

Row operations are left multiplications by elementary $T_s$ (Proof D), and $T(C\,|\,D)=(TC\,|\,TD)$. If they turn $(A\,|\,I_n)$ into $(I_n\,|\,B)$, then $T=T_k\cdots T_1$ satisfies $TA=I_n$ and $T=B$; $T$ is invertible, so $A=T^{-1}$ is invertible with $A^{-1}=B$. If the left half cannot become $I_n$, $A$ is not row equivalent to $I_n$ and not invertible (Proof C). ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. $(A\,|\,I_n)$, eliminate down, then up, then scale.
  2. Right half $=A^{-1}$; a zero row on the left means “not invertible”.
  3. Always check one or two entries of $AA^{-1}=I_n$.

In short: do to the identity what you do to $A$. Points are lost on arithmetic, not on the method: check before you box the answer.

Worked example 2: a system with a parameter (HS20, problem 4, 12 points)

Worked example 3: quick exam items (FS17, FS18, HS17, FS19)

  1. FS18, (xix): if $E\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb R)$ is elementary and $b\in\mathbb R^n$, then $EX=b$ has a solution.
    Answer

    True. $E$ is invertible (Proof D), so $X=E^{-1}b$.

  2. FS18, (xviii): the elementary matrices in $M_{n\times n}(K)$ form a group under multiplication.
    Answer

    False (for $n\ge2$): not closed, $Q_{1,2}(1)Q_{2,1}(1)=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ is not elementary (Proof D, Rung 2).

  3. HS17, (xix): $EA=AE$ for all $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ and all elementary $E$.
    Answer

    False. $P_{1,2}A$ swaps rows, $AP_{1,2}$ swaps columns. For $A=E_{11}\in M_{2\times2}$: $P_{1,2}E_{11}=E_{21}\ne E_{12}=E_{11}P_{1,2}$.

  4. FS19, (VI): a homogeneous system of $n$ equations in $m$ unknowns certainly has a nonzero solution if (a) $n=m$; (b) $n>m$; (c) $n<m$; (d) $n\ne m$.
    Answer

    (c). $\dim\mathrm{Sol}=m-\mathrm{rank}\ge m-n>0$ (Proof A; the rank is at most the number of rows $n$). The others fail for a matrix with independent columns, e.g. $I_m$ with zero rows added.

  5. FS17, (xxii): for $A,B\in M_{n\times n}(K)$, $AB=0\Rightarrow BA=0$.
    Answer

    False. $A=E_{12}$, $B=E_{11}$: $AB=E_{12}E_{11}=0$, but $BA=E_{11}E_{12}=E_{12}\ne0$. (Compare (xxi), $AB=I_n\Rightarrow BA=I_n$: true, lesson 15, Proof D.)

Why it's cool

Every computer that solves a linear system, from weather forecasts to the physics engine of a video game, runs Gaussian elimination, and Proof E is the reason it can store its work so cheaply. The downward steps are lower triangular elementary matrices; their product $L$ and the resulting upper triangular $R$ give $A=LR$ (the LR decomposition, FS18, problem 5, 15 points; Exercise 6). Once $L$ and $R$ are stored, every new right-hand side $b$ costs only two quick substitutions instead of a new elimination. The idea is exactly today's: the stamps are worth keeping.

For wonder: shears, and an area that never changes (optional, 5 min)

Draw the unit square and apply $Q_{1,2}(\lambda)$: it slides the top edge sideways, a shear, like pushing a deck of cards. The square becomes a parallelogram with the same base and the same height, so the same area. $P_{1,2}$ reflects the square in the diagonal: same area, but the orientation flips. $S_1(\mu)$ stretches it by $\mu$: the area is multiplied by $\mu$. By Proof E every invertible map is a chain of these three, so its effect on areas is a product of factors $1$, $-1$ and $\mu$. That product is a single number attached to the matrix, the determinant. Lesson 18 builds it from exactly these three rules.

Exercise 4 (script Lemma 3.6.14, second half: column operations)

Let $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ with columns $a_1,\dots,a_n$. Show: $AQ_{i,j}(\lambda)$ is $A$ after $C_j+\lambda C_i\to C_j$; $AP_{i,j}$ swaps columns $i$ and $j$; $AS_i(\mu)$ multiplies column $i$ by $\mu$.

Hint

Column $k$ of $AE$ is $A$ times column $k$ of $E$, i.e. $\sum_le_{lk}a_l$. Or transpose: $(AE)^T=E^TA^T$, and $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)^T=Q_{j,i}(\lambda)$.

Solution

Frame: column $k$ of a product; compare with $I_n$. (Checked twice.)

Column $k$ of $AE$ is $\sum_le_{lk}a_l$. If column $k$ of $E$ is $e_k$, column $k$ of $AE$ is $a_k$. Column $j$ of $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)=I_n+\lambda E_{ij}$ is $e_j+\lambda e_i$, so column $j$ of the product is $a_j+\lambda a_i$; all other columns are those of $I_n$. Columns $i,j$ of $P_{i,j}$ are $e_j,e_i$, giving $a_j,a_i$. Column $i$ of $S_i(\mu)$ is $\mu e_i$, giving $\mu a_i$. Note the index swap: $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)$ adds $\lambda\cdot$row $j$ to row $i$ from the left, but $\lambda\cdot$column $i$ to column $j$ from the right.

Exercise 5 (script Exercises 3.6.10–3.6.11: the $2\times2$ case)

Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in M_{2\times2}(K)$. Show: $A$ is invertible if and only if $ad-bc\ne0$, and then $A^{-1}=\dfrac1{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$.

Hint

Compute $A\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$. For “⇒”: if $ad-bc=0$, that product is $0$; what does this say about $A$ if the second matrix is not $0$? And if it is $0$?

Solution

Frame: one product, then cases. (Checked twice.)

Let $\tilde A=\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$. Then $A\tilde A=\tilde AA=(ad-bc)I_2$ (multiply out). “⇐”: if $\delta:=ad-bc\ne0$, then $A\cdot\tfrac1\delta\tilde A=\tfrac1\delta\tilde A\cdot A=I_2$, so $A^{-1}=\tfrac1\delta\tilde A$. “⇒”: let $\delta=0$, so $A\tilde A=0$. If $\tilde A\ne0$, some column $v\ne0$ of $\tilde A$ has $Av=0$, so $A$ is not invertible (Proof C (2)). If $\tilde A=0$, then $a=b=c=d=0$ and $A=0$ is not invertible. (b) $Ly=b$: $y_1=3$, $y_2=18-6=12$, $y_3=-1-3+\tfrac13\cdot12=0$. $Rv=y$: $v_3=0$, $6v_2=12$ so $v_2=2$, $v_1=3-4-0=-1$. So $v=(-1,2,0)^T$; check $Av=(-1+4,\,-2+20,\,-1)=(3,18,-1)$ ✓.

Exercise 7 (inverses of triangular matrices)

Let $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ be upper triangular with all diagonal entries nonzero. Show that $A$ is invertible and that $A^{-1}$ is upper triangular.

Hint

Run Gauss–Jordan on $(A\,|\,I_n)$ using only scalings and operations $L_i+\lambda L_j\to L_i$ with $j>i$ (clearing upwards). Which elementary matrices are these, and what shape does their product have?

Solution

Frame: Gauss–Jordan with upward steps only; products of upper triangular matrices are upper triangular (lesson 12). (Checked twice.)

$A$ is already in echelon form with $n$ pivots, so $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$ and $A$ is invertible (Proof C). To reach $I_n$: scale row $i$ by $a_{ii}^{-1}$ ($S_i(a_{ii}^{-1})$, diagonal), then clear the entries above each pivot from the last column to the first with $L_i-\lambda L_j\to L_i$, $j>i$, i.e. $Q_{i,j}(-\lambda)$ with $i<j$, which is upper triangular. No swap is needed, and no step creates an entry below the diagonal. So $A^{-1}=T_k\cdots T_1$ (Proof E) is a product of upper triangular matrices, hence upper triangular.
