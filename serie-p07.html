Serie 7, explained

The Lauberhorn downhill, the conical pendulum from the lecture, a glider on an air-track wagon that turns into a rocket, and the DART probe hitting an asteroid. For every task: the idea in plain words, a sketch, up to three hints that each give away a little more, then the solution with every step explained next to what you write, and the trap people fall into.

How to use this page.

  1. Read the task and try it on paper for 10 minutes. Draw a sketch first: in physics it is half the work.
  2. Stuck on where to start? Read The idea and open You need.
  3. Still stuck? Open Hint 1, try again, then Hint 2, then Hint 3. Hint 3 is almost the whole solution.
  4. Only then open the solution. The left column says why each step is there, the right column is what you write.

The solutions follow the official Lösung (Serie 7, Physik I HS26). The task texts are short English summaries: keep the official sheet next to you for the exact wording. With the StudyHub connector, your own AI can check your attempt before you look.

Aufgabe 1 · The Lauberhorn downhill

In 2026 the Lauberhorn race started at the Hundschopf (about $1970\,$m above sea level). The top speed, $149.65\,$km/h, was measured at the Haneggschuss (about $1590\,$m). Take the distance skied between the two as $1.7\,$km.

  1. What fraction of the potential energy was turned into kinetic energy?
  2. If all the rest went into friction work, what effective friction coefficient would that need? (Use an average slope angle.)
  3. Ski on snow has a friction coefficient of about $0.03$–$0.06$. How do you explain the difference?
The idea

Energy bookkeeping. Going down $380\,$m releases $mg\Delta h$. Only part of it shows up as speed; the rest was taken by friction along the way. Treat the whole run as one straight slope: then the friction force is constant, $\mu mg\cos\alpha$, and friction work is that force times the $1.7\,$km. The mass cancels everywhere, so you never need Odermatt's weight. Part c) asks you to think like a physicist: what else, apart from ski-on-snow friction, eats energy at $150\,$km/h?

The model: one straight slope. Only $s$ and $\Delta h$ are given; the horizontal side follows from Pythagoras, and $\cos\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{s^2-\Delta h^2}}{s}$.

You need

$E_{\text{kin}}=\frac12mv^2$, $E_{\text{pot}}=mg\Delta h$. Friction work $W=F_Rs$ with $F_R=\mu F_N=\mu mg\cos\alpha$ on a slope (Physics lesson 3, lesson 6). Convert: $149.65\,$km/h $=41.57\,$m/s (divide by $3.6$).

Hint 1

a) The fraction is $\frac{\Delta E_{\text{kin}}}{\Delta E_{\text{pot}}}=\frac{\frac12mv^2}{mg\Delta h}$. The mass cancels.

Hint 2

b) The lost energy is $mg\Delta h-\frac12mv^2$, and it equals the friction work $\mu mg\cos\alpha\cdot s$. You don't know $\alpha$, but you know $\sin\alpha=\frac{\Delta h}s$, so $\cos\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{s^2-\Delta h^2}}s$.

Hint 3

Then $s\cos\alpha=\sqrt{s^2-\Delta h^2}$ and $$\mu=\frac{g\Delta h-\frac12v^2}{g\sqrt{s^2-\Delta h^2}}.$$ c) Think of the air at $150\,$km/h, of the turns, and of the jumps.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Write both energies; the mass cancels in the ratio.

$\Delta E_{\text{kin}}=\frac12mv^2$, $\ \Delta E_{\text{pot}}=mg\Delta h$, $$\begin{aligned}\frac{\Delta E_{\text{kin}}}{\Delta E_{\text{pot}}}&=\frac{v^2}{2g\Delta h}=\frac{41.57^2}{2\cdot9.81\cdot380}\\&\approx0.232=23.2\,\%.\end{aligned}$$

b) Everything that didn't become speed went into friction work along the run.

$W=\Delta E_{\text{pot}}-\Delta E_{\text{kin}}=F_R\,s$.

On a slope the normal force is only the part of gravity perpendicular to the slope.

$F_R=\mu F_N=\mu mg\cos\alpha$.

The angle comes from the triangle: we know the slope length and the height drop.

$\cos\alpha=\dfrac{\sqrt{s^2-\Delta h^2}}{s}$, so $F_R\,s=\mu mg\sqrt{s^2-\Delta h^2}$.

Set the two expressions for the work equal and solve for $\mu$; $m$ cancels.

$$\begin{aligned}\mu&=\frac{g\Delta h-\frac12v^2}{g\sqrt{s^2-\Delta h^2}}\\&=\frac{3728-864}{9.81\cdot1657}\approx0.18.\end{aligned}$$

c) $0.18$ is three to six times the ski-on-snow value, so other losses must be at work.

  • Air drag is far from negligible at $150\,$km/h.
  • Turns brake: not the circular motion itself, but the large force pressing the skis into the snow deforms it and throws snow aside.
  • Jumps end in inelastic landings.

Sense check. With no losses at all, $380\,$m of drop would give $v=\sqrt{2g\Delta h}\approx86\,$m/s $\approx310\,$km/h. The real top speed is about half of that, and since energy goes with $v^2$, a quarter of the energy is left: $23\,\%$ fits.

Trap

Using $F_N=mg$ instead of $mg\cos\alpha$, or using $1.7\,$km as the horizontal distance. Here the effect is small (the slope is gentle, $\cos\alpha\approx0.97$), but on an exam with a steep slope it costs the whole result. Also: convert km/h to m/s before squaring.

Aufgabe 2 · The conical pendulum

In the lecture, four balls hung on wires of different lengths from a rotating pole. Above a certain rotation speed, all four balls were at the same height. This task explains why.

  1. Sketch the forces on one ball: include the angle $\alpha$, the radius $r$ of the circle, the wire force $\vec F_s$, gravity $\vec F_g$ and the resultant force $\vec F$.
  2. What must hold for $\vec F_s$ and $\vec F_g$ so that the ball moves on a steady circle with angular velocity $\omega$?
  3. Find the height $h$ (how far the ball hangs below the suspension point) as a function of $\omega$ and whatever else you need. What happens for $\omega\to0$?
The idea

Only two forces act on the ball: the wire pulls along the wire, gravity pulls down. Split the wire force into an up part and an inward part. Up part: it must cancel gravity, since the ball neither rises nor falls. Inward part: it is the only horizontal force, so it is the centripetal force $mr\omega^2$. Dividing the two equations removes the unknown wire force, and the geometry gives $h=r\tan\alpha$. The surprise: $h=\frac g{\omega^2}$ contains neither the mass nor the wire length. That is why all four balls end up level.

The wire force's vertical part (dotted) exactly cancels $\vec F_g$; what is left, $\vec F$, points horizontally to the axis and keeps the ball on its circle. $\alpha$ is measured from the horizontal, so $h=r\tan\alpha$.

You need

Uniform circular motion needs a net force $mr\omega^2$ towards the centre (Physics lesson 1). Free-body diagram and splitting a force into components (Physics lesson 3).

Hint 1

a) Two forces only. The “centripetal force” is not a third force: it is the name of the resultant $\vec F=\vec F_s+\vec F_g$, and it must point to the axis.

Hint 2

b) With $\alpha$ measured from the horizontal: vertical part of $\vec F_s$ is $F_s\sin\alpha$, horizontal part is $F_s\cos\alpha$. Vertical: balance. Horizontal: $mr\omega^2$.

Hint 3

c) Divide: $\tan\alpha=\frac{F_s\sin\alpha}{F_s\cos\alpha}=\frac{mg}{mr\omega^2}$. Combine with $h=r\tan\alpha$. For $\omega\to0$, ask what $r$ does.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) The sketch above: wire force along the wire towards the suspension point, gravity down, resultant horizontal towards the axis.

$\vec F=\vec F_s+\vec F_g$, horizontal, pointing to the axis.

b) The ball stays at the same height: the vertical parts cancel.

$F_{s,y}=F_{g}$: $\quad F_s\sin\alpha=mg$.

The horizontal part of the wire force is the only horizontal force, so it is what keeps the ball on its circle.

$F_{s,x}=F=F_Z$: $\quad F_s\cos\alpha=mr\omega^2$.

c) Geometry of the triangle suspension point, axis, ball.

$h=r\tan\alpha$.

For $r\neq0$: divide the two equations from b). The unknown $F_s$ and the mass cancel.

$$\begin{aligned}\frac hr=\tan\alpha&=\frac{F_s\sin\alpha}{F_s\cos\alpha}=\frac{mg}{mr\omega^2}\\ \Rightarrow\ h&=\frac g{\omega^2}.\end{aligned}$$

Neither $m$ nor the wire length $l$ appears: every ball on the pole hangs the same $h$ below the suspension point. That is the lecture demo.

$h$ is independent of $m$ and of $l$.

But $h$ can't be larger than the wire. If $\frac g{\omega^2}>l$, the ball can't swing out: $r=0$ and it hangs straight down.

$$h=\begin{cases}l, & \omega<\sqrt{g/l},\\[2pt] \dfrac g{\omega^2}, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

$\omega\to0$: eventually $\omega<\sqrt{g/l}$.

For $\omega\to0$ the ball hangs straight down: $h=l$, $r=0$.

Sense check. One turn per second, $\omega=2\pi\,$s⁻¹: $h=\frac{9.81}{39.5}\,$m $\approx25\,$cm. Faster turning makes $h$ smaller, so the balls fly out more, as you would expect. And the threshold $\omega=\sqrt{g/l}$ is the angular frequency of a simple pendulum of length $l$: a longer wire starts swinging out at a lower speed.

Trap

Drawing the centripetal force as an extra arrow next to $\vec F_s$ and $\vec F_g$. It is not a force of its own; it is what the real forces add up to. Second trap: mixing up the angle. If you measure $\alpha$ from the vertical (as many textbooks do), sine and cosine swap and $h=\frac r{\tan\alpha}$. Say which angle you use, and check with a limit: a horizontal wire can never balance gravity.

Aufgabe 3 · Air-track wagon (old exam problem)

A glider of mass $m$ sits on a wagon of mass $M$, at height $h$ above the air track. Both move without friction. At first everything is at rest; then the glider slides down the wagon's curved side. Afterwards the wagon moves left (speed $v$) and the glider right (speed $u$).

  1. Which quantities are conserved while the glider slides down? Use them to write equations for $v$ and $u$.
  2. Find $v$ and $u$ in terms of $m$, $M$, $g$ and $h$.

From now on, $M=m$.

  1. Find $v$ and $u$ in this case.
  2. Split the glider into two gliders of mass $m/2$ that slide down one after the other (the second starts once the first has left). Find $v$ after both have left.
  3. Split it into $N$ pieces of mass $m/N$. For $N\to\infty$ this is a rocket. Find $v$ after all pieces have left.
The idea

No friction and no horizontal outside force: horizontal momentum is conserved (it starts at zero), and so is energy (height energy $mgh$ becomes kinetic energy of both). Two unknowns, two equations. Parts d) and e) repeat the same game in steps: after each piece leaves, the wagon moves at constant speed, and in the wagon's frame the next piece is the same problem again with a smaller mass. In the limit of many tiny pieces you get the rocket equation from the lecture. The twist at the end: splitting does not help here, unlike for a real rocket.

Before: all at rest, the glider $h$ above the track. After: wagon left with $v$, glider right with $u$ (both speeds positive numbers).

You need

Momentum conservation without outside horizontal forces, energy conservation without friction, and the rocket equation $M\frac{dv}{dt}=u\frac{dm}{dt}$, which integrates to $v_{\text{end}}=v_0+u\ln\frac{M_0}{M_0-m_g}$ (script equations 3.49 and 3.53; Physics lesson 2, lesson 6).

Hint 1

a) Energy: $mgh=\frac12Mv^2+\frac12mu^2$. Momentum (only horizontal, right positive): $0=-Mv+mu$. b) Get $v$ from the momentum equation and put it into the energy equation.

Hint 2

d) First glider: use b) with glider mass $\frac m2$ and wagon mass $M+\frac m2=\frac{3m}2$ (the second glider still rides on the wagon). Second glider: in the frame of the wagon (which now moves at constant $v_1$), it's b) again with glider $\frac m2$, wagon $m$. Speeds add: $v_2=v_1+\Delta v$.

Hint 3

e) Each tiny piece falls $h$ in the wagon's frame and takes almost all of that energy (the wagon is much heavier than one piece): $\frac mNgh=\frac12\frac mN u^2$, so it leaves at $u=\sqrt{2gh}$ relative to the wagon. That is the exhaust speed. Then use the rocket equation with $M_0=2m$, $m_g=m$, $v_0=0$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) No friction, so mechanical energy is conserved. No outside horizontal force, so horizontal momentum is conserved (and it starts at $0$).

Energy: $mgh=\frac12Mv^2+\frac12mu^2$.
Momentum: $0=-Mv+mu$.

b) The momentum equation gives $v$ in terms of $u$.

$v=\dfrac mM\,u$.

Put it into the energy equation.

$$\begin{aligned}mgh&=\frac M2\Big(\frac mM\Big)^2u^2+\frac m2u^2\\&=\frac m2u^2\Big(1+\frac mM\Big).\end{aligned}$$

Solve for $u$, then $v=\frac mMu$.

$$\begin{aligned}u&=\sqrt{2gh}\,\frac1{\sqrt{1+m/M}},\\ v&=\sqrt{2gh}\,\frac{m/M}{\sqrt{1+m/M}}.\end{aligned}$$

c) Put $m/M=1$.

$u=v=\sqrt{2gh}\,\frac1{\sqrt2}=\sqrt{gh}$.

d) First glider ($\frac m2$) leaves; the wagon still carries the second glider, so it has mass $\frac{3m}2$. Ratio $\frac{m/2}{3m/2}=\frac13$.

$$v_1=\sqrt{2gh}\,\frac{1/3}{\sqrt{4/3}}=\sqrt{\tfrac16\,gh}.$$

The wagon now moves at constant $v_1$: in its frame the second glider is the same problem with ratio $\frac{m/2}{m}=\frac12$. Add the speeds.

$$\begin{aligned}v_2&=\sqrt{2gh}\,\frac{1/2}{\sqrt{3/2}}+v_1\\&=\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac13}+\sqrt{\tfrac16}\Big)\sqrt{gh}\\&\approx0.986\,\sqrt{gh}.\end{aligned}$$

e) Many tiny pieces: a rocket. Momentum conservation for one small piece $dm$ leaving at speed $u$ relative to the wagon (script eq. 3.49).

$M\dfrac{dv}{dt}=u\dfrac{dm}{dt}$.

Integrate from the full mass $M_0$ to the empty mass $M_0-m_g$ (script eq. 3.53).

$v_{\text{end}}=u\ln\Big(\dfrac{M_0}{M_0-m_g}\Big)+v_0$.

The exhaust speed: each piece turns its height energy into its own kinetic energy (the wagon's share is negligible for large $N$).

$\dfrac mN\,gh=\dfrac12\,\dfrac mN\,u^2\ \Rightarrow\ u=\sqrt{2gh}$.

Here $M_0=2m$ (wagon plus all pieces), $m_g=m$, $v_0=0$.

$$\begin{aligned}v_{\text{end}}&=\sqrt{2gh}\,\ln2\\&\approx0.980\,\sqrt{gh}.\end{aligned}$$

Why splitting doesn't help here. The results go $1$, $0.986$, $0.980$ (times $\sqrt{gh}$): more pieces, slightly slower wagon. For a real rocket, the exhaust speed $u$ is fixed, and using it in many small portions beats one big push. Here, what is fixed is the energy per kilogram, $gh$. In one big push the wagon recoils strongly and the glider leaves at $2\sqrt{gh}$ relative to it; tiny pieces leave at only $\sqrt{2gh}\approx1.41\sqrt{gh}$ relative to the wagon. Lower exhaust speed, slightly less push.

Trap

Writing momentum conservation with both speeds positive: $0=Mv+mu$ would give a negative speed. Pick a direction as positive and give the wagon a minus sign. In d), forgetting that the second glider is still on the wagon when the first one leaves (wagon mass $\frac{3m}2$, not $m$). In e), using the speed relative to the ground in the rocket equation: $u$ is always relative to the wagon.

Aufgabe 4 · The DART mission

On 26 September 2022 the DART probe hit the asteroid Dimorphos, which circles the much bigger asteroid Didymos. The change in its orbit can be seen from Earth.

  1. In the rest frame of Didymos, treat Dimorphos' orbit as a circle. Derive Kepler's third law (orbit radius vs period) from the forces on Dimorphos.
  2. Before the impact, Dimorphos circled Didymos at $1.15\,$km distance once every $11\,$h $55\,$min. Find its tangential speed and the mass of Didymos.
  3. Assume a perfectly inelastic collision. Just before impact the probe ($570\,$kg) had $v_x=-6.1\,$km/s in Didymos' frame; Dimorphos has $m_D=5\cdot10^9\,$kg. Find the tangential speed of asteroid plus probe after the impact, and the change in speed.
  4. The measured change is much bigger than c). NASA credits the rock thrown off the asteroid. Explain with momentum why this helps slow the asteroid down.
The idea

a)–b) are the circular-orbit recipe: gravity is the centripetal force. From the period you get the speed and, with Kepler's law, even the mass of the central body, without ever touching it. c) is a perfectly inelastic collision: add up momenta. The probe is tiny compared with the asteroid, so expect a tiny change. d) is the clever part: rock flung back out of the crater carries momentum away, and by momentum conservation the asteroid gets an extra push in the opposite direction, like a rocket firing forwards to brake.

Not to scale. The probe hits head-on, against Dimorphos' direction of motion; the ejecta leave the crater forwards, in Dimorphos' direction of motion.

You need

Circular orbit: $m\omega^2r=\frac{GMm}{r^2}$ with $\omega=\frac{2\pi}T$; $v=\omega r$ (Physics lesson 9). Perfectly inelastic collision: total momentum before $=$ after (Physics lesson 8). $G=6.674\cdot10^{-11}\,$m³/(kg s²).

Hint 1

a) Set centripetal force equal to gravity, cancel Dimorphos' mass, replace $\omega$ by $\frac{2\pi}T$. b) $T=11\,$h $55\,$min $=42\,900\,$s.

Hint 2

b) $v=\frac{2\pi r}T$, and from a): $M=\frac{4\pi^2r^3}{GT^2}$. c) Momentum along the direction of motion, with the probe's velocity negative: $m_Sv_x+m_Dv_1=(m_D+m_S)\,v_{\text{after}}$.

Hint 3

c) Because $v_1\ll|v_x|$, the change is about $\Delta v\approx\frac{m_S\,|v_x|}{m_D}$. d) In which direction do the ejecta fly, and what does that mean for the momentum left on the asteroid?

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) In Didymos' frame, gravity is the only force on Dimorphos and must supply the centripetal force ($m_2$: Dimorphos, $M$: Didymos, $r_1$: distance).

$F_Z=F_G$: $\quad m_2\omega^2r_1=\dfrac{m_2MG}{r_1^2}$.

Cancel $m_2$ and use $\omega=2\pi f=\frac{2\pi}T$.

$$\frac{4\pi^2r_1^3}{T^2}=MG\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{r_1^3}{T^2}=\frac{MG}{4\pi^2}.$$ This is Kepler's third law: $r^3/T^2$ is the same for everything orbiting the same body.

b) Speed on a circle: circumference over period.

$$\begin{aligned}v_1=\omega r_1&=\frac{2\pi}{42\,900\,\text s}\cdot1150\,\text m\\&\approx0.168\,\tfrac{\text m}{\text s}.\end{aligned}$$

Solve Kepler's law for the mass.

$$M=\frac{4\pi^2r_1^3}{T_1^2G}\approx4.89\cdot10^{11}\,\text{kg}.$$

c) Perfectly inelastic: probe and asteroid move on together, and total momentum is conserved. The probe's velocity is negative (head-on).

$$\begin{aligned}&v_x\,m_{\text{S}}+v_1\,m_2\\&\quad=v_{\text{after}}\,(m_2+m_{\text{S}}).\end{aligned}$$

Numbers.

$$\begin{aligned}v_{\text{after}}&=\frac{m_{\text S}v_x+m_2v_1}{m_2+m_{\text S}}\\&=\frac{-3.48\cdot10^6+8.40\cdot10^8}{5.0000001\cdot10^9}\\&\approx0.167\,\tfrac{\text m}{\text s}\end{aligned}$$ (numbers in SI units: $-6100\cdot570$ for the probe, $0.168\cdot5\cdot10^9$ for Dimorphos).

The difference is tiny; compute it directly to see its size.

$$\begin{aligned}\Delta v&\approx\frac{m_{\text S}\,|v_x|}{m_2}=\frac{570\cdot6100}{5\cdot10^9}\,\tfrac{\text m}{\text s}\\&\approx0.7\,\tfrac{\text{mm}}{\text s}.\end{aligned}$$ The official solution rounds this to $0.001\,$m/s.

d) The ejecta fly out of the crater, back towards where the probe came from: that is Dimorphos' direction of motion.

The ejected rock carries momentum in the direction of flight away from the asteroid. By momentum conservation, that momentum is now missing from the asteroid: it is slowed down further, on top of the probe's own impact.

Sense check. $0.7\,$mm/s out of $168\,$mm/s is a change of $0.4\,\%$. That sounds like nothing, but the period changes by a similar fraction, and over $11.9\,$h that is minutes, which telescopes on Earth can measure. NASA measured a period change of about $32\,$minutes, several times what c) predicts: that is the ejecta effect from d).

Trap

Forgetting the sign of the probe's velocity: with $+6.1\,$km/s the asteroid would speed up. In a), using the radius of Didymos instead of the distance between the two bodies. And in b), forgetting to convert the period to seconds ($11\,$h $55\,$min $=42\,900\,$s, not $11.55\,$h).
