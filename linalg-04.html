StudyHub · Linear Algebra I · Lesson 4

The clerk's first verdicts: proofs from the axioms

Halbeisen's §2 proves three small theorems that are classic exam material: a left neutral element and left inverses already make a group (the lecture's Satz 2.0), fields have no zero divisors (Lemma 1.3.10), and in every ring $0\cdot x=0$, $(-1)\cdot x=-x$ and $(-x)(-y)=xy$ (Satz 2.2). Each proof is a short chain where every step is one axiom.

🌧
Bad day? Read the story in section 1 and answer the recap questions at the top. That counts. Tomorrow, simply carry on from here: a missed or light day is not a restart.
Covers
Script EN 2023, §1.3 (pp. 17–20): Lemma 1.3.4 (multiplication by $0$), Lemma 1.3.10 (properties of a field) and Exercise 1.3.17 (one-sided inverses), plus the lecture's §2 “Einfache Beweise” (Halbeisen's notes, pp. 11–13): Satz 2.0, the inverse of the inverse, the notation $-x$, $x^{-1}$, $x-y$, $x:y$, the non-example $a*b:=a$.
Time
About 70 minutes.
You're done when
you can (1) prove Satz 2.0 (a) from the exam box, (2) prove $0\cdot x=0$, $(-1)\cdot x=-x$, $(-x)(-y)=xy$ in a ring, (3) explain why $a*b:=a$ is not a group, and (4) use Lemma 1.3.10 (c) to show a ring is no field.
Script
Main: Script EN 2023, §1.3 (pp. 17–20). Lecture: Halbeisen's notes W3a, pages 11–13, where these results are Satz 2.0, Satz 2.1 and Satz 2.2.
Why it matters for the exam

The exam comes from the lecture, and these are the first full proofs in them: expect “prove that in a group every left inverse is a right inverse” or “prove $0\cdot x=0$ from the ring axioms”. Old exams ask the consequences as true/false: “In every group, $b=c\iff a\circ b=a\circ c$” (FS17) is Exercise 1. And Lemma 2.0.2 in lesson 5 (rules for vector spaces) is proved exactly like the ring rules here.

Recap of lesson 3 (5 min)

A proof is a finite chain of statements, each an axiom or following from earlier ones by a rule (modus ponens, contrapositive, contradiction). Check: in Proof D of lesson 3, why could we not simply divide by $\bar a^s$?

Answer

Because we didn't know yet that inverses exist in $\mathbb Z_p$; that is what was being proved. We used “no zero divisors mod a prime” instead.

The story: the clerk's first verdicts

The Group guild's charter is long: brackets don't matter, a quiet member exists on both sides, and every member can be undone from both sides. A thrifty clerk notices they only ever need half of it: brackets, a quiet member on the left, and an undoer on the left. The other half follows. Then they turn to the great houses and shows that some rules people think are conventions, like “minus times minus is plus”, are forced by the charter.

1. Three axioms are enough for a group

The script defines a group with four two-sided axioms (lesson 1). The lecture shows that half of them suffice. The script has no lemma for this, but its Exercise 1.3.17 (p. 20) asks for the key step: a one-sided inverse is also an inverse from the other side.

Satz 2.0 (lecture, Halbeisen's notes p. 11). Let $M$ be a set with a binary operation $\circ$ and let $e\in M$. Then $(M,\circ,e)$ is a group if and only if it satisfies the three group axioms:

  • $G_0$: $\forall x\forall y\forall z\ \big(x\circ(y\circ z)=(x\circ y)\circ z\big)$ ($\circ$ is associative)
  • $G_1$: $\forall x\ (e\circ x=x)$ ($e$ is a left neutral element)
  • $G_2$: $\forall x\ \exists\bar x\ (\bar x\circ x=e)$ (every $x$ has a left inverse)

The idea. “$\Rightarrow$” is immediate: a group has an associative operation, a neutral element and inverses. For “$\Leftarrow$” we must show four things: (a) every left inverse is also a right inverse; (b) $e$ is also right neutral; (c) the neutral element is unique; (d) inverses are unique. The trick in (a): multiply by the left inverse of the left inverse, $\bar{\bar x}$.

Proof A: every left inverse is a right inverse (Satz 2.0 (a))

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Given: $G_0$, $G_1$, $G_2$; a left inverse $\bar x$ of $x$, i.e. $\bar x\circ x=e$.
  • To show: $x\circ\bar x=e$.
  • The gap: we may only cancel from the left, and only with left inverses. So we need an equation of the form $\bar x\circ(\text{something})=\bar x$ and a left inverse of $\bar x$.
The storyWhat you write

The undoer $\bar x$ itself has an undoer, $\bar{\bar x}$ (the charter guarantees one for everybody).

Let $\bar{\bar x}$ be a left inverse of $\bar x$ ($G_2$): $\bar{\bar x}\circ\bar x=e$.

Undoing $x$ and then letting the quiet member act on $\bar x$ changes nothing.

$(\bar x\circ x)\circ\bar x=e\circ\bar x=\bar x$ (by $\bar x\circ x=e$ and $G_1$), so by $G_0$: $\bar x\circ(x\circ\bar x)=\bar x$.

Hit both sides from the left with $\bar{\bar x}$. On the right, $\bar{\bar x}$ undoes $\bar x$.

$\bar{\bar x}\circ\big(\bar x\circ(x\circ\bar x)\big)=\bar{\bar x}\circ\bar x=e$.

On the left, rebracket: $\bar{\bar x}$ undoes $\bar x$ there too, and the quiet member leaves $x\circ\bar x$ alone. So $x\circ\bar x$ is the quiet member.

By $G_0$ the left side is $(\bar{\bar x}\circ\bar x)\circ(x\circ\bar x)=e\circ(x\circ\bar x)=x\circ\bar x$ (by $G_1$). Hence $x\circ\bar x=e$. ∎

The proof in one piece

Let $\bar x\circ x=e$ and let $\bar{\bar x}$ be a left inverse of $\bar x$. By $G_1$, $(\bar x\circ x)\circ\bar x=e\circ\bar x=\bar x$, so by $G_0$, $\bar x\circ(x\circ\bar x)=\bar x$. Multiplying from the left by $\bar{\bar x}$: $\bar{\bar x}\circ(\bar x\circ(x\circ\bar x))=\bar{\bar x}\circ\bar x=e$. By $G_0$ and $G_1$ the left side equals $(\bar{\bar x}\circ\bar x)\circ(x\circ\bar x)=e\circ(x\circ\bar x)=x\circ\bar x$. So $x\circ\bar x=e$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1. Which equation do you multiply by $\bar{\bar x}$, and from which side?

    Answer

    $\bar x\circ(x\circ\bar x)=\bar x$, from the left.

  2. Rung 2. Write the two lines that compute the left side after multiplying.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof A from the frame alone.

How to reproduce Satz 2.0 (a) under exam pressure
  1. Write $\bar x=e\circ\bar x=(\bar x\circ x)\circ\bar x=\bar x\circ(x\circ\bar x)$.
  2. Multiply from the left by $\bar{\bar x}$ (a left inverse of $\bar x$).
  3. Right side: $\bar{\bar x}\circ\bar x=e$.
  4. Left side: rebracket to $(\bar{\bar x}\circ\bar x)\circ(x\circ\bar x)=x\circ\bar x$.

In short: build $\bar x=\bar x(x\bar x)$, then cancel $\bar x$ with its own left inverse.

Proof B: the rest of Satz 2.0, parts (b), (c), (d)

The idea. Now that left inverses work on both sides, each remaining part is a one-line chain.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (b) To show $x\circ e=x$: write $e=\bar x\circ x$ and rebracket. (c) Two neutral elements $e,e'$: compute $e\circ e'$. (d) Two inverses $\bar x,\tilde x$: sneak in $e$.
The storyWhat you write

The quiet member is quiet on the right too: replace it by “undo then do”.

(b) $x\circ e=x\circ(\bar x\circ x)=(x\circ\bar x)\circ x=e\circ x=x$, by $G_2$, $G_0$, (a), $G_1$.

Two quiet members performing together give each of them (lesson 1, Proof A).

(c) If $e,e'$ are neutral: $e\circ e'=e'$ ($e$ left neutral) and $e\circ e'=e$ ($e'$ right neutral), so $e=e'$.

Two undoers of the same member are the same undoer (lesson 1, Proof B).

(d) If $\bar x,\tilde x$ are inverses of $x$: $\bar x=\bar x\circ e=\bar x\circ(x\circ\tilde x)=(\bar x\circ x)\circ\tilde x=e\circ\tilde x=\tilde x$. ∎

The proof in one piece

(b) $x\circ e=x\circ(\bar x\circ x)=(x\circ\bar x)\circ x=e\circ x=x$. (c) For neutral $e,e'$: $e=e\circ e'=e'$. (d) For inverses $\bar x,\tilde x$ of $x$: $\bar x=\bar x\circ(x\circ\tilde x)=(\bar x\circ x)\circ\tilde x=\tilde x$. Together with (a), $(M,\circ,e)$ is a group. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1. In (b), where is part (a) used?

    Answer

    In $(x\circ\bar x)\circ x=e\circ x$: we need $x\circ\bar x=e$, i.e. that the left inverse is also a right inverse.

  2. Rung 2. Write (b), (c), (d) from the frame alone.

2. The inverse of the inverse, and notation

Claim (p. 12). If $(M,\circ)$ is a group, $\bar x$ the inverse of $x$, and $\bar{\bar x}$ the inverse of $\bar x$, then $\bar{\bar x}=x$.

Proof. By associativity the same element can be bracketed in two ways: $(\bar{\bar x}\circ\bar x)\circ x=e\circ x=x$ and $\bar{\bar x}\circ(\bar x\circ x)=\bar{\bar x}\circ e=\bar{\bar x}$. So $x=\bar{\bar x}$. ∎

Notation (p. 12). If the group operation is written as addition $+$, the inverse of $x$ is written $-x$; in multiplicative groups it is written $x^{-1}$. By the claim, $-(-x)=x$ and $(x^{-1})^{-1}=x$. In additive groups $x-y:=x+(-y)$; in multiplicative groups $x:y:=x\cdot y^{-1}$.

3. A warning non-example: the sides must match (p. 12)

Let $M$ be a nonempty set and $a*b:=a$ for all $a,b\in M$. Then $*$ is associative ($(a*b)*c=a=a*(b*c)$), every element is right neutral ($x*e=x$), and every element has a left inverse with respect to any fixed $e$ (take $\bar x=e$: $e*x=e$). But for $|M|\ge2$, $(M,*)$ is no group: in a group the neutral element is unique, and here every element is right neutral.

Lesson: Satz 2.0 needs the neutral element and the inverses on the same side. “Right neutral + left inverses” is not enough.

4. Fields: $0\ne1$ and no zero divisors (Lemma 1.3.10)

Lemma 1.3.10 (a), (c) (Script EN 2023, p. 19). In every field $(K,+,\cdot,0,1)$: (a) $0\ne1$; (c) $\forall x\forall y\ \big(x\cdot y=0\to(x=0\vee y=0)\big)$.

Proof C: Lemma 1.3.10 (a), (c)

The idea. Both parts are hidden inside the demand “$(K\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is a group with neutral element $1$”: a group contains its neutral element, and a group is closed under its operation.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (a) By contradiction: assume $0=1$. (c) By contrapositive: assume $x\ne0$ and $y\ne0$, show $x\cdot y\ne0$.
The storyWhat you write

The multiplication guild of a field consists of everyone except zero, and its quiet member is $1$. If $1$ were $0$, the guild would have expelled its own quiet member.

(a) By definition $(K\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is an abelian group with neutral element $1$. If $0=1$, then $1\notin K\setminus\{0\}$, so it can't be a group with neutral element $1$. Hence $0\ne1$.

Two guild members combine into a guild member, never into the expelled zero.

(c) Contrapositive: let $x\ne0$ and $y\ne0$. Then $x^{-1},y^{-1}$ exist, and $(x\cdot y)^{-1}=x^{-1}\cdot y^{-1}$ (as $\cdot$ is commutative). So $x\cdot y$ has an inverse, hence $x\cdot y\ne0$ (equivalently: $x,y\in K\setminus\{0\}$ and this group is closed under $\cdot$). ∎

The proof in one piece

(a) $(K\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is a group with neutral element $1$, so $1\in K\setminus\{0\}$, i.e. $0\ne1$. (c) If $x\ne0\ne y$, then $x,y$ lie in the group $(K\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$, which is closed under $\cdot$, so $x\cdot y\in K\setminus\{0\}$, i.e. $x\cdot y\ne0$. By contraposition, $x\cdot y=0\to(x=0\vee y=0)$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1. Which ring has $0=1$? Is it a field?

    Answer

    The zero ring $(\{0\},+,\cdot)$ with $0_R=1_R=0$. Not a field, by (a).

  2. Rung 2. Write (c) as a contrapositive from the frame.

5. Consequences of the distributive laws (Lemma 1.3.4, Lemma 1.3.10 (d))

Ring rules (Script EN 2023: (a) is Lemma 1.3.4, p. 17; (b), (c) are Lemma 1.3.10 (d), p. 19, stated there for fields, but the proof only uses ring axioms). Let $(R,+,\cdot,0,1)$ be a ring. Then: (a) $\forall x\,(0\cdot x=0\wedge x\cdot0=0)$; (b) $\forall x\,\big((-1)\cdot x=-x\wedge x\cdot(-1)=-x\big)$; (c) $\forall x\forall y\,\big((-x)\cdot(-y)=x\cdot y\big)$.

Proof D: the ring rules

The idea. The distributive law is the only axiom connecting $+$ and $\cdot$, so every step must go through it. For (a), write $x=(1+0)\cdot x$ and distribute; for (b), write $0=((-1)+1)\cdot x$; (c) is (b) twice.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (a) To show $0\cdot x=0$, i.e. that $0\cdot x$ is the neutral element of $+$. (b) To show $(-1)\cdot x$ is the additive inverse of $x$: show $(-1)\cdot x+x=0$ and use uniqueness.
  • The gap: where does a “$+$” come from inside a product? From $1=1+0$ or $0=(-1)+1$.
The storyWhat you write

Split $x$ as “$x$ plus nothing” and let the multiplication spread over the plus.

(a) $x=1\cdot x=(1+0)\cdot x=1\cdot x+0\cdot x=x+0\cdot x$.

Undo $x$ on both sides: what remains is “$0\cdot x$ equals nothing”.

Add $-x$ from the left: $0=(-x)+x=(-x)+(x+0\cdot x)=((-x)+x)+0\cdot x=0+0\cdot x=0\cdot x$. Analogously $x\cdot0=0$.

Zero is “minus one plus one”; spread it over $x$.

(b) $0=0\cdot x=((-1)+1)\cdot x=(-1)\cdot x+1\cdot x=(-1)\cdot x+x$. So $(-1)\cdot x$ is an additive inverse of $x$; inverses are unique, so $(-1)\cdot x=-x$. Analogously $x\cdot(-1)=-x$.

Two minus signs: pull both out as $-1$ factors, and $(-1)(-1)$ is the inverse of the inverse of $1$.

(c) $(-x)\cdot(-y)=(x\cdot(-1))\cdot((-1)\cdot y)=x\cdot((-1)\cdot(-1))\cdot y$. By (b), $(-1)\cdot(-1)=-(-1)=1$ (inverse of the inverse, §2). So $(-x)\cdot(-y)=x\cdot1\cdot y=x\cdot y$. ∎

The proof in one piece

(a) $x=(1+0)x=x+0\cdot x$; adding $-x$ gives $0=0\cdot x$; similarly $x\cdot0=0$. (b) $0=0\cdot x=((-1)+1)x=(-1)x+x$, so $(-1)x=-x$ by uniqueness of inverses; similarly $x(-1)=-x$. (c) $(-x)(-y)=x(-1)(-1)y=x\cdot(-(-1))\cdot y=x\cdot1\cdot y=xy$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1. In (a), which axiom justifies $(1+0)\cdot x=1\cdot x+0\cdot x$?

    Answer

    The distributive law $(x+y)\cdot z=x\cdot z+y\cdot z$.

  2. Rung 2. Write (b) from the frame alone.

  3. Rung 3. Write (a), (b) and (c) without looking.

How to reproduce the ring rules under exam pressure
  1. (a) $x=(1+0)x=x+0x$, then add $-x$.
  2. (b) $0=0x=((-1)+1)x=(-1)x+x$, then uniqueness of inverses.
  3. (c) $(-x)(-y)=x(-1)(-1)y$ and $(-1)(-1)=1$.

In short: sneak in $1+0$ or $(-1)+1$, distribute, cancel.

Why it's cool. “Minus times minus is plus” is not a school convention: any number system with the distributive law is forced into it. Lemma 3.0 in lesson 5 repeats this proof word for word for vectors.

Worked examples

Worked example 1: $\mathbb Z_6$ is no field

  1. Find two nonzero elements with product $\bar0$.

    Answer

    $\bar2\cdot\bar3=\bar6=\bar0$.

  2. Which theorem finishes the argument?

    Answer

    Lemma 1.3.10 (c): in a field, $x\cdot y=0$ forces $x=0$ or $y=0$. Here neither holds, so $\mathbb Z_6$ is no field (although it is a commutative ring, Serie 1, Aufgabe 8(b)).

Worked example 2: the inverse of a product

In a group, show $(x\circ y)^{-1}=y^{-1}\circ x^{-1}$.

  1. What must you check, by Satz 2.0?

    Answer

    Only that $y^{-1}\circ x^{-1}$ is a left inverse of $x\circ y$: then by (a) it's an inverse, and by (d) it's the inverse.

  2. Check it.

    Answer

    $(y^{-1}\circ x^{-1})\circ(x\circ y)=y^{-1}\circ(x^{-1}\circ x)\circ y=y^{-1}\circ e\circ y=y^{-1}\circ y=e$, using associativity.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (exam FS17, true/false iv)

True or false: in every group $(G,\circ,e)$, $\forall a,b,c\in G:\ b=c\iff a\circ b=a\circ c$.

Solution

True. “$\Rightarrow$”: equal elements give equal products. “$\Leftarrow$”: multiply $a\circ b=a\circ c$ from the left by $a^{-1}$ and rebracket: $(a^{-1}\circ a)\circ b=(a^{-1}\circ a)\circ c$, so $e\circ b=e\circ c$, so $b=c$ (cancellation law).

Exercise 2 (Satz 2.0 in action)

On $\mathbb R$ define $a\circ b:=a+b-1$. Using Satz 2.0, show $(\mathbb R,\circ)$ is a group. (Find $e$ and left inverses.)

Solution

Frame: check only $G_0$, $G_1$, $G_2$.

$G_0$: $(a\circ b)\circ c=(a+b-1)+c-1=a+b+c-2=a\circ(b\circ c)$. $G_1$: $e=1$, since $1\circ x=1+x-1=x$. $G_2$: for $x$ take $\bar x=2-x$: $\bar x\circ x=2-x+x-1=1=e$. By Satz 2.0, $(\mathbb R,\circ,1)$ is a group (abelian, as $\circ$ is commutative).

Exercise 3 (the ring rules)

In a ring, show $(-x)\cdot y=-(x\cdot y)$.

Solution

By rule (b), $-x=(-1)\cdot x$, so $(-x)\cdot y=((-1)\cdot x)\cdot y=(-1)\cdot(x\cdot y)=-(x\cdot y)$, using associativity of $\cdot$ and rule (b) again.

Exercise 4 (from the non-example)

Let $|M|\ge2$ and $a*b:=a$. Which of $G_0$, $G_1$, $G_2$ fail?

Solution

$G_0$ holds. $G_1$ fails for every candidate $e$: $e*x=e\ne x$ for any $x\ne e$. So there is no left neutral element, and $G_2$ doesn't even make sense. The structure has right neutral elements and “left inverses” only in the twisted sense of §3; Satz 2.0 needs left neutral and left inverses.

What we skipped

Nothing from §2. Non-commutative fields (skew fields) are not in this course. Next: Lesson 5, vector spaces.
