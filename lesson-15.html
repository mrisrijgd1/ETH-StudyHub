Recap of lesson 14 (5 min)

  • A series is its partial sums: $\sum a_k$ converges means $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^na_k$ converges.
  • Term test: if $a_n\not\to0$, the series diverges. The converse is false (harmonic series).
  • Comparison and $p$-test: $0\le a_n\le b_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converges ⇒ $\sum a_n$ converges. $\sum\frac1{n^p}$ converges ⇔ $p>1$.
  • Absolute convergence implies convergence. Leibniz: positive, decreasing, $\to0$ ⇒ the alternating series converges.
  • The tail tends to $0$: if $\sum M_k$ converges, then $$\sum_{k>n}M_k=\sum_{k=1}^\infty M_k-\sum_{k=1}^nM_k\to0.$$You'll need this today.

  1. Does $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k^2+k}$ converge?

    Answer

    Yes: $0<\frac1{k^2+k}\le\frac1{k^2}$ and $\sum\frac1{k^2}$ converges (comparison). Its value is $1$, by telescoping (lesson 14).

  2. Converge or diverge: $\sum\frac{(-1)^k}{k}$, $\sum\frac{(-1)^k k}{k+1}$?

    Answer

    The first converges (Leibniz). The second diverges: its terms don't tend to $0$.

  3. Why is $\sum_{k>n}\frac1{k^2}\to0$ as $n\to\infty$?

    Answer

    It equals $S-s_n$, where $S=\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k^2}$ and $s_n\to S$.

1. The story: the mapmaker's apprentices

The queen wants a perfect map of her coastline. Every night, the mapmaker's apprentices draw a new version, and every version is a little better than the last. Map number $n$ shows the coast as a curve $f_n$; the true coast is $f$.

After a month the apprentices report: “At every village, our maps are eventually right. Name any village, and from some night on, our maps place it within a hair of the truth.” The queen is not satisfied. “Village by village is not good enough. Near the northern cliffs you needed a thousand nights, near the next cliff a million, and on some cliffs you may never finish. I want a night after which the whole map is within a hair of the truth, everywhere at once.”

The first promise is pointwise convergence: for each place $x$ there is a night $N$, and $N$ may depend on $x$. The queen's demand is uniform convergence: one night $N$ that works for all places. Draw a thin ribbon of width one hair around the true coast. Uniform means: from night $N$ on, every map lies entirely inside the ribbon.

Why does the queen care? A sailor following the map along the coast needs the whole map to be right. Good apprentices' maps have no gaps (continuous), and the queen wants the true coast to have no gaps either. With village-by-village maps, a gap can appear in the limit. With ribbon-tight maps, it can't. That is today's first exam theorem.

2. Pointwise convergence, and what goes wrong

Definition 7.40 (p. 313). A sequence of functions on a set $X$ is a sequence $(f_n)_n$ of functions $f_n:X\to\mathbb R$ (or $\mathbb C$). It converges pointwise to $f:X\to\mathbb R$ if $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for every $x\in X$:

$$\begin{aligned}&\forall x\in X\ \ \forall\varepsilon>0\ \ \exists N\in\mathbb N\ \ \forall n\ge N:\\&\quad |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

The function $f$ is the pointwise limit. It is unique, because each limit $\lim_nf_n(x)$ of a number sequence is unique.

The idea in one sentence: freeze $x$, and you have an ordinary sequence of numbers $f_1(x),f_2(x),\dots$. Pointwise convergence asks nothing more than that each of these number sequences converges. Everything from lessons 10–12 applies, one $x$ at a time.

Worked example 1: $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1]$ (Beispiel 7.42, p. 313)

Try each step in your head, then open the answer.

  1. Fix $x\in[0,1)$. What is $\lim_nx^n$?

    Answer

    $0$, since $0\le x<1$ (lesson 10).

  2. And at $x=1$?

    Answer

    $1^n=1$ for all $n$, so the limit is $1$.

  3. Write down the pointwise limit $f$. Is it continuous?

    Answer

    $f(x)=0$ for $x<1$ and $f(1)=1$. It jumps at $x=1$, so it is not continuous, although every $f_n$ is continuous (even a polynomial).

  4. For $\varepsilon=\frac12$ and $x=0.99$: from which $n$ on is $x^n<\frac12$? What happens as $x$ gets closer to $1$?

    Answer

    $0.99^{69}\approx0.4998$, so from $n=69$ on. For $x=0.999$ you need $n\ge693$, for $x=0.9999$ about $6932$. The $N$ grows without bound as $x\to1$: no single $N$ works for all $x$. That's the apprentices near the cliff.

Worked example 2: the tents (Beispiel 7.43, p. 314)

On $[0,1]$ let $f_n$ be the tent with peak $\frac n2$ at $\frac1{2n}$ and base $\left[0,\frac1n\right]$, and $f_n=0$ on $\left[\frac1n,1\right]$. As a formula: $f_n(x)=n^2x$ on $\left[0,\frac1{2n}\right]$, $f_n(x)=n^2\left(\frac1n-x\right)$ on $\left[\frac1{2n},\frac1n\right]$, and $0$ after that.

  1. What is $\int_0^1f_n\,dx$?

    Answer

    The area of a triangle with base $\frac1n$ and height $\frac n2$: $\frac12\cdot\frac1n\cdot\frac n2=\frac14$, for every $n$.

  2. Fix $x>0$. What is $f_n(x)$ once $\frac1n<x$? And $f_n(0)$?

    Answer

    $0$, since $x$ lies to the right of the tent. And $f_n(0)=0$ for all $n$. So $f_n\to0$ pointwise on $[0,1]$.

  3. Compare $\lim_n\int_0^1f_n$ with $\int_0^1\lim_nf_n$.

    Answer

    $\lim_n\int_0^1f_n=\frac14$, but $\int_0^1 0\,dx=0$. Limit and integral cannot be swapped, although all functions involved are continuous.

A third warning (Beispiel 7.44, p. 314–315). List the rationals in $[0,1]$ as $q_1,q_2,\dots$ (they are countable, lesson 3), and let $f_n=1$ at $q_1,\dots,q_n$ and $0$ elsewhere. Each $f_n$ is integrable with integral $0$ (finitely many jumps). But the pointwise limit is the Dirichlet function $1_{\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]}$, which is not integrable (lesson 9).

Summary: pointwise limits can destroy continuity (Worked example 1), can change the integral (Worked example 2), and can destroy integrability altogether (the third warning). We need a stronger notion.

3. Uniform convergence

Definition 7.45 (p. 315). $(f_n)_n$ converges uniformly to $f$ on $X$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N\in\mathbb N$ such that $|f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$ and all $x\in X$:

$$\begin{aligned}&\forall\varepsilon>0\ \ \exists N\in\mathbb N\ \ \forall n\ge N\ \ \forall x\in X:\\&\quad |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

The idea. Compare the two lines of quantifiers. The only change is where “$\forall x$” stands:

OrderWhat $N$ may depend on
pointwise$\forall x\ \forall\varepsilon\ \exists N\ \forall n\ge N$$\varepsilon$ and $x$
uniform$\forall\varepsilon\ \exists N\ \forall n\ge N\ \forall x$only $\varepsilon$

It's the same pattern as continuity versus uniform continuity in lesson 8, where $\delta$ was allowed to depend on the point or not. “Uniform” always means: one number for all points. In Worked example 1, the $N$ for $\varepsilon=\frac12$ grew without bound as $x\to1$, so $x^n$ does not converge uniformly on $[0,1]$.

The picture: the $\varepsilon$-tube. For real functions, $|f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ means $f(x)-\varepsilon<f_n(x)<f(x)+\varepsilon$. So uniform convergence says: for every $\varepsilon>0$, from some $N$ on, the whole graph of $f_n$ lies in the band between $f-\varepsilon$ and $f+\varepsilon$.

Uniform implies pointwise (Übung 7.46 (ii)): if one $N$ works for all $x$, it works for each $x$. So a uniform limit is always the pointwise limit, and in exams you always start by computing the pointwise limit $f$. It is the only candidate.

Proof A: the sup test (Übung 7.46 (i), p. 315)

The sup test. Let $d_n=\sup_{x\in X}|f_n(x)-f(x)|$ (a number $\ge0$, or $+\infty$). Then

$$f_n\to f\ \text{uniformly on }X\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d_n\to0.$$

In words: the largest distance between the graphs of $f_n$ and $f$ tends to $0$. (The script also writes $d_n=\|f_n-f\|_\infty$, the supremum norm.)

The idea. “$|f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x$” says that $\varepsilon$ is an upper bound of all the distances, so the supremum is at most $\varepsilon$. Conversely, each single distance is at most the supremum. The only care: “$\sup\le\varepsilon$” is not “$\sup<\varepsilon$”, so aim for $\frac\varepsilon2$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • “⇒” starts with “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” and must end with “$d_n<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.”
  • “⇐” starts with “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” and must end with “$|f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$ and all $x\in X$.”
  • The gap: translate between “for all $x$” and “the supremum”.
The storyWhat you write

If every map from night $N$ on lies inside a ribbon of half a hair, then its worst error is at most half a hair.

“⇒”: Let $\varepsilon>0$. By uniform convergence (with $\frac\varepsilon2$) there is $N$ with $|f_n(x)-f(x)|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $n\ge N$ and $x\in X$. So $\frac\varepsilon2$ is an upper bound of $\{|f_n(x)-f(x)|:x\in X\}$, and $d_n\le\frac\varepsilon2<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. Since $d_n\ge0$, this means $d_n\to0$.

If the worst error is below a hair, every single error is below a hair.

“⇐”: Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $d_n\to0$, there is $N$ with $d_n<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. Then for all $n\ge N$ and all $x\in X$: $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le d_n<\varepsilon$. ∎

Moves used: sup ≤ an upper bound ε/2 twice bound independently of x

The proof in one piece

“⇒”: Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $|f_n(x)-f(x)|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $n\ge N$, $x\in X$. Then $0\le d_n\le\frac\varepsilon2<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$, so $d_n\to0$. “⇐”: Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $d_n<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. Then $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le d_n<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$, $x\in X$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why does the proof use $\frac\varepsilon2$ in “⇒” and not $\varepsilon$?

    Answer

    From “$<\varepsilon$ for all $x$” we only get $\sup\le\varepsilon$. Example: $|f_n(x)-f(x)|=\varepsilon(1-x)$ on $(0,1)$ is $<\varepsilon$ everywhere, with supremum exactly $\varepsilon$. With $\frac\varepsilon2$ we get $\sup\le\frac\varepsilon2<\varepsilon$.

  2. Rung 2 (1 min). Compute $d_n$ for $x^n$ on $[0,1]$ with $f$ from Worked example 1. What does the sup test say?

    Answer

    $|x^n-f(x)|=x^n$ for $x<1$ and $0$ at $x=1$, so $d_n=\sup_{x<1}x^n=1$ (take $x$ close to $1$). $d_n=1\not\to0$, so the convergence is not uniform.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write both directions from the frame alone.

The recipe for “does $f_n$ converge uniformly?”

  1. Pointwise limit first. Fix $x$ and compute $f(x)=\lim_nf_n(x)$. If it fails to exist somewhere, there is no uniform convergence either.
  2. Yes: find a bound $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le r_n$ that does not contain $x$, with $r_n\to0$. Then $d_n\le r_n\to0$, so the convergence is uniform. (You don't need the exact supremum.)
  3. No: find points $x_n$ with $|f_n(x_n)-f(x_n)|\ge c$ for a fixed $c>0$. Then $d_n\ge c$, so $d_n\not\to0$. The bad point $x_n$ usually moves with $n$. Or: if all $f_n$ are continuous and $f$ is not, use Proof B below.

Typical bounds for step 2: drop terms from a denominator ($\frac1{x^2+n}\le\frac1n$), or use $a^2+b^2\ge2ab$ ($1+nx^2\ge2\sqrt n\,|x|$).

Worked example 3: $x^n$ on $[0,a]$ for a fixed $a<1$

  1. What is the pointwise limit on $[0,a]$?

    Answer

    $f=0$, since every $x\in[0,a]$ is $<1$.

  2. Find a bound for $|x^n-0|$ that doesn't contain $x$.

    Answer

    For $0\le x\le a$: $x^n\le a^n$. And $a^n\to0$.

  3. Conclude. Why is there no contradiction with Rung 2 above?

    Answer

    $d_n\le a^n\to0$, so $x^n\to0$ uniformly on $[0,a]$. On $[0,1]$ (and even on $[0,1)$) the trouble sits arbitrarily close to $1$. Cutting off a piece $(a,1]$ removes all the bad points. Whether convergence is uniform depends on the set $X$.

4. Uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous

Proof B: the uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous (Satz 7.48, p. 316)

Theorem. Let $D\subseteq\mathbb R$ (or $\mathbb C$) and let $f_n:D\to\mathbb R$ (or $\mathbb C$) be continuous. If $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $D$, then $f$ is continuous.

You'll need these (lessons 7 and 10)
  • Continuity at $x_0$: for every $\eta>0$ there is $\delta>0$ with $|g(x)-g(x_0)|<\eta$ for all $x\in D$ with $|x-x_0|<\delta$.
  • Triangle inequality with two middle points: $|a-d|\le|a-b|+|b-c|+|c-d|$.

The idea. We want $f(x)$ close to $f(x_0)$. We know nothing about $f$ directly, but we know a continuous $f_N$ that is uniformly close to $f$. So walk in three hops: from $f(x)$ to $f_N(x)$ (small, by uniform convergence), from $f_N(x)$ to $f_N(x_0)$ (small, by continuity of $f_N$), from $f_N(x_0)$ back to $f(x_0)$ (small, by uniform convergence again). Give each hop $\frac\varepsilon3$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First lines: “Let $x_0\in D$ and $\varepsilon>0$.” Last line: “so $|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x\in D$ with $|x-x_0|<\delta$. Hence $f$ is continuous at $x_0$, and $x_0$ was arbitrary.”
  • The gap: find $\delta$. It can only come from the continuity of some $f_n$, so first fix one good $n$.
The storyWhat you write

Pick a place $x_0$ on the coast and a hair's width $\varepsilon$.

Let $x_0\in D$ and $\varepsilon>0$.

Choose one night's map that lies, all along the coast, within a third of a hair of the truth.

By uniform convergence there is $N$ with $|f_N(x)-f(x)|<\frac\varepsilon3$ for all $x\in D$.

That map has no gaps: near $x_0$ it barely changes.

$f_N$ is continuous at $x_0$, so there is $\delta>0$ with $|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|<\frac\varepsilon3$ for all $x\in D$ with $|x-x_0|<\delta$.

Three hops: true coast at $x$ to the map at $x$, along the map to $x_0$, back to the true coast at $x_0$. Each hop is less than a third of a hair.

For $x\in D$ with $|x-x_0|<\delta$:

$$\begin{aligned}|f(x)-f(x_0)|&\le |f(x)-f_N(x)|+|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|+|f_N(x_0)-f(x_0)|\\&<\tfrac\varepsilon3+\tfrac\varepsilon3+\tfrac\varepsilon3\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

So the true coast has no gap at $x_0$ either, and $x_0$ was any place.

So $f$ is continuous at $x_0$. Since $x_0\in D$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous. ∎

Moves used: ε/3 three times insert a middle point continuity with a chosen ε

The proof in one piece

Let $x_0\in D$ and $\varepsilon>0$. By uniform convergence there is $N$ with $|f_N(x)-f(x)|<\frac\varepsilon3$ for all $x\in D$. Since $f_N$ is continuous at $x_0$, there is $\delta>0$ with $|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|<\frac\varepsilon3$ for all $x\in D$ with $|x-x_0|<\delta$. For such $x$,

$$\begin{aligned}|f(x)-f(x_0)|&\le |f(x)-f_N(x)|+|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|+|f_N(x_0)-f(x_0)|\\&<\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

So $f$ is continuous at $x_0$, and since $x_0$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Fix $x_0$ and $\varepsilon$.
  2. One $N$ with $|f_N-f|<\frac\varepsilon3$ everywhere (uniform convergence).
  3. One $\delta$ from the continuity of that $f_N$ at $x_0$.
  4. Three hops with the triangle inequality: $f(x)\to f_N(x)\to f_N(x_0)\to f(x_0)$.

In short: “fix $N$, then $\delta$, then three thirds”. Say clearly in step 2 that the estimate holds for all $x$: that is exactly where “uniform” is used.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). The first and third hops are estimated at two different points, $x$ and $x_0$. Why does pointwise convergence not suffice for that?

    Answer

    With pointwise convergence, $N$ depends on the point. We need $|f(x)-f_N(x)|<\frac\varepsilon3$ for every $x$ near $x_0$, but $\delta$ (and so the set of these $x$) comes from $f_N$, which needs $N$ first. Pointwise, $N$ would depend on $x$, and $x$ on $\delta$, and $\delta$ on $N$: a circle. Uniform convergence gives one $N$ for all $x$ before $\delta$ is chosen.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Use Proof B to show in one line that $x^n$ does not converge uniformly on $[0,1]$.

    Answer

    If it did, the limit would be continuous by Proof B, since all $x^n$ are continuous. But the limit is $1_{\{1\}}$, which jumps at $1$. So the convergence is not uniform.

  3. Rung 3 (5 min). Write the whole proof from the exam box alone. Then do Exercise 4, which is the exam version.

Why it's cool. Proof B is a free test for non-uniform convergence: continuous functions with a discontinuous limit can never converge uniformly. But the converse fails. On $[0,1)$, $x^n\to0$, a continuous limit, and the convergence is still not uniform (Rung 2 of Proof A: the supremum is still $1$). A continuous limit doesn't prove anything; you still need the sup.

5. Uniform limits of integrable functions are integrable

Proof C: integral and uniform limit can be swapped (Satz 7.49, p. 317)

Theorem. Let $f_n:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ be Riemann integrable, and $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $[a,b]$. Then $f$ is Riemann integrable, and

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_a^bf_n\,dx=\int_a^b\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n\,dx=\int_a^bf\,dx.$$
You'll need these (lesson 9)
  • The $\varepsilon$-criterion: $f$ is integrable ⇔ for every $\varepsilon>0$ there are step functions $u\le f\le o$ with $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$.
  • Monotonicity: $g\le h$ ⇒ $\int_a^bg\le\int_a^bh$. Constants: $\int_a^bc\,dx=c\,(b-a)$. A step function plus a constant is a step function.

The idea. Integrability: $f_N$ is integrable, so it is squeezed between step functions $u$ and $o$ that are close together. And $f$ lies in the $\varepsilon$-tube around $f_N$. So move the lower step function down by $\varepsilon$ and the upper one up by $\varepsilon$: they now squeeze $f$, and their gap grew only by $2\varepsilon(b-a)$. The integrals: $f-\varepsilon\le f_n\le f+\varepsilon$, so the integrals differ by at most $\varepsilon(b-a)$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Part 1 must end with “so by the $\varepsilon$-criterion, $f$ is integrable.” So: for every $\varepsilon>0$, produce step functions $u'\le f\le o'$ with $\int(o'-u')$ small.
  • Part 2 must end with “$\left|\int_a^bf_n-\int_a^bf\right|\le\dots$ for all $n\ge N$, so $\int f_n\to\int f$.”
  • The gap: the step functions for $f$ must come from those of one $f_N$.
The storyWhat you write

Choose one night's map that is within a hair of the true coast everywhere.

Part 1. Let $\varepsilon>0$. By uniform convergence there is $N$ with $|f_N(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x\in[a,b]$, so $f_N-\varepsilon\le f\le f_N+\varepsilon$.

That map is squeezed between two staircase outlines that are close together.

Since $f_N$ is integrable, there are step functions $u\le f_N\le o$ with $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$.

Lower one staircase by a hair and raise the other by a hair: now they squeeze the true coast.

Let $u'=u-\varepsilon$ and $o'=o+\varepsilon$. These are step functions, and

$$u'=u-\varepsilon\le f_N-\varepsilon\le f\le f_N+\varepsilon\le o+\varepsilon=o'.$$

The gap between them grew by only two hairs times the length of the coast.

$$\begin{aligned}\int_a^b(o'-u')\,dx&=\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx+2\varepsilon(b-a)\\&<\varepsilon\,(1+2(b-a))\end{aligned}.$$

Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $\varepsilon(1+2(b-a))$ can be made as small as we like, so $f$ is integrable by the $\varepsilon$-criterion.

(Why “$<\varepsilon$ times a constant” is enough: the criterion asks for a gap below some given $\delta>0$. Run the argument with $\varepsilon=\frac{\delta}{1+2(b-a)}$; then the gap is $<\varepsilon(1+2(b-a))=\delta$. The constant $1+2(b-a)$ doesn't depend on $\varepsilon$, so it can always be divided out like this. If you prefer, start with $\varepsilon$ and use the tube $\frac{\varepsilon}{1+2(b-a)}$ from the beginning; the gap then comes out $<\varepsilon$ exactly.)

The true coast's area and the map's area differ by at most a hair times the coast's length.

Part 2. Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $N$ with $f-\varepsilon\le f_n\le f+\varepsilon$ on $[a,b]$ for all $n\ge N$. By monotonicity, for $n\ge N$:

$$\begin{aligned}\int_a^bf\,dx-\varepsilon(b-a)&\le \int_a^bf_n\,dx\\&\le \int_a^bf\,dx+\varepsilon(b-a)\end{aligned},$$

that is, $\left|\int_a^bf_n\,dx-\int_a^bf\,dx\right|\le\varepsilon(b-a)$. Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $\int_a^bf_n\,dx\to\int_a^bf\,dx$. ∎

Moves used: the ε-criterion (squeeze) bound independently of x smaller than every ε

The proof in one piece

Integrability. Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $|f_N(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x\in[a,b]$, and step functions $u\le f_N\le o$ with $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$. Then $u-\varepsilon$ and $o+\varepsilon$ are step functions with $u-\varepsilon\le f\le o+\varepsilon$ and $$\int_a^b\big((o+\varepsilon)-(u-\varepsilon)\big)dx<\varepsilon(1+2(b-a)).$$As $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, $f$ is integrable by the $\varepsilon$-criterion.

Integrals. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $f-\varepsilon\le f_n\le f+\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. Monotonicity of the integral gives $\left|\int_a^bf_n-\int_a^bf\right|\le\varepsilon(b-a)$ for $n\ge N$. Hence $\int_a^bf_n\to\int_a^bf$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Tube: one $N$ with $f_N-\varepsilon\le f\le f_N+\varepsilon$ on all of $[a,b]$.
  2. Squeeze $f_N$: step functions $u\le f_N\le o$ with $\int(o-u)<\varepsilon$.
  3. Shift: $u-\varepsilon\le f\le o+\varepsilon$, gap $<\varepsilon(1+2(b-a))$. The $\varepsilon$-criterion gives integrability.
  4. Integrals: monotonicity of the integral turns $f-\varepsilon\le f_n\le f+\varepsilon$ into $\left|\int f_n-\int f\right|\le\varepsilon(b-a)$.

In short: “tube, squeeze $f_N$, shift by $\varepsilon$, then integrate the tube”. If you forget step 3, write at least step 4: it alone is often worth half the points.

6. Series of functions and the Weierstrass M-test

A series of functions $\sum_{k=1}^\infty g_k$ is, as in lesson 14, the sequence of its partial sums $s_n(x)=\sum_{k=1}^ng_k(x)$. “Converges uniformly” means: $s_n\to s$ uniformly. Next lesson's power series $\sum a_kx^k$ are exactly such series, and this test is how the script proves they are continuous (p. 319–320).

Proof D: the Weierstrass M-test

M-test. Let $g_k:X\to\mathbb R$ (or $\mathbb C$), and suppose there are numbers $M_k$ with $|g_k(x)|\le M_k$ for all $x\in X$ and $\sum_{k=1}^\infty M_k<\infty$. Then for every $x$, $\sum_kg_k(x)$ converges absolutely, and the partial sums converge uniformly on $X$ to $s(x)=\sum_{k=1}^\infty g_k(x)$.

The idea. Lesson 14's majorant test gives convergence at each $x$. For uniformity, look at the error after $n$ terms: it is the tail $\sum_{k>n}g_k(x)$, and it is at most $\sum_{k>n}M_k$, the tail of a convergent number series. That bound doesn't contain $x$ and tends to $0$. The sup test finishes.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “For each $x$, $\sum|g_k(x)|\le\sum M_k$ converges, so $s(x)$ exists.” Last line: “so $\sup_x|s(x)-s_n(x)|\to0$, and by the sup test the convergence is uniform.”
  • The gap: bound $|s(x)-s_n(x)|$ by something without $x$ that tends to $0$.
The storyWhat you write

Each night's correction to the map is at most $M_k$, anywhere on the coast, and these maximal corrections add up to a finite total.

Fix $x\in X$. Since $|g_k(x)|\le M_k$ and $\sum M_k$ converges, the majorant test gives absolute convergence of $\sum_kg_k(x)$. Let $s(x)$ be its value.

What's still missing after night $n$ is all the later corrections, and these are at most the rest of the total.

For every $n$ and $x$:

$$\begin{aligned}|s(x)-s_n(x)|&=\Bigl|\sum_{k=n+1}^\infty g_k(x)\Bigr|\\&\le \sum_{k=n+1}^\infty|g_k(x)|\\&\le \sum_{k=n+1}^\infty M_k\\&=:r_n\end{aligned}.$$

(The middle step is the triangle inequality for finite sums, then the limit.)

The rest of a finite total shrinks to nothing, and this bound is the same all along the coast.

$r_n=\sum_{k=1}^\infty M_k-\sum_{k=1}^nM_k\to0$. So $\sup_{x\in X}|s(x)-s_n(x)|\le r_n\to0$, and by the sup test (Proof A), $s_n\to s$ uniformly on $X$. ∎

Moves used: find a majorant bound independently of x go to partial sums

The proof in one piece

For each $x$, $\sum|g_k(x)|\le\sum M_k<\infty$, so $s(x)=\sum_kg_k(x)$ exists. For all $n$ and $x$, $|s(x)-s_n(x)|=\left|\sum_{k>n}g_k(x)\right|$, which is $\le\sum_{k>n}M_k=r_n$, and $r_n\to0$ as the tail of a convergent series. So $\sup_x|s(x)-s_n(x)|\le r_n\to0$, and the convergence is uniform by the sup test. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Show that $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{\sin(kx)}{k^2}$ converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$, and that its sum is continuous.

    Answer

    $\left|\frac{\sin(kx)}{k^2}\right|\le\frac1{k^2}=M_k$ for all $x$, and $\sum\frac1{k^2}$ converges. By the M-test the series converges uniformly. Its partial sums are continuous (finite sums of continuous functions), so by Proof B the sum is continuous.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Does the M-test apply to $\sum_{k=0}^\infty x^k$ on $[-a,a]$ for $0<a<1$? On $(-1,1)$?

    Answer

    On $[-a,a]$: $|x^k|\le a^k=M_k$, and $\sum a^k$ converges, so yes: uniform convergence. On $(-1,1)$ the best bound is $\sup_{|x|<1}|x^k|=1$, and $\sum1$ diverges, so the test doesn't apply. (In fact the convergence is not uniform there; lesson 16 shows why.)

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the proof from the frame alone.

7. Five useful facts

These are short exercises in the script (p. 318). Each one has a two-line reason, and two of them have been asked in exams.

  • Finite unions (Übung 7.50). If $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $X_1$ and on $X_2$, then also on $X_1\cup X_2$: take the larger of the two $N$s. It fails for infinitely many pieces: $x^n$ converges uniformly on each $[0,1-\frac1m]$, but not on their union $[0,1)$.
  • Boundedness (Übung 7.51). A uniform limit of bounded functions is bounded: if $|f_N-f|<1$ everywhere and $|f_N|\le B$, then $|f|\le B+1$. For pointwise limits this fails (Exercise 6).
  • Moving points (Übung 7.52). If continuous $f_n\to f$ uniformly and $z_n\to z_0$, then $f_n(z_n)\to f(z_0)$. Insert a middle point:$$\begin{aligned}|f_n(z_n)-f(z_0)|&\le |f_n(z_n)-f(z_n)|+|f(z_n)-f(z_0)|\end{aligned}.$$The first term is at most $d_n$, and the second tends to $0$ because $f$ is continuous (Proof B). Pointwise, this fails: $x^n$ at $z_n=1-\frac1n$ gives $\left(1-\frac1n\right)^n\to\frac1e\neq0$.
  • Uniform continuity (Übung 7.53). A uniform limit of uniformly continuous functions is uniformly continuous. Same proof as Proof B, with $\delta$ independent of $x_0$. On $[a,b]$ you get it for free: the limit is continuous (Proof B), so uniformly continuous (Heine, lesson 8). That was the 2018 summer exam's 1-point follow-up question.
  • Derivatives are not safe. $f_n(x)=\frac{\sin(nx)}n$ converges uniformly to $0$ on $\mathbb R$ (since $|f_n|\le\frac1n$), but $f_n'(x)=\cos(nx)$ doesn't converge at all (try $x=\pi$: $(-1)^n$). Uniform convergence protects continuity and integrals, not derivatives. Worked example 5 below goes further: a uniform limit of smooth functions can have a corner. Derivatives come back in lesson 17.

8. Worked examples

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

Worked example 4: $f_n(x)=\dfrac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}$ on $[0,1]$

  1. Pointwise limit? Treat $x=0$ and $x>0$ separately.

    Answer

    $f_n(0)=0$. For $x>0$: $0\le f_n(x)\le\frac{nx}{n^2x^2}=\frac1{nx}\to0$. So $f=0$.

  2. Is there a bad point? Try $x_n=\frac1n$.

    Answer

    $f_n\left(\frac1n\right)=\frac{1}{1+1}=\frac12$. So $d_n\ge\frac12$ for all $n$, and the convergence is not uniform on $[0,1]$. (The bump of height $\frac12$ slides towards $0$.)

  3. Is it uniform on $[a,1]$ for a fixed $a>0$?

    Answer

    Yes: for $x\ge a$, $0\le f_n(x)\le\frac1{nx}\le\frac1{na}\to0$, a bound without $x$. The bad points $\frac1n$ eventually leave $[a,1]$.

Worked example 5: $f_n(x)=\sqrt{x^2+\frac1{n^2}}$ on $\mathbb R$

  1. Pointwise limit?

    Answer

    $\sqrt{x^2+\frac1{n^2}}\to\sqrt{x^2}=|x|$, by continuity of the square root.

  2. Bound $f_n(x)-|x|$ without $x$. (Multiply by $\sqrt{x^2+\frac1{n^2}}+|x|$ on top and bottom.)

    Answer

    $$0\le\sqrt{x^2+\tfrac1{n^2}}-|x|=\frac{\frac1{n^2}}{\sqrt{x^2+\frac1{n^2}}+|x|}\le\frac{\frac1{n^2}}{\frac1n}=\frac1n,$$ since the denominator is at least $\sqrt{\frac1{n^2}}=\frac1n$.

  3. Conclude. What's surprising?

    Answer

    $d_n\le\frac1n\to0$: uniform convergence to $|x|$. Every $f_n$ is differentiable everywhere (a smooth curve), but the limit $|x|$ has a corner at $0$. Uniform convergence keeps continuity, but not differentiability.

Worked example 6: the travelling bump $f_n(x)=\dfrac1{1+(x-n)^2}$ on $\mathbb R$

  1. Pointwise limit?

    Answer

    For fixed $x$, $(x-n)^2\to\infty$, so $f_n(x)\to0$.

  2. Uniform on $\mathbb R$?

    Answer

    No: the bad point is $x_n=n$, where $f_n(n)=1$. So $d_n=1$. The bump never shrinks; it just walks away to the right.

  3. Uniform on $(-\infty,R]$ for fixed $R$?

    Answer

    Yes. For $n>R$ and $x\le R$: $n-x\ge n-R>0$, so $f_n(x)\le\frac1{1+(n-R)^2}\to0$, a bound without $x$.

The three ways convergence fails to be uniform, all with a moving bad point: the bump gets taller (the tents), it slides into a corner (Worked example 4), or it walks off to infinity (this one).

Exercise 1 (Kobel-Keller HS24, question 7, 2 points)

Open the original: HS24 exam

Let $f_n:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ ($n\ge1$) be continuous functions on a compact interval. Which statement is correct? (A) If $\sup_{x\in[a,b]}|f_n(x)|\ge\frac1{2n}$, the sequence cannot converge uniformly to $0$. (B) If the maximum of each $f_n$ is attained at a different point $x_n$, the sequence cannot converge uniformly to $0$. (C) If $f_n\to f$ pointwise, then $f$ is continuous. (D) If $f_n\to f$ uniformly, then $f$ is Riemann integrable.

Solution

Answer (D). Each $f_n$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, so integrable (lesson 9). By Proof C, the uniform limit $f$ is integrable. (Or: $f$ is continuous by Proof B, hence integrable.)

  • (A) False. The constants $f_n=\frac1{2n}$ have $\sup|f_n|=\frac1{2n}$ and converge uniformly to $0$, since $d_n=\frac1{2n}\to0$. A lower bound only rules out uniform convergence if it doesn't tend to $0$.
  • (B) False. Take $[a,b]=[0,1]$, $x_n=\frac1{n+1}$ (all different) and $f_n(x)=\frac1n\bigl(1-|x-x_n|\bigr)$. Its maximum $\frac1n$ is attained only at $x_n$, and $|f_n|\le\frac1n\to0$ uniformly.
  • (C) False. $x^n$ on $[0,1]$ (Worked example 1).

Moves: a small counterexample.

Exercise 2 (Kobel-Keller HS24, question 18 (b) and (c), 5 points)

Open the original: HS24 exam

Let $f_n(x)=\dfrac x{1+nx^2}$ on $\mathbb R$, $n\ge1$. (b) Determine the pointwise limit. (c) Does $(f_n)_n$ converge uniformly? Justify your answer.

Solution

Frame: the recipe: pointwise limit first, then a bound without $x$. Moves: bound independently of x.

(b) (2 points). $f_n(0)=0$ for all $n$. For $x\neq0$: $|f_n(x)|=\frac{|x|}{1+nx^2}\le\frac{|x|}{nx^2}=\frac1{n|x|}\to0$. So the pointwise limit is $f=0$.

(c) (3 points). Yes. For all $x\in\mathbb R$: $(1-\sqrt n\,|x|)^2\ge0$ gives $1+nx^2\ge2\sqrt n\,|x|$. So for $x\neq0$,

$$\begin{aligned}|f_n(x)-0|&=\frac{|x|}{1+nx^2}\\&\le \frac{|x|}{2\sqrt n\,|x|}\\&=\frac1{2\sqrt n}\end{aligned},$$

and at $x=0$ this holds too. The bound doesn't contain $x$, so $\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}|f_n(x)|\le\frac1{2\sqrt n}\to0$, and $f_n\to0$ uniformly by the sup test. (The bound is attained at $x=\frac1{\sqrt n}$: $f_n\left(\frac1{\sqrt n}\right)=\frac{1/\sqrt n}{2}$.) ∎

Part (a) of the exam question asks whether each $f_n$ is uniformly continuous. The answer is yes, via the derivative: $|f_n'|\le1$ makes $f_n$ Lipschitz. That needs lesson 17.

Exercise 3 (Einsiedler-style exam, winter 2012, question 5, 3 points here)

Let $f_n:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$, $f_n(x)=\dfrac{x^k}{x^2+n}$. For which $k\in\{0,1,2\}$ does $(f_n)_n$ converge uniformly on $(0,\infty)$?

Solution

Answer: for $k=0$ and $k=1$. In all three cases the pointwise limit is $0$: for fixed $x$, the numerator is fixed and the denominator tends to $\infty$.

  • $k=0$ (1 point). $0<\frac1{x^2+n}<\frac1n$ for all $x$. So $d_n\le\frac1n\to0$: uniform.
  • $k=1$ (1 point). $x^2+n\ge2\sqrt n\,x$ (from $(x-\sqrt n)^2\ge0$), so $0<\frac x{x^2+n}\le\frac1{2\sqrt n}$. So $d_n\le\frac1{2\sqrt n}\to0$: uniform.
  • $k=2$ (1 point). The bad point is $x_n=\sqrt n$: $f_n(\sqrt n)=\frac n{n+n}=\frac12$. So $d_n\ge\frac12$ for all $n$: not uniform.

Moves: bound independently of x.

Exercise 4 (Felder HS22, question 2.A1, 7 points)

Open the original: HS22 exam

(a) Prove: let $(f_n)_n$ be a sequence of continuous complex-valued functions on $D\subseteq\mathbb R$ that converges uniformly to a function $f$. Then $f$ is continuous. (As in the original exam: the inputs are real, $D\subseteq\mathbb R$, and only the values are complex, $f_n:D\to\mathbb C$. The proof is word for word the same for $D\subseteq\mathbb C$.) (b) Give an example of a sequence of continuous functions that converges pointwise to a function that is not continuous.

Solution

(a) (5 points) Frame: Proof B. The absolute value of complex numbers satisfies the same triangle inequality, so nothing changes. Let $x_0\in D$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $|f_N(x)-f(x)|<\frac\varepsilon3$ for all $x\in D$. Since $f_N$ is continuous at $x_0$, choose $\delta>0$ with $|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|<\frac\varepsilon3$ for $x\in D$, $|x-x_0|<\delta$. For these $x$:

$$\begin{aligned}|f(x)-f(x_0)|&\le |f(x)-f_N(x)|+|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|+|f_N(x_0)-f(x_0)|\\&<\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

So $f$ is continuous at $x_0$. Since $x_0$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous. ∎

(b) (2 points) $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1]$. Each $f_n$ is continuous, and $f_n\to f$ pointwise with $f(x)=0$ for $x<1$ and $f(1)=1$, which is not continuous at $1$. (Write out both limits: $x^n\to0$ for $0\le x<1$, and $1^n=1$.) Moves: ε/3 three times a small counterexample.

Exercise 5 (Einsiedler HS21, question 12, 7 points)

Open the original: HS21 exam · solutions

Let $I=[a,b]$ with $a<b$, and let $f_n:I\to\mathbb R$ be continuous functions that converge pointwise to $f=0$. Suppose that for all $x\in I$: $n\ge m\Rightarrow f_n(x)\le f_m(x)$. Show that $f_n\to0$ uniformly. Hint: argue indirectly.

Solution

Frame: assume not uniform. Negate the definition to get bad points $x_k$, use Bolzano–Weierstrass on them (lesson 12), and get a contradiction at the limit point. Moves: the negation recipe assume the opposite Bolzano–Weierstrass for a candidate.

Step 0: $f_n\ge0$. For fixed $x$, $(f_n(x))_n$ is decreasing with limit $0$, so $f_n(x)\ge0$ for all $n$ (a decreasing sequence stays above its limit). So $|f_n(x)-0|=f_n(x)$.

Step 1: negation (1 point). Suppose the convergence is not uniform. Then there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that for every $N$ there are $n\ge N$ and $x\in I$ with $f_n(x)\ge\varepsilon$. Applying this with $N=1$, then $N=n_1+1$, and so on, we get indices $n_1<n_2<\dots$ and points $x_k\in I$ with $f_{n_k}(x_k)\ge\varepsilon$.

Step 2: a limit point (2 points). $(x_k)_k$ is bounded (it lies in $[a,b]$), so by Bolzano–Weierstrass it has a convergent subsequence $x_{k_j}\to x^*$. Since $a\le x_{k_j}\le b$, also $x^*\in[a,b]$.

Step 3: monotonicity (2 points). Fix $m\in\mathbb N$. For all $j$ with $n_{k_j}\ge m$ (all but finitely many $j$), monotonicity gives

$$f_m(x_{k_j})\ge f_{n_{k_j}}(x_{k_j})\ge\varepsilon.$$

Step 4: continuity and contradiction (2 points). $f_m$ is continuous at $x^*$ and $x_{k_j}\to x^*$, so $f_m(x^*)=\lim_jf_m(x_{k_j})\ge\varepsilon$ (limits keep $\ge$). This holds for every $m$, so $f_m(x^*)\not\to0$, contradicting the pointwise convergence at $x^*$. Hence the convergence is uniform. ∎

This is Dini's theorem. Each hypothesis is needed: without compactness, $x^n$ on $[0,1)$ is a counterexample. Without monotonicity, the tents of Worked example 2 are one. Without continuity of the limit, $x^n$ on $[0,1]$ is one (there $f\neq0$).

Exercise 6 (short theory questions, older Einsiedler exams)

(a) Give a sequence of continuous functions that converges pointwise but not uniformly (2017 summer, 1 point). (b) Let continuous $f_n:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ converge uniformly to $f$. Is $f$ uniformly continuous? Answer yes or no, with a short reason (2018 summer, 1 point). (c) Show that a uniform limit of bounded functions is bounded, and find bounded functions with an unbounded pointwise limit (Übung 7.51).

Solution

(a) $x^n$ on $[0,1]$: the limit $1_{\{1\}}$ is not continuous, so by Proof B the convergence can't be uniform. (Or $x^n$ on $[0,1)$, where $d_n=1$.)

(b) Yes. $f$ is continuous by Proof B, and a continuous function on a compact interval is uniformly continuous (Heine).

(c) Choose $N$ with $|f_N(x)-f(x)|<1$ for all $x$, and $B$ with $|f_N|\le B$. Then $$\begin{aligned}|f(x)|&\le |f(x)-f_N(x)|+|f_N(x)|\\&<1+B\end{aligned}$$for all $x$. Counterexample: on $(0,1]$, $f_n(x)=\min\left\{n,\frac1x\right\}$. Each $f_n$ is bounded by $n$, and $f_n(x)=\frac1x$ as soon as $n\ge\frac1x$, so the pointwise limit is $\frac1x$, which is unbounded. Moves: insert a middle point a small counterexample.

Bonus (Figalli FS26, question A2, 7 points)

Open the original: FS26 exam

Let $f_n:[0,1]\to\mathbb R$ be $1$-Lipschitz, that is $|f_n(x)-f_n(y)|\le|x-y|$ for all $x,y$, and let $f_n\to f$ pointwise on $[0,1]$. (a) Show that $f$ is $1$-Lipschitz. (b) Show that $f_n\to f$ uniformly.

Solution

Frame: pointwise convergence controls finitely many points at once (take the largest $N$). The Lipschitz condition controls everything in between. Moves: finitely many points, then fill the gaps take the larger N ε/3 three times.

(a) (2 points). For fixed $x,y$: $|f_n(x)-f_n(y)|\le|x-y|$ for all $n$, and $f_n(x)-f_n(y)\to f(x)-f(y)$. Limits keep $\le$, and $|\cdot|$ is continuous, so $|f(x)-f(y)|\le|x-y|$.

(b) (5 points). Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $M\in\mathbb N$ with $\frac1M<\frac\varepsilon3$, and the grid points $x_i=\frac iM$, $i=0,1,\dots,M$. For each of these finitely many points there is $N_i$ with $|f_n(x_i)-f(x_i)|<\frac\varepsilon3$ for $n\ge N_i$. Let $N=\max\{N_0,\dots,N_M\}$. Now let $x\in[0,1]$ and $n\ge N$. Pick a grid point with $|x-x_i|\le\frac1{2M}<\frac\varepsilon3$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}|f_n(x)-f(x)|&\le \underbrace{|f_n(x)-f_n(x_i)|}_{\le|x-x_i|}+\underbrace{|f_n(x_i)-f(x_i)|}_{<\varepsilon/3}+\underbrace{|f(x_i)-f(x)|}_{\le|x-x_i|\text{ by (a)}}\\&<\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

$N$ doesn't depend on $x$, so $f_n\to f$ uniformly. ∎

10. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

A monster built with the M-test. In 1872 Weierstrass shocked Berlin with the function $W(x)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{\cos(13^k\pi x)}{2^k}$. The M-test (with $M_k=\frac1{2^k}$) makes it a uniform limit of continuous functions, so it is continuous by Proof B. But it has a corner at every point: it is differentiable nowhere. Its graph is a coastline that looks equally jagged under any magnifying glass. Mathematicians of the time called such functions “monsters”. Today they are the start of fractal geometry.

And in the other direction. Weierstrass also proved (1885) that every continuous function on $[a,b]$ is a uniform limit of polynomials. However wild a continuous coast is, a polynomial map fits it inside any $\varepsilon$-tube you like. So even the monster is a uniform limit of the simplest functions there are.
