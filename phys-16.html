Recap of lesson 15 (3 min)

  1. What are $Q^\swarrow$ and $W^\swarrow$ for an isothermal expansion from $V_1$ to $V_2$?

    Answer

    $Q^\swarrow=\nu RT\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}=-W^\swarrow$, and $\Delta U=0$.

  2. How do you tell an engine from a refrigerator in the $p$–$V$ diagram?

    Answer

    Clockwise loop: the gas does net work, an engine. Counterclockwise: net work is done on the gas, a refrigerator or heat pump.

  3. What is the Carnot efficiency, and what did Carnot's argument rest on?

    Answer

    $\eta_C=1-\frac{T_k}{T_w}$. It rests on the second law: a better engine could pump heat from cold to hot for free.

1. The story: the ice palace

In winter the castle lake freezes, and the children skate. The blade presses so hard on the ice that a thin film melts under it, and they glide on water. In the kitchen a kettle boils: the cook keeps the fire roaring, but the water won't get hotter than boiling, it just turns to steam faster.

The alchemist opens a flask of perfume in one corner of the hall. A minute later the whole hall smells of roses. She has waited all her life for the perfume to gather itself back into the flask. It never has.

In physics: the perfume spreads because spread-out states are overwhelmingly more probable than gathered ones. Entropy counts this, and the second law says it never decreases in a closed system. The kettle shows latent heat: during a phase transition the heat goes into breaking bonds, not into temperature. And the skater shows that water is strange: its ice melts under pressure. To see any of this we need gases that are not ideal: real gases.

2. Entropy

Entropy as probability (section 10.7.3, page 314). Boltzmann's gravestone in Vienna carries the formula

$$S=k\ln W,$$

where $W$ measures how probable (how many ways realizable) the state is. Example: the chance that one molecule is in the left part $V_1$ of a box $V_0$ is $\frac{V_1}{V_0}$. For all $N=\nu N_A$ molecules at once it is $\left(\frac{V_1}{V_0}\right)^{N}$, so gathering the gas into $V_1$ changes the entropy by

$$\Delta S=k\ln\left(\frac{V_1}{V_0}\right)^{\nu N_A}=-\nu R\ln\frac{V_0}{V_1}<0.$$

For a mole of gas and half the box, the probability is $2^{-6\cdot10^{23}}$: it never happens. That is the perfume.

Entropy as heat over temperature (section 10.7.4, pages 315–317). A process is reversible if it runs through equilibrium states, infinitely slowly, and can be undone without leaving any trace. Heat conduction, mixing, friction and the free expansion into vacuum are irreversible. If the heat $\delta Q^\swarrow_{\text{rev}}$ is supplied reversibly at temperature $T$, the entropy grows by

$$dS=\frac{\delta Q^\swarrow_{\text{rev}}}T.$$

The script checks it on the isothermal compression from $V_0$ to $V_1$: $\Delta S=\frac{Q^\swarrow}T=-\nu R\ln\frac{V_0}{V_1}$, exactly the result from probability. $S$ is a state function (set to zero at $T=0$), so $\Delta S$ depends only on the start and end states. To compute it for any process, even an irreversible one, invent a reversible path between the same two states and integrate $\frac{\delta Q}T$ along it.

The second law as a formula (page 317): in a closed system

$$\Delta S\ge0,\qquad\oint dS=\oint\frac{\delta Q^\swarrow_{\text{rev}}}T=0\ \text{for a reversible cycle}.$$

Real processes raise the total entropy; idealized reversible ones keep it constant. That is the arrow of time.

The ideal gas. With $\delta Q^\swarrow=dU-\delta W^\swarrow=\nu C_V\,dT+p\,dV$ and $p=\frac{\nu RT}V$, integration gives $\Delta S=\nu C_V\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}+\nu R\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}$. For the four processes:

Process$\Delta S$ of the gas
isochoric$\nu C_V\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}$
isobaric$\nu C_p\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}$
isothermal$\nu R\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}=\frac{Q^\swarrow}T$
reversible adiabatic$0$ (“isentropic”)
a heat reservoir at $T_R$$\frac{Q^\swarrow_R}{T_R}$ (its temperature doesn't change)

The free expansion (page 316). Gas in an insulated box flows through a flap into vacuum. No heat ($Q=0$), no work (nothing is pushed: $W=0$), so $\Delta U=0$ and for an ideal gas $T$ stays the same. Yet the process is irreversible: the gas never flows back. Its entropy rise is computed along a reversible isotherm between the same states: $\Delta S=\nu R\ln\frac{V_E}{V_A}>0$, although no heat flowed. $\Delta S=\frac QT$ holds only for reversible heat.

Try it: why the gas never goes back

The molecules start in the left half. Press Go to pull out the wall. The slider sets the number of molecules: watch how quickly “all on the left again” becomes hopeless.

With 2 molecules both are on the left a quarter of the time. With 60 the chance is $10^{-18}$. A real gas has $10^{23}$.

The Carnot limit from entropy (section 10.7.5, pages 317–318). An engine takes $Q_1$ from the hot reservoir and gives $Q_2$ to the cold one. After a full cycle the gas is back where it started, so all the entropy change is in the reservoirs: $\Delta S=\frac{Q_2}{T_k}-\frac{Q_1}{T_w}\ge0$. Hence $\frac{Q_2}{Q_1}\ge\frac{T_k}{T_w}$ and

$$\eta=1-\frac{Q_2}{Q_1}\le1-\frac{T_k}{T_w},$$

with equality for the reversible (Carnot) engine. No pistons, no Poisson equations: the second law alone. In a $T$–$S$ diagram, where the area under a curve is the heat $\int T\,dS$, the Carnot cycle is a rectangle: two horizontal isotherms and two vertical adiabats.

Worked example 1: melting ice and the universe

$1\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at $0\,^\circ\mathrm C$ melts in a room at $20\,^\circ\mathrm C$ (melting heat $333\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$). Find the entropy change of the ice, of the room, and of both together.

  1. The ice melts at constant $T=273\,\mathrm K$. Its $\Delta S$?

    Answer

    Melting at a fixed temperature is reversible for the ice: $\Delta S_{\text{ice}}=\frac{Q}{T}$ $=\frac{333\,000}{273}\,\mathrm{J/K}=+1220\,\mathrm{J/K}$.

  2. The room is a reservoir at $293\,\mathrm K$ that gives off the same heat.

    Answer

    $\Delta S_{\text{room}}=\frac{-333\,000}{293}\,\mathrm{J/K}$ $=-1137\,\mathrm{J/K}$.

  3. Total?

    Answer

    $\Delta S=1220-1137=+83\,\mathrm{J/K}>0$. Heat flowing from warm to cold raises the total entropy, as the second law demands. The other way round it would be negative: forbidden.

Result: $+1220\,\mathrm{J/K}$, $-1137\,\mathrm{J/K}$, total $+83\,\mathrm{J/K}$. The same heat counts for more entropy at a lower temperature, so heat flowing downhill always creates entropy.

3. Real gases: van der Waals

Real molecules attract each other and take up space. Van der Waals put both into the equation of state (section 10.8.1, page 318):

$$\left(p+\frac{a\nu^2}{V^2}\right)(V-\nu b)=\nu RT.$$

The internal pressure $\frac{a\nu^2}{V^2}$ comes from the attraction: a molecule about to hit the wall is pulled back by the others, so it hits more softly and the measured $p$ is lower than ideal. The covolume $\nu b$ is the volume the molecules themselves occupy, about four times their own volume. With $a=b=0$ it is the ideal gas again. Deviations are largest when the gas is dense and cold, because then the molecules are close and slow.

The critical point (pages 320–321). Below a critical temperature an isotherm has a wiggle; above it, none. At $T_K$ the isotherm has a horizontal inflection point: setting $\frac{\partial p}{\partial V_M}=0$ and $\frac{\partial^2p}{\partial V_M^2}=0$ gives

$$V_{MK}=3b,\qquad T_K=\frac{8a}{27bR},\qquad p_K=\frac a{27b^2}.$$

Below $T_K$, compressing the gas does not follow the wiggle: from some volume on, the pressure stays constant while more and more of the gas turns liquid (the coexistence region), until it is all liquid and the isotherm shoots up. Above $T_K$ no pressure, however high, liquefies the gas.

Try it: isotherms of CO₂

Van der Waals isotherms of carbon dioxide ($a=0.366\,\mathrm{Pa\,m^6/mol^2}$, $b=44\,\mathrm{cm^3/mol}$) against the molar volume. The dashed line is the ideal gas at the same temperature; below $T_K$ the flat orange line is where liquid and gas coexist.

At $360\,\mathrm K$ the real and ideal curves nearly agree except at small volume. Lower the temperature below about $295\,\mathrm K$ (the van der Waals value; real CO₂ has $304\,\mathrm K$) and the wiggle appears. The flat line cuts it so that the two areas between them are equal (Maxwell's rule, not in the script).

4. Phase transitions

Vapour pressure (section 10.8.2, page 321). In the coexistence region the pressure depends only on $T$: that is the vapour pressure $p_d(T)$. Plotted against $T$ it is the boiling curve of the phase diagram, running from the triple point to the critical point. Water boils when its vapour pressure reaches the outside pressure, so on a mountain, at lower pressure, it boils below $100\,^\circ\mathrm C$.

Latent heat (pages 321–322). During a phase transition, supplied heat does not raise the temperature: it breaks the bonds between molecules. For the whole mass,

$$Q=m\,L_m.$$

Water: melting $333\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$, vaporization $2260\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$. Boiling a kilogram of water away takes more than five times the heat needed to bring it from $0$ to $100\,^\circ\mathrm C$.

Try it: heating a block of ice

One kilogram of ice at $-20\,^\circ\mathrm C$ is heated at constant pressure. Move the slider (or press Go) to supply heat and watch the temperature.

The two flat stretches are the latent heats. On them the “heat capacity” $\frac{dQ}{dT}$ is infinite, not zero: heat flows in, the temperature doesn't move.

Clausius–Clapeyron (pages 322–323). Running a tiny Carnot cycle across the coexistence region gives the slope of the vapour pressure curve:

$$\frac{dp_d}{dT}=\frac{L_m}{T\,(V_{m,d}-V_{m,fl})},$$

with the specific volumes of vapour and liquid. The same holds for melting. For water, ice has the larger volume (it floats), so the melting curve has negative slope: pressure melts ice. That is the skater (page 324).

Humidity (page 323): the relative humidity is $\varphi=\frac{p_{\text{eff}}}{p_d}$, the actual water vapour pressure over the vapour pressure. Warming air lowers $\varphi$, because $p_d$ rises: that is why heated winter air feels dry.

5. Enthalpy, Joule–Thomson and the third law

Enthalpy (section 10.9.1, page 325) is the state function

$$H=U+pV.$$

At constant pressure, $dH=dU+p\,dV=\delta Q^\swarrow$: the heat supplied at constant pressure is the change of enthalpy, and $C_p=\frac1\nu\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}$. Chemistry tables list melting, boiling and reaction heats as enthalpies for this reason, because most processes happen at atmospheric pressure.

Joule–Thomson (pages 325–328). Push a gas slowly through a porous plug from high pressure $p_1$ to low pressure $p_2$, insulated. The work balance gives $U_1+p_1V_1=U_2+p_2V_2$: the enthalpy stays constant. For an ideal gas $H$ depends only on $T$, so nothing happens. A real gas cools (below its inversion temperature) because the molecules do work against their mutual attraction as they spread. Linde's machine liquefies air this way.

The third law (Nernst, page 328): absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite number of steps.

Why it's cool

Entropy is the one law of physics that knows the difference between past and future. Every other law in this course (Newton, energy, momentum) works the same played backwards. A film of perfume gathering into a flask breaks no law of mechanics, only this one, and that is how you know the film runs backwards.

6. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed. Exercises 1–5 are multiple choice, 6–9 are long problems. Exercise 9 is the longest problem of the course; it's fine to do it on another day.

Exercise 1 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.6(b), 1 point, original)

A perfectly insulated container has a dividing wall: ideal gas on the left, vacuum on the right. The wall is suddenly removed and the gas fills the whole container. How does the temperature of the gas change? (A) It falls. (B) It rises. (C) It stays exactly constant. (D) It first falls, then rises back to the original value.

Solution

C. Free expansion: $Q=0$ (insulated), $W=0$ (no piston is pushed), so $\Delta U=0$, and for an ideal gas $U$ depends only on $T$. (A real gas would cool a little, because of the attraction.)

Exercise 2 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.6(a), 1 point, original)

An ideal gas goes from state A to state B. In case 1 by a reversible isothermal expansion, in case 2 by an irreversible free expansion. What about the pressure change $\Delta p_{\text{Gas}}$? (A) It is larger in case 2. (B) In case 1 it is zero. (C) It is exactly equal in both cases. (D) It is smaller in case 2.

Solution

C. Pressure is a state variable: same start A and same end B mean the same $\Delta p$, whatever the path. (B) is wrong because an isothermal expansion lowers $p$ ($pV=$ const). The same holds for $\Delta S$: equal in both cases, although in case 2 no heat flows.

Exercise 3 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.5(b), 1 point, original)

Which statement about the van der Waals equation is FALSE? (A) The reduced volume $(V-nb)$ comes from the finite volume of the gas particles. (B) The ideal gas equation can be seen as a simplification of the van der Waals equation. (C) Deviations from the ideal gas equation are more significant at low temperatures. (D) The equation accounts, among other things, for the repulsive forces between the gas particles.

Solution

D. The force term $\frac{a\nu^2}{V^2}$ describes the attraction between the particles (the internal pressure lowers $p$). (A) and (B) are the script's words, and (C) is true because slow molecules feel the attraction more. (Strictly, the covolume $b$ is a crude model of the hard repulsion at contact, but the expected answer is D.)

Exercise 4 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.6(c), 1 point, original)

A pot of water is heated at constant pressure ($1\,\mathrm{bar}$) with a constant heat supply $\frac{dQ}{dt}>0$. Which statement about the water while it boils is correct? (A) The temperature keeps rising as $dT=\frac1{mc_p}dQ$ with $c_p$ of liquid water. (B) The same with $c_p$ of steam. (C) The effective heat capacity of the system during boiling is exactly zero. (D) During boiling the temperature does not rise, since all the heat goes into the phase transition.

Solution

D. The heat is latent heat: $Q=mL_m$ at constant $T$. (C) is backwards: $C=\frac{dQ}{dT}$ with $dT=0$ is infinite, not zero.

Exercise 5 (FS23, Aufgabe 1.4(c), 2 points, original)

The cycle of lesson 15, Exercise 6: one mole, $C_V=15\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$, B → C is an isochoric cooling from $T_2=500\,\mathrm K$ to $T_1=300\,\mathrm K$. What is the entropy change between B and C? (A) $7.6\,\mathrm{J/K}$ (B) $8.6\,\mathrm{J/K}$ (C) $9.6\,\mathrm{J/K}$ (D) $10.6\,\mathrm{J/K}$ ($\ln\frac53\approx0.51$)

Solution

A (in magnitude). Isochoric: $\Delta S=\nu C_V\ln\frac{T_1}{T_2}=15\ln\frac35\,\mathrm{J/K}$ $\approx-7.7\,\mathrm{J/K}$. The gas cools, so its entropy falls by about $7.6\,\mathrm{J/K}$; the options list only the size. The exam also gives heats for the other steps; you don't need them.

Exercise 6 (FS24, problem 5(c)–(e), “Wasserstoffballon”, 7 points, original)

The balloon of lesson 14, Exercise 8: $n=20\,\mathrm{mol}$ of hydrogen ($f=5$), always at the outside pressure and temperature, $V_1=0.6\,\mathrm{m^3}$ at $p_1=1\,\mathrm{bar}$, $T_1=300\,\mathrm K$; $R\approx10\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$. A slow change of weather brings the pressure to $p_2=1.1\,p_1$ at constant $T_1$. (c) Find $V_2$ and the work the balloon does on its surroundings. ($\ln1.1\approx0.1$) (d) How did the internal energy change? How much heat did the balloon take in? What is the entropy change of the hydrogen? Then at constant $p_2$ the temperature rises from $T_1$ to $T_2=330\,\mathrm K$. (e) Find the heat and the entropy change of the balloon.

Solution

(The exam text says the pressure “decreases” to $1.1\,p_1$; the number is what counts: it rises by $10\,\%$.)

(c) Isothermal: $V_2=\frac{p_1}{p_2}V_1=\frac{0.6}{1.1}\,\mathrm{m^3}\approx0.55\,\mathrm{m^3}$. Work done by the balloon: $W^\nearrow=nRT_1\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}=-nRT_1\ln1.1$ $=-20\cdot10\cdot300\cdot0.1\,\mathrm J=-6\,\mathrm{kJ}$. Negative: the air does $6\,\mathrm{kJ}$ of work on the balloon.

(d) $T$ is constant, so $\Delta U=0$. Then $Q^\swarrow=W^\nearrow=-6\,\mathrm{kJ}$: the balloon gives off $6\,\mathrm{kJ}$ of heat. $\Delta S=\frac{Q^\swarrow}{T_1}=\frac{-6000}{300}\,\mathrm{J/K}=-20\,\mathrm{J/K}$.

(e) Isobaric with $C_p=\frac72R=35\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$: $Q^\swarrow=nC_p\Delta T=20\cdot35\cdot30\,\mathrm J=21\,\mathrm{kJ}$. $\Delta S=nC_p\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}=700\ln1.1\,\mathrm{J/K}$ $\approx70\,\mathrm{J/K}$.

Exercise 7 (FS25, problem 5, “Kreisprozess”, 10 points, original)

A two-atom ideal gas (vibrations frozen) runs reversibly through A → B isobaric at $p_H$ from $V_L$ to $V_H$; B → C isochoric at $V_H$ down to $p_L$; C → A isothermal at $T_L$ back to $V_L$. Use a clear sign convention. (a) Where is the temperature highest? Give $T_H$ in terms of $p_H$ and $V_H$. (b) What heat does the gas take in during A → B and B → C, in terms of $T_H$ and $T_L$? (c) How much does the entropy of the cold bath ($T_L$) change during C → A, in terms of $V_H$, $V_L$? (d) And the entropy of the gas during C → A? Mind the sign. (e) The entropy change of the gas during A → B, in terms of $T_H$, $T_L$? (f) What work does the gas do in one cycle? (g) Define the efficiency of this cycle used as an engine, using only $W^\nearrow_{A\to B}$, $W^\nearrow_{B\to C}$, $W^\nearrow_{C\to A}$ and $Q^\swarrow_{A\to B}$, $Q^\swarrow_{B\to C}$, $Q^\swarrow_{C\to A}$.

Solution

Convention: $\swarrow$ into the gas, $\nearrow$ out of it. $C_V=\frac52R$, $C_p=\frac72R$. A and C lie on the isotherm $T_L$.

(a) $T\propto pV$ is largest at B, the corner with the largest $p$ and $V$: $T_H=\frac{p_HV_H}{nR}$.

(b) $Q^\swarrow_{A\to B}=nC_p(T_H-T_L)$ $=\frac72nR(T_H-T_L)>0$. $Q^\swarrow_{B\to C}=nC_V(T_L-T_H)$ $=-\frac52nR(T_H-T_L)<0$: here heat is given off.

(c) The isothermal compression C → A pushes the heat $Q^\nearrow=nRT_L\ln\frac{V_H}{V_L}$ into the bath. The bath's entropy grows: $\Delta S_{\text{bath}}=\frac{Q^\nearrow}{T_L}=nR\ln\frac{V_H}{V_L}>0$.

(d) The gas loses exactly that: $\Delta S_{\text{gas}}=nR\ln\frac{V_L}{V_H}=-nR\ln\frac{V_H}{V_L}<0$. (Reversible: gas plus bath together change by zero.)

(e) Isobaric: $\Delta S_{A\to B}=nC_p\ln\frac{T_H}{T_L}=\frac72nR\ln\frac{T_H}{T_L}$.

(f) $W^\nearrow_{A\to B}=p_H(V_H-V_L)$, $W^\nearrow_{B\to C}=0$, $W^\nearrow_{C\to A}=-nRT_L\ln\frac{V_H}{V_L}$. With $nRT_L=p_HV_L$ (point A): $W^\nearrow_{\text{net}}=p_H(V_H-V_L)-p_HV_L\ln\frac{V_H}{V_L}$, positive (a clockwise loop).

(g) Heat is taken in only during A → B, so $\eta=\frac{W^\nearrow_{A\to B}+W^\nearrow_{B\to C}+W^\nearrow_{C\to A}}{Q^\swarrow_{A\to B}}$.

Exercise 8 (HS25, problem 5, “Kreisprozess”, 9 points, original)

A one-atom ideal gas ($n$ moles) runs through A → B isothermal expansion, B → C isobaric expansion, C → D isothermal compression, D → A isobaric compression. (a) Sketch the cycle in the $p$–$V$ diagram. (b) Which of $T_A,\dots,T_D$, of $p_A,\dots,p_D$ and of $V_A,\dots,V_D$ are equal? Name the temperatures $T_w$ (warm) and $T_k$ (cold). (c) Compute the work done by the gas in each step and over a cycle, $W^\nearrow_{\text{ges}}$. (d) Compute the change of internal energy in each step and over the cycle. (e) Is this an engine or a refrigerator? Why? (f) Give a sensible upper bound for its efficiency in terms of $T_w$ and $T_k$. Which cycle do you compare it with?

Solution

(b) The isobaric expansion B → C heats the gas, so the second isotherm is the warmer one: $T_A=T_B=T_k$, $T_C=T_D=T_w$. Pressures: $p_B=p_C$ and $p_D=p_A$. No two volumes are equal.

(a) A → B runs down the cold isotherm to the right; B → C goes right at the low pressure $p_B$; C → D climbs the warm isotherm to the left; D → A goes left at the high pressure $p_A$. The loop runs counterclockwise.

(c) With $r=\frac{V_B}{V_A}=\frac{p_A}{p_B}=\frac{V_C}{V_D}$ (both isotherms have the same pressure ratio): $W^\nearrow_{A\to B}=nRT_k\ln r$, $W^\nearrow_{B\to C}=p_B(V_C-V_B)=nR(T_w-T_k)$, $W^\nearrow_{C\to D}=-nRT_w\ln r$, $W^\nearrow_{D\to A}=-nR(T_w-T_k)$. The isobaric works cancel: $W^\nearrow_{\text{ges}}=-nR(T_w-T_k)\ln r<0$.

(d) $\Delta U_{A\to B}=\Delta U_{C\to D}=0$, $\Delta U_{B\to C}=\frac32nR(T_w-T_k)$, $\Delta U_{D\to A}=-\frac32nR(T_w-T_k)$; over the cycle $0$.

(e) A refrigerator (or heat pump): the gas does negative net work, so work must be put in, and it takes heat in at $T_k$ (A → B) and gives it off at $T_w$ (C → D).

(f) No machine between $T_k$ and $T_w$ beats Carnot: as a refrigerator $\eta\le\frac{T_k}{T_w-T_k}$ (as a heat pump $\frac{T_w}{T_w-T_k}$). Compare with the Carnot cycle. (Two isotherms and two isobars is the Ericsson cycle; with a heat store between the isobars it reaches the Carnot value.)

Exercise 9 (FS23, problem 5, “Gasgefüllter Zylinder”, 20 points, original)

A closed cylinder of length $L$ and radius $r$ is split at $x=0$ by a frictionless movable plate. Each side holds $\nu$ moles of ideal gas at temperature $T$ and pressure $p_0$; the temperature stays constant. (a) The plate is moved to $x$. Find $p_1$ (left) and $p_2$ (right) in terms of $x$, $L$, $p_0$, and show that the total gas force on the plate is $F_G=-\pi p_0r^2L\frac x{L^2/4-x^2}$. (b) How much work does the gas do on the plate from $x=0$ to $x_f$ (isothermal)? Conclude that the plate can't be pushed against an end ($x_f=\pm\frac L2$). Hint: $\int\frac x{1-x^2}dx=-\frac12\ln(1-x^2)+C$. (c) Now the left pressure is held at $p_0$ during the compression. What work is done on the gas to move the plate to $x_f$? (d) Draw the isothermal compression of the right gas in a $p$–$V$ diagram, and complete it with two isochores and an isotherm to a cycle. Mark which reservoir (“cold” or “warm”) each corner is coupled to. The cycle is a Stirling engine.

Solution

(a) Volumes $V_1=\pi r^2(\frac L2+x)$, $V_2=\pi r^2(\frac L2-x)$. Isothermal with the same $\nu RT=p_0\pi r^2\frac L2$ on each side: $p_1=p_0\frac{L/2}{L/2+x}$, $p_2=p_0\frac{L/2}{L/2-x}$. The force in $+x$ direction is $(p_1-p_2)\pi r^2$ $=p_0\pi r^2\frac L2\cdot\frac{(L/2-x)-(L/2+x)}{L^2/4-x^2}$ $=-\pi p_0r^2L\frac x{L^2/4-x^2}$. ✓ It always pushes the plate back to the middle.

(b) $W=\int_0^{x_f}F_G\,dx$. Substitute $u=\frac{2x}L$: $\frac{x\,dx}{L^2/4-x^2}=\frac{u\,du}{1-u^2}$, so $W=-\pi p_0r^2L\left[-\frac12\ln(1-u^2)\right]_0^{2x_f/L}$ $=\frac{\pi p_0r^2L}2\ln\left(1-\frac{4x_f^2}{L^2}\right)$. This is negative (we must do the work), and it goes to $-\infty$ as $x_f\to\pm\frac L2$: infinite work, so the plate never reaches an end.

(c) Only the right gas is compressed against a changing pressure: $W^\swarrow_2=-\int p_2\,dV_2=\nu RT\ln\frac{V_{2,\text{start}}}{V_{2,\text{end}}}$ $=\frac{\pi p_0r^2L}2\ln\frac L{L-2x_f}$. (The left gas, at constant $p_0$, expands and does $p_0\pi r^2x_f$ of work on the plate, so the outside force has to supply only the difference.)

(d) The compression is a falling hyperbola run leftwards: $V_2$ from $\pi r^2\frac L2$ down, $p_2$ up from $p_0$. Close the cycle like Stirling (page 312): isochoric heating at the small volume, isothermal expansion at the higher temperature back to the large volume, isochoric cooling to the start. The two corners of the compression isotherm are coupled to the cold reservoir, the two corners of the expansion isotherm to the warm one. Run clockwise, it is an engine.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Life against the arrow? A tree builds ordered wood from disordered air and water, lowering its own entropy. No law is broken: it absorbs low-entropy sunlight (few energetic photons) and radiates high-entropy heat (many weak ones) into the cold sky. The total goes up, as always. Every living thing is an engine running on that temperature difference between the sun and space.

Pressure cookers and mountain tea. On top of Mont Blanc the air pressure is about half, and water boils at about $85\,^\circ\mathrm C$: tea brews badly and pasta takes forever. A pressure cooker does the opposite: at $2\,\mathrm{bar}$ the water boils at about $120\,^\circ\mathrm C$ and food cooks much faster. Both are the vapour pressure curve.
