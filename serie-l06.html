Serie 6, explained: The dual space and Fibonacci

Two infinite-dimensional spaces where the “dual basis” fails to be a basis of the dual space, and the Fibonacci recursion turned into a matrix whose special vectors give the closed formula. The point: in finite dimension $V^*$ looks like $V$; in infinite dimension it is much bigger.

You're done when
you can (1) find a basis of the space of finitely supported sequences, and of step functions; (2) prove that a given map (a parity count, an integral) is a functional; (3) explain why the functionals $e_i^*$ span only functionals that vanish on all but finitely many basis vectors, and give a functional outside their span; (4) turn the Fibonacci recursion into $M^n\binom ab$ and find the vectors that $M$ only stretches.

Aufgabe 27 · Finitely supported sequences over $\mathbb F_2$

Let $c_{00}(\mathbb F_2)$ be the set of all sequences $(a_n:n\in\mathbb N)$ in $\mathbb F_2$ such that $\{n:a_n=1\}$ is finite.

(a) Show that $B=\{e_i:i\in\mathbb N\}$, with $e_i$ the sequence with a $1$ in place $i$ and $0$ elsewhere, is a basis of $c_{00}(\mathbb F_2)$.

(b) Show that for every $S\subseteq\mathbb N$, $f_S\big((a_n)\big):=\sum_{n\in S}a_n$ defines a functional $f_S:c_{00}(\mathbb F_2)\to\mathbb F_2$.

(c) Show that $\langle\{e_i^*:i\in\mathbb N\}\rangle\ne c_{00}(\mathbb F_2)^*$, i.e. $\{e_i^*\}$ is not a basis of the dual space.

Official remark: one can show $c_{00}(\mathbb F_2)^*$ has uncountable dimension, while $c_{00}(\mathbb F_2)$ has countable dimension; the dual basis theorem fails in infinite dimension.

In short: (a) A sequence with finitely many $1$s is the sum of the $e_i$ at those places. (b) The sum has only finitely many non-zero terms, and is linear. (c) $f_{\mathbb N}$ (parity of the number of $1$s) is $1$ on every $e_j$, but each combination of the $e_i^*$ is $0$ on almost all $e_j$.

The idea

A sequence in $c_{00}$ is a finite set of positions with a $1$. (a) It is the sum of the unit sequences at those positions, and that sum is unique. (b) $f_S$ counts the $1$s inside $S$ mod 2; only finitely many are $1$, so the sum makes sense, and counting is additive. (c) The dual functionals $e_i^*$ read off one position each, and a finite combination reads off finitely many positions. The parity functional $f_{\mathbb N}$ needs all positions at once.

You need

Lesson 10, section 5: for a basis $(b_i)$ the dual functionals $b_i^*$ are defined by $b_i^*(b_j)=\delta_{ij}$ and linear extension. In finite dimension they form a basis of $V^*$; section 7 shows why this fails in infinite dimension. Bases (lesson 6, section 5). In $\mathbb F_2$: $1+1=0$.

Hint 1

(a) For $a\in c_{00}$ let $T:=\{n:a_n=1\}$ (finite) and compare $a$ with $\sum_{i\in T}e_i$ term by term. (b) Only $n\in S\cap T$ contribute, a finite sum. For linearity let $T$ contain the supports of both $a$ and $b$.

Hint 2

(c) What is $g(e_j)$ for $g=\sum_{i\in T}\lambda_ie_i^*$ and $j\notin T$? And what is $f_{\mathbb N}(e_j)$?

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

(a) Spanning: a sequence with $1$s at the finite set $T$ is the sum of the $e_i$, $i\in T$. Compare term $n$.

(a) $e_i\in V:=c_{00}(\mathbb F_2)$, since it has one $1$. For $a\in V$ let $T:=\{n:a_n=1\}$, finite. The $n$-th term of $\sum_{i\in T}e_i$ is $1$ if $n\in T$ (exactly one summand contributes) and $0$ otherwise, i.e. $a_n$. So $a=\sum_{i\in T}e_i$ and $\langle B\rangle=V$.

Independence: in a combination of distinct $e_{i_k}$, position $i_l$ only sees $\lambda_l$.

If $\sum_{k=1}^m\lambda_ke_{i_k}=0$ with distinct $i_k$, the $i_l$-th term is $\lambda_l$, so $\lambda_l=0$. Hence $B$ is a basis (and $V$ is infinite-dimensional).

(b) Well defined: only finitely many non-zero terms.

(b) For $a\in V$ only $n\in S\cap T$ give $a_n\ne0$, so $f_S(a)=\sum_{n\in S\cap T}a_n\in\mathbb F_2$ is a finite sum.

Linear: on a common finite set the sum splits.

For $a,b\in V$, $\lambda\in\mathbb F_2$, let $T$ be the (finite) union of their supports. Then $f_S(a+b)=\sum_{n\in S\cap T}(a_n+b_n)=f_S(a)+f_S(b)$ and $f_S(\lambda a)=\lambda f_S(a)$. So $f_S\in V^*$. (E.g. $f_{\{i\}}=e_i^*$.)

(c) Everything in the span of the $e_i^*$ vanishes on almost all $e_j$.

(c) Let $W:=\langle e_i^*:i\in\mathbb N\rangle$. Each $g\in W$ is $g=\sum_{i\in T}\lambda_ie_i^*$ with $T$ finite, and for $j\notin T$: $g(e_j)=\sum_{i\in T}\lambda_ie_i^*(e_j)=0$.

The parity functional is $1$ on every $e_j$: not in $W$.

But $f_{\mathbb N}(e_j)=1$ for all $j$. If $f_{\mathbb N}\in W$, it would vanish on all $e_j$ outside a finite set, a contradiction. So $f_{\mathbb N}\notin W$, $W\ne V^*$, and $\{e_i^*\}$ is not a basis of $V^*$. ∎

The key observation for (c): a finite combination $g=\sum_{i\in T}\lambda_ib_i^*$ vanishes on every basis vector $b_j$ with $j\notin T$. So everything in $\langle b_i^*\rangle$ is zero on all but finitely many basis vectors. A functional that is non-zero on infinitely many basis vectors is therefore outside the span.

For wonder: the dual is uncountably big (official remark)

$W$ is countable (each element is fixed by a finite set of indices). But $S\mapsto f_S$ is injective from $\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$ to $V^*$ (if $j$ is in exactly one of $S,S'$, then $f_S(e_j)\ne f_{S'}(e_j)$), and $\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$ is uncountable by Cantor. So $V^*$ is uncountable and far bigger than $W$.

Trap

Arguing “$f_{\mathbb N}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb N}e_i^*$, so it is in the span”. A span contains only finite combinations; an infinite sum is not a linear combination. That is exactly why the dual basis fails here.

Aufgabe 28 · Step functions and the integral

For $a\lt b$ let $\mathrm{TF}([a,b])$ be the vector space of step functions $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ (constant on finitely many open intervals, arbitrary at the finitely many points in between). For $t\in(a,b]$ and $s\in[a,b]$ let $\chi_t(r)=1$ for $a\le r\lt t$ and $0$ otherwise, and $\chi_{\{s\}}(r)=1$ for $r=s$ and $0$ otherwise.

(a) Show that $B:=\{\chi_t:t\in(a,b]\}\cup\{\chi_{\{s\}}:s\in[a,b]\}$ is a basis of $\mathrm{TF}([a,b])$.

(b) Show that $F:\mathrm{TF}([a,b])\to\mathbb R$, $f\mapsto\int_a^bf\,dx$ is a functional.

(c) Show: $F\notin\langle\{\chi^*:\chi\in B\}\rangle$.

In short: Steps $\chi_t-\chi_u=\mathbf 1_{[u,t)}$ build the plateaus, point functions $\chi_{\{s\}}$ fix the values at the jumps. The integral is linear and is non-zero on every $\chi_t$, infinitely many basis vectors.

The idea

The functions $\chi_t$ are “staircase from the left”: $1$ on $[a,t)$. Differences of two of them give a single plateau $\mathbf 1_{[u,t)}$, so they build every step function up to finitely many points; the point functions $\chi_{\{s\}}$ repair those points. Independence: evaluate at cleverly chosen points, from the right end inwards. (b) is the linearity of the integral from Analysis. (c) is the same idea as Aufgabe 27 (c): $F(\chi_t)=t-a\gt0$ for infinitely many basis vectors.

You need

Lesson 10, section 5: for a basis $(b_i)$ the dual functionals $b_i^*$ are defined by $b_i^*(b_j)=\delta_{ij}$ and linear extension. In finite dimension they form a basis of $V^*$; section 7 shows why this fails in infinite dimension. Step functions and their integral: Analysis I (Analysis lesson 15). Aufgabe 27 (c) for the key observation.

Hint 1

Useful: for $a\le u\lt t\le b$, $\chi_t-\chi_u=\mathbf 1_{[u,t)}$ (with $\chi_a:=0$). For $f$ with partition $a=t_0\lt\dots\lt t_k=b$ and value $c_j$ on $(t_{j-1},t_j)$, set $g:=\sum_jc_j(\chi_{t_j}-\chi_{t_{j-1}})$. What is $f-g$?

Hint 2

Independence: order $t_1\lt\dots\lt t_m$ and the points $s_1,\dots,s_l$. Pick $r\in[t_{m-1},t_m)$ not equal to any $s_j$ and evaluate: you get $\lambda_m=0$. Repeat downwards.

Hint 3

(c) Every $G\in\langle\chi^*\rangle$ vanishes on all but finitely many elements of $B$. Compute $F(\chi_t)$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

(a) The plateau identity: two staircases differ by one plateau.

(a) For $a\le u\lt t\le b$ (with $\chi_a:=0$): $\chi_t-\chi_u=\mathbf 1_{[u,t)}$, since for $r\lt u$ both are $1$, for $u\le r\lt t$ only $\chi_t$ is $1$, and for $r\ge t$ both are $0$.

Spanning: build the plateaus with staircases, then repair the finitely many jump points.

Let $f$ have partition $a=t_0\lt\dots\lt t_k=b$ and value $c_j$ on $(t_{j-1},t_j)$. Put $g:=\sum_{j=1}^kc_j(\chi_{t_j}-\chi_{t_{j-1}})$, a combination of elements of $B$; then $g=c_j$ on $[t_{j-1},t_j)$. So $h:=f-g$ is $0$ except possibly at $t_0,\dots,t_k$, and $h=\sum_{j=0}^kh(t_j)\chi_{\{t_j\}}$. Hence $f=g+h\in\langle B\rangle$.

Independence: a finite combination; evaluate at points avoiding the $s_j$, from the right.

Let $\sum_{i=1}^m\lambda_i\chi_{t_i}+\sum_{j=1}^l\mu_j\chi_{\{s_j\}}=0$ with $a\lt t_1\lt\dots\lt t_m\le b$, distinct $s_j$, and $t_0:=a$. Suppose $\lambda_{i+1}=\dots=\lambda_m=0$. The interval $[t_{i-1},t_i)$ is infinite, so pick $r$ in it with $r\ne s_j$ for all $j$. There $\chi_{t_k}(r)=1$ for $k\ge i$ and $0$ for $k\lt i$, so $0=\lambda_i+\dots+\lambda_m=\lambda_i$.

Then only point functions remain; evaluate at each $s_j$.

By downward induction all $\lambda_i=0$. Then $\sum\mu_j\chi_{\{s_j\}}=0$, and evaluating at $s_j$ gives $\mu_j=0$. So $B$ is a basis of $\mathrm{TF}([a,b])$.

(b) The integral of a step function is a finite sum; refine to a common partition to see linearity.

(b) For $f$ as above, $\int_a^bf\,dx=\sum_{j=1}^kc_j(t_j-t_{j-1})$; the values at the points do not matter. For $f,g$ use a common refinement, with values $c_j,d_j$: $F(f+g)=\sum(c_j+d_j)(t_j-t_{j-1})=F(f)+F(g)$ and $F(\lambda f)=\lambda F(f)$. So $F$ is a functional.

(c) The key observation from Aufgabe 27: the span of the $\chi^*$ only contains functionals that vanish on almost all of $B$.

(c) If $G=\sum_{k=1}^m\lambda_k\chi_k^*$ with $\chi_k\in B$, then $G(\chi')=0$ for every $\chi'\in B\setminus\{\chi_1,\dots,\chi_m\}$.

The integral is non-zero on every staircase.

But $F(\chi_t)=\int_a^b\chi_t\,dx=t-a\gt0$ for every $t\in(a,b]$, infinitely many elements of $B$. So $F\notin\langle\chi^*:\chi\in B\rangle$. ∎

The key observation for (c): a finite combination $g=\sum_{i\in T}\lambda_ib_i^*$ vanishes on every basis vector $b_j$ with $j\notin T$. So everything in $\langle b_i^*\rangle$ is zero on all but finitely many basis vectors. A functional that is non-zero on infinitely many basis vectors is therefore outside the span.

Trap

Using $\{\mathbf 1_{[u,t)}\}$ for all $u\lt t$ as a “basis”: these span, but they are not independent ($\mathbf 1_{[a,c)}=\mathbf 1_{[a,b')}+\mathbf 1_{[b',c)}$). The staircases $\chi_t$ avoid this because each is fixed by one number $t$.

Aufgabe 29 · The Fibonacci matrix and its special vectors

Let $\varphi_{\mathrm{Fib}}:\mathrm{Fib}\to\mathrm{Fib}$ be the linear map with $\varphi_{\mathrm{Fib}}(F_{1,0}):=F_{0,1}$, $\varphi_{\mathrm{Fib}}(F_{0,1}):=F_{1,0}+F_{0,1}$, and let $M_{\mathrm{Fib}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ be its matrix in the basis $\{F_{1,0},F_{0,1}\}$.

(a) For $F_{a,b}=(f_0,f_1,\dots)$ (so $f_0=a$, $f_1=b$), show for all $n\in\mathbb N$: $M_{\mathrm{Fib}}^n\binom ab=\binom{f_n}{f_{n+1}}$, where $M^0:=I$.

(b) Find two different vectors $\binom1{x_1},\binom1{x_2}$ and $\lambda_1,\lambda_2\in\mathbb R$ with $M_{\mathrm{Fib}}\binom1{x_i}=\lambda_i\binom1{x_i}$.

In short: $M$ shifts a window of two consecutive terms: $\binom uv\mapsto\binom v{u+v}$. Induction gives (a). For (b), $\binom1x\mapsto\binom x{1+x}$ is a multiple of $\binom1x$ iff $x^2=x+1$: $x=\varphi,\psi$.

The idea

$M\binom uv=\binom v{u+v}$: it slides a window of two consecutive Fibonacci terms one step forward. Applying it $n$ times slides $n$ steps: that is (a). In (b) you look for windows that are only stretched by one step, i.e. geometric sequences $1,x,x^2,\dots$; that happens exactly when $x^2=x+1$. These are the two sequences of the basis $B_3$ from Serie 3, Aufgabe 17, and the special vectors are the eigenvectors of lesson 21.

You need

The matrix–vector product and $M^{n+1}=M\cdot M^n$ (lesson 12, section 2); induction (lesson 3, section 5); eigenvectors (lesson 21, section 2).

Hint 1

(a) Induction on $n$. For the step, $M^{n+1}\binom ab=M\big(M^n\binom ab\big)=M\binom{f_n}{f_{n+1}}$; compute with $M\binom uv=\binom v{u+v}$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

The one computation everything uses.

For $u,v\in\mathbb R$: $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\binom uv=\binom v{u+v}$. Write $M:=M_{\mathrm{Fib}}$.

(a) Base case $n=0$.

(a) $M^0\binom ab=I\binom ab=\binom ab=\binom{f_0}{f_1}$.

Step: apply $M$ once more and use the recursion.

If $M^n\binom ab=\binom{f_n}{f_{n+1}}$, then $$\begin{aligned}M^{n+1}\tbinom ab&=M\tbinom{f_n}{f_{n+1}}\\&=\tbinom{f_{n+1}}{f_n+f_{n+1}}=\tbinom{f_{n+1}}{f_{n+2}}.\end{aligned}$$

(b) Write out $M\binom1x=\lambda\binom1x$ coordinatewise.

(b) $M\binom1x=\binom x{1+x}$. So $M\binom1x=\lambda\binom1x$ means $x=\lambda$ and $1+x=\lambda x$.

Substitute $\lambda=x$: the golden-ratio equation.

With $\lambda=x$: $x^2=x+1$, i.e. $x^2-x-1=0$, with the two different real solutions $x_1=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2=:\varphi$ and $x_2=\frac{1-\sqrt5}2=:\psi$.

Check: with $\lambda_i:=x_i$ the equation holds.

With $\lambda_1:=\varphi$, $\lambda_2:=\psi$: $M\binom1{x_i}=\binom{x_i}{1+x_i}=\binom{x_i}{x_i^2}=x_i\binom1{x_i}=\lambda_i\binom1{x_i}$. ∎

Remark (official): by (a) and (b), $M^n\binom1{x_i}=\lambda_i^n\binom1{x_i}=\binom{x_i^n}{x_i^{n+1}}$, so $F_{1,x_i}=(1,x_i,x_i^2,\dots)$: the basis $B_3$ of Aufgabe 17. Writing $\binom01=\frac1{\sqrt5}\binom1\varphi-\frac1{\sqrt5}\binom1\psi$ and applying $M^n$ gives Binet's formula $f_n=\frac1{\sqrt5}(\varphi^n-\psi^n)$ again. In the language of lesson 21: $\binom1\varphi,\binom1\psi$ are eigenvectors of $M$ for the eigenvalues $\varphi,\psi$.

Trap

In (b), solving $x=\lambda$ and stopping, or allowing $\binom1x$ with any $x$ and $\lambda=0$. The two coordinates give two equations, and both must hold. Also note the task asks for vectors with first entry $1$, which rules out the zero vector.
