Recap of lesson 15 (5 min)

  • Pointwise: $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for each $x$; $N$ may depend on $x$. Uniform: one $N$ for all $x$, that is $\sup_x|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$.
  • Uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous, and of integrable functions are integrable with $\int f_n\to\int f$.
  • M-test: $|g_k(x)|\le M_k$ for all $x$ and $\sum M_k<\infty$ ⇒ $\sum g_k$ converges uniformly.
  • From lesson 14: root test ($\limsup\sqrt[n]{|c_n|}<1$ ⇒ converges absolutely, $>1$ ⇒ diverges) and ratio test (same with $\frac{|c_{n+1}|}{|c_n|}$).

  1. Does $\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{x^k}{2^k}$ converge uniformly on $[-1,1]$?

    Answer

    Yes, by the M-test with $M_k=\frac1{2^k}$: $\left|\frac{x^k}{2^k}\right|\le\frac1{2^k}$ for $|x|\le1$.

  2. What is $\lim_n\sqrt[n]{n}$? And $\lim_n\sqrt[n]{n^3}$?

    Answer

    Both are $1$ (lesson 11): $\sqrt[n]{n^3}=\left(\sqrt[n]n\right)^3\to1$. Powers of $n$ are invisible to the $n$-th root. You'll use this constantly today.

  3. Ratio test on $\sum\frac{3^n}{n!}$?

    Answer

    $\frac{3^{n+1}/(n+1)!}{3^n/n!}=\frac3{n+1}\to0<1$: converges.

1. The story: the spell with endless words

In the old tower lives a wizard whose spells are sentences with infinitely many words. The first word is a constant, the second is multiplied by the distance $x$ from the tower, the third by $x^2$, the fourth by $x^3$, and so on. Spoken at distance $x$, the spell's strength is $a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3+\dots$

Every such spell has a range: a circle around the tower. Inside the circle, the words fade fast enough and the spell takes effect. Outside, the words grow and the spell explodes. On the circle itself, nobody can say in advance; each spell must be tested there separately.

One spell is special. Its words are $1,\ x,\ \frac{x^2}{2},\ \frac{x^3}{6},\ \dots,\ \frac{x^k}{k!},\dots$, and its range is infinite: it works everywhere, even at imaginary distances. It is the spell of growth, $\exp$. Spoken at an imaginary distance $i\theta$, it doesn't grow at all: it turns. That turning is sine and cosine, and the spell needs exactly $2\pi$ to turn once around.

In maths: a spell is a power series $\sum a_nz^n$, its range is the radius of convergence $R$, and the special spell is $\exp(z)=\sum\frac{z^n}{n!}$. Its “turning” is Euler's formula $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$.

2. The exponential function and the logarithm

Class defined $\exp$ (section 6.3) before series existed, through compound interest. A bank pays $x$ interest per year, split into $n$ payments of $\frac xn$, each one earning interest on the previous ones. After a year you have $\left(1+\frac xn\right)^n$ per franc. More payments give more money, but not unlimited money:

Definition and Proposition 6.29 (p. 266). For every $x\in\mathbb R$ the limit

$$\exp(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac xn\right)^n$$

exists. $e=\exp(1)\approx2.718$. The function $\exp:\mathbb R\to(0,\infty)$ is continuous, strictly increasing and bijective, and satisfies

$$\begin{aligned}&\exp(x+y)=\exp(x)\exp(y)\\&\exp(-x)=\frac1{\exp(x)}\\&1+x\le \exp(x)\end{aligned}.$$

Why each fact holds, in one line (the full proofs are on p. 267–271; they are on the skip list):

  • The limit exists: $\left(1+\frac xn\right)^n$ is increasing (from some $n$ on, by Bernoulli's inequality, lesson 4) and bounded, so it converges (monotone convergence, lesson 11).
  • $1+x\le\exp(x)$: Bernoulli's inequality gives $\left(1+\frac xn\right)^n\ge1+n\cdot\frac xn=1+x$ for large $n$, and limits keep $\ge$. This inequality is the workhorse of the section.
  • Addition formula: multiply the sequences and show the error tends to $0$. Section 5 gives a much cleaner proof with power series.
  • Strictly increasing: for $y>x$: $\exp(y)=\exp(x)\exp(y-x)>\exp(x)$, because $\exp(y-x)\ge1+(y-x)>1$.
  • Onto $(0,\infty)$: for $y>0$, $\exp(-\frac1y)<y<\exp(y)$ (both from $1+x\le e^x$), and the intermediate value theorem (lesson 7) gives an $x$ with $\exp(x)=y$.

The logarithm and powers (Korollar 6.33, p. 271). The inverse function of $\exp:\mathbb R\to(0,\infty)$ is the natural logarithm $\log:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ (also written $\ln$). It is continuous and strictly increasing (inverse function theorem, lesson 8), and

$$\begin{aligned}&\log(ab)=\log a+\log b\\&\log1=0\\&\log e=1\end{aligned}.$$

For $x>0$ and any $a\in\mathbb R$ we define $x^a=\exp(a\log x)$. This agrees with the old powers for rational $a$, and gives $e^x=\exp(x)$. The usual rules hold: $\log(x^a)=a\log x$, $x^ax^b=x^{a+b}$, $(x^a)^b=x^{ab}$.

The idea behind $x^a=\exp(a\log x)$: $x=e^{\log x}$, so “$x^a$” should be $\left(e^{\log x}\right)^a=e^{a\log x}$. For irrational $a$, like $2^{\sqrt2}$, there was no definition before; now there is one, and it is continuous in both $x$ and $a$.

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

Worked example 1: logarithms lose to every power

Claim: for every $\alpha>0$ and $x>0$, $\log x<\frac{x^\alpha}\alpha$. Hence $\frac{\log n}{n^\alpha}\to0$ (Übung 6.35, p. 272).

  1. Apply $1+y\le e^y$ with $y=\alpha\log x$. What do you get?

    Answer

    $1+\alpha\log x\le e^{\alpha\log x}=x^\alpha$, so $\alpha\log x<x^\alpha$, that is $\log x<\frac{x^\alpha}\alpha$.

  2. Use this with $\frac\alpha2$ instead of $\alpha$ to bound $\frac{\log n}{n^\alpha}$.

    Answer

    $\log n<\frac2\alpha n^{\alpha/2}$, so $$\begin{aligned}0&<\frac{\log n}{n^\alpha}\\&<\frac2\alpha\cdot\frac{n^{\alpha/2}}{n^\alpha}\\&=\frac2\alpha n^{-\alpha/2}\to0\end{aligned}$$(for $n\ge2$). Sandwich.

This was the missing step in lesson 14, Exercise 2 (“$\log n\le n^{2\eta}$ for large $n$”). The growth ladder for $n\to\infty$: $\log n\ll n^\alpha\ll a^n\ll n!\ll n^n$ (for $\alpha>0$, $a>1$). Each one divided by the next tends to $0$.

Worked example 2: two bounds for $\log(1+y)$

Claim: for $y>-1$, $\frac y{1+y}\le\log(1+y)\le y$. (So $\log(1+y)\approx y$ for small $y$.)

  1. Get the upper bound from $1+y\le e^y$.

    Answer

    Take $\log$ (increasing) of $1+y\le e^y$: $\log(1+y)\le y$.

  2. For the lower bound, apply the upper bound to $\frac1{1+y}=1+\left(-\frac y{1+y}\right)$.

    Answer

    $\log\frac1{1+y}\le-\frac y{1+y}$. The left side is $-\log(1+y)$, so $\log(1+y)\ge\frac y{1+y}$.

Exercise 9 (an Einsiedler exam limit) uses exactly these two bounds.

3. Power series and the radius of convergence

Definitions 7.54 and 7.55 (p. 319). A power series is an expression $\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_nz^n$ with coefficients $a_n\in\mathbb C$. Its radius of convergence is

$$R=\frac1{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}}\in[0,\infty],$$

with the conventions $\frac10=\infty$ and $\frac1\infty=0$ (used only here). This is the root formula (also called Cauchy–Hadamard).

The idea in one sentence: for a fixed $z$, the power series is an ordinary series with terms $a_nz^n$, so lesson 14's root test decides. The root of the term is $\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}\,|z|$, which is less than $1$ in the long run exactly when $|z|<\frac1{\limsup\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}}=R$.

Proof A: what the radius of convergence says (Satz 7.56, p. 319)

Theorem. Let $R$ be the radius of convergence of $\sum a_nz^n$. Then:

  1. the series converges absolutely for every $z$ with $|z|<R$;
  2. it diverges for every $z$ with $|z|>R$;
  3. for every $S$ with $0<S<R$, the partial sums converge uniformly on $\{|z|\le S\}$, and $f(z)=\sum a_nz^n$ is continuous on $\{|z|<R\}$.

On the circle $|z|=R$ the theorem says nothing.

You'll need these (lessons 14 and 15)
  • Root test: $\limsup\sqrt[n]{|c_n|}<1$ ⇒ $\sum c_n$ converges absolutely; $>1$ ⇒ diverges.
  • M-test and uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous.
  • limsup rule: for a constant $c\ge0$, $\limsup(c\,b_n)=c\limsup b_n$ (with $0\cdot\infty$ avoided below).

The idea. Parts 1 and 2: compute the root test's number for the series at $z$; it is $\frac{|z|}R$. Part 3: on the smaller disc $|z|\le S$, the terms are at most $|a_n|S^n$, and $\sum|a_n|S^n$ converges by part 1 (since $S<R$). That is an M-test majorant.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Parts 1–2 start with “Fix $z$ and compute $\limsup\sqrt[n]{|a_nz^n|}$.” They end with the root test.
  • Part 3 must produce numbers $M_n$ with $|a_nz^n|\le M_n$ for all $|z|\le S$ and $\sum M_n<\infty$, then use the M-test.
  • Watch the edge cases: $R=0$ and $R=\infty$.
The storyWhat you write

Speak the spell at distance $|z|$: the $n$-th word shrinks or grows like $\left(\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}\,|z|\right)^n$.

Let $L=\limsup_n\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}\in[0,\infty]$, so $R=\frac1L$. Fix $z\neq0$. Then $\sqrt[n]{|a_nz^n|}=\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}\,|z|$, so $\limsup_n\sqrt[n]{|a_nz^n|}=L\,|z|$ (also if $L=\infty$, since $|z|>0$). At $z=0$ the series is $a_0$ and converges.

Inside the range the words fade geometrically; outside they explode.

If $|z|<R$, then $L|z|<1$ (for $L=0$: $0<1$), so the root test gives absolute convergence. If $|z|>R$, then $L|z|>1$ (for $L=\infty$: $\infty>1$), so the series diverges. This proves 1 and 2.

On a slightly smaller circle, each word is at most its strength at the edge of that circle, and those strengths add up to something finite.

Let $0<S<R$. By part 1 at $z=S$, $\sum_n|a_n|S^n$ converges. For $|z|\le S$: $|a_nz^n|\le|a_n|S^n=:M_n$. By the M-test, the partial sums $\sum_{n\le N}a_nz^n$ converge uniformly on $\{|z|\le S\}$.

Every point inside the range lies on some smaller circle, so the spell's strength changes without jumps everywhere inside.

The partial sums are polynomials, so continuous; by uniform convergence $f$ is continuous on $\{|z|\le S\}$. Every $z$ with $|z|<R$ has $|z|<S<R$ for some $S$ (e.g. $S=\frac{|z|+R}2$, or $S=|z|+1$ if $R=\infty$), so $f$ is continuous at $z$. ∎

Moves used: find a majorant bound independently of x

The proof in one piece

Let $L=\limsup\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}$, $R=\frac1L$. For $z\neq0$, $\limsup\sqrt[n]{|a_nz^n|}=L|z|$, which is $<1$ if $|z|<R$ and $>1$ if $|z|>R$. The root test gives absolute convergence, respectively divergence. For $0<S<R$, $\sum|a_n|S^n$ converges, and $|a_nz^n|\le|a_n|S^n$ for $|z|\le S$; by the M-test the partial sums (polynomials) converge uniformly there, so $f$ is continuous on $\{|z|\le S\}$. Every $|z|<R$ lies in such a disc, so $f$ is continuous on $\{|z|<R\}$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why does part 3 need $S<R$ and not $S=R$?

    Answer

    The majorant $\sum|a_n|S^n$ must converge. Part 1 guarantees that only for $S<R$. At $S=R$ it may diverge (for $\sum z^n$: $\sum1^n$).

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Radius of $\sum z^n$? Is the convergence uniform on $\{|z|<1\}$?

    Answer

    $a_n=1$, $\sqrt[n]1=1$, so $R=1$. The series converges at every point of the open disc, but that is pointwise convergence. Uniform would need one $N$ that works for all $|z|<1$ at once, and that fails. The error is $\left|\frac{z^{N+1}}{1-z}\right|$, and for real $z=x\nearrow1$ it tends to $\infty$, so its supremum over the disc is $\infty$ for every $N$. Another way to see it, as a proof: if $\left|\sum_{k\le N}z^k-\frac1{1-z}\right|<1$ for all $|z|<1$, then $\left|\frac1{1-z}\right|<1+(N+1)$ for all such $z$, but $\frac1{1-x}\to\infty$ as $x\nearrow1$. Uniform only on the smaller discs $|z|\le S<1$.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write parts 1 and 2 from the frame alone.

Proof B: the ratio formula (Lemma 7.60, p. 321)

Ratio formula. If $a_n\neq0$ for all $n$ and the limit $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|a_n|}{|a_{n+1}|}$ exists in $[0,\infty]$, then it equals $R$.

Careful: it is $\frac{|a_n|}{|a_{n+1}|}$, the reciprocal of the ratio test's fraction. That's why it gives $R$ directly, not $\frac1R$.

The idea. Use the ratio test instead of the root test: the ratio of neighbouring terms of $\sum a_nz^n$ is $\frac{|a_{n+1}|}{|a_n|}|z|$, which tends to $\frac{|z|}{\rho}$, where $\rho$ is the limit in the box. So the series converges for $|z|<\rho$ and diverges for $|z|>\rho$. Only one number has that property: $R$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Last line: “so $\rho=R$.” The gap: show convergence for $|z|<\rho$ and divergence for $|z|>\rho$; then compare with Proof A.
The storyWhat you write

Compare each word with the next one.

Let $\rho=\lim\frac{|a_n|}{|a_{n+1}|}$. For $z\neq0$: $\dfrac{|a_{n+1}z^{n+1}|}{|a_nz^n|}=\dfrac{|a_{n+1}|}{|a_n|}\,|z|\to\dfrac{|z|}\rho$ (read as $0$ if $\rho=\infty$, and $\infty$ if $\rho=0$).

Inside distance $\rho$ the words shrink, outside they grow.

By the ratio test: for $|z|<\rho$ the limit is $<1$, so the series converges; for $|z|>\rho$ it is $>1$, so it diverges.

A range is fixed by where the spell works and where it explodes, so this is the range.

If $\rho<R$, pick $\rho<|z|<R$: divergence contradicts Proof A (1). If $\rho>R$, pick $R<|z|<\rho$: convergence contradicts Proof A (2). So $\rho=R$. ∎

Moves used: assume the opposite

The proof in one piece

Let $\rho=\lim\frac{|a_n|}{|a_{n+1}|}$. For $z\neq0$, $\frac{|a_{n+1}z^{n+1}|}{|a_nz^n|}\to\frac{|z|}\rho$, so by the ratio test $\sum a_nz^n$ converges for $|z|<\rho$ and diverges for $|z|>\rho$. By Proof A, a $z$ with $\rho<|z|<R$ or $R<|z|<\rho$ would give a contradiction. Hence $\rho=R$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Compute the radius of $\sum\frac{z^n}{n!}$ with the ratio formula.

    Answer

    $\frac{|a_n|}{|a_{n+1}|}=\frac{(n+1)!}{n!}=n+1\to\infty$, so $R=\infty$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Why can't the ratio formula handle $a_n=2^n$ for even $n$ and $a_n=3^n$ for odd $n$? What does the root formula give?

    Answer

    The ratios jump between about $\frac{2^n}{3^{n+1}}\to0$ and $\frac{3^n}{2^{n+1}}\to\infty$, so the limit doesn't exist. The root formula: $\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}$ is $2$ or $3$, so $\limsup=3$ and $R=\frac13$. The root formula always works; the ratio formula only when its limit exists.

  3. Rung 3 (2 min). Write the proof from the frame alone.

The recipe for “compute the radius of convergence”

  1. Factorials or products ($n!$, $(2n)!$, $\binom{2n}n$): use the ratio formula $R=\lim\frac{|a_n|}{|a_{n+1}|}$. Factorials cancel nicely in ratios.
  2. $n$-th powers ($2^n$, $n^n$, $\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{n^2}$): use the root formula. Powers of $n$ disappear: $\sqrt[n]{n^k}\to1$.
  3. Sums like $\frac{n\,6^n}{3^{2n+1}+n2^n}$: find the dominant term on top and at the bottom, take its root, and check with a sandwich if needed.
  4. Gaps like $\sum b_nx^{2n}$ (only even powers): substitute $y=x^2$, find the radius $R_y$ in $y$, then $R=\sqrt{R_y}$.
  5. Boundary $|z|=R$: only if asked. Plug in $z=\pm R$ and use lesson 14's tests.

Worked example 3: four radii

  1. $\displaystyle\sum n^2x^n$.

    Answer

    Root: $\sqrt[n]{n^2}\to1$, so $R=1$.

  2. $\displaystyle\sum n!\,x^n$.

    Answer

    Ratio: $\frac{n!}{(n+1)!}=\frac1{n+1}\to0$, so $R=0$. It converges only at $x=0$.

  3. $\displaystyle\sum\frac{2^n}{n}x^n$.

    Answer

    Root: $\sqrt[n]{\frac{2^n}n}=\frac2{\sqrt[n]n}\to2$, so $R=\frac12$.

  4. $\displaystyle\sum 4^nx^{2n}$.

    Answer

    With $y=x^2$: $\sum4^ny^n$ has $R_y=\frac14$. It converges for $|y|=x^2<\frac14$, i.e. $|x|<\frac12$, and diverges for $|x|>\frac12$. So $R=\frac12$. (Directly with the root formula: $a_{2n}=4^n$, $\sqrt[2n]{4^n}=2$, odd coefficients are $0$, so $\limsup=2$ and $R=\frac12$.)

Worked example 4: the boundary is unpredictable

All three series have $R=1$. What happens at $x=1$ and $x=-1$?

  1. $\sum x^n$.

    Answer

    At $\pm1$ the terms have absolute value $1\not\to0$: diverges at both.

  2. $\sum\frac{x^n}n$.

    Answer

    At $1$: harmonic, diverges. At $-1$: $\sum\frac{(-1)^n}n$ converges by Leibniz.

  3. $\sum\frac{x^n}{n^2}$.

    Answer

    At $\pm1$: $\left|\frac{(\pm1)^n}{n^2}\right|=\frac1{n^2}$, absolutely convergent at both.

Everything can happen on the circle. Abel's theorem (Satz 7.65, p. 322): if the series converges at $x=R$, the function $x\mapsto\sum a_nx^n$ is also continuous at $R$ from the left. In lesson 19 this gives the ferryman's point from lesson 14: $1-\frac12+\frac13-\dots=\log2$.

4. Adding and multiplying series

Adding is easy: $\sum a_n+\sum b_n=\sum(a_n+b_n)$ (lesson 14). Multiplying is harder: in $(a_0+a_1+a_2+\dots)(b_0+b_1+b_2+\dots)$ every $a_k$ meets every $b_j$, and we must decide in which order to add up the infinitely many products. The Cauchy product collects them by $k+j=n$, exactly as when multiplying polynomials:

Cauchy product (Korollar 7.37, p. 310). If $\sum_{n\ge0}a_n$ and $\sum_{n\ge0}b_n$ converge absolutely, then so does $\sum_{n\ge0}c_n$ with $c_n=\sum_{k=0}^na_kb_{n-k}$, and

$$\Bigl(\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{n=0}^\infty b_n\Bigr)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\ \sum_{k=0}^na_kb_{n-k}.$$

For power series (Proposition 7.62): for $|z|$ less than both radii, $\left(\sum a_nz^n\right)\left(\sum b_nz^n\right)=\sum c_nz^n$ with the same $c_n$, because $a_kz^k\cdot b_{n-k}z^{n-k}=a_kb_{n-k}z^n$.

Why “absolutely”? Absolutely convergent series may be rearranged freely (lesson 14, section 7), and the Cauchy product is a rearrangement of all the products $a_kb_j$. For conditionally convergent series it can fail: the Cauchy product of $\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n+1}}$ with itself diverges (Übung 7.39). The proof is on the skip list.

Worked example 5: $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac n{2^n}$ (Übung 7.64)

The plan. We know one series value by heart: $\sum_{n\ge0}x^n=\frac1{1-x}$ for $|x|<1$. The new series has an extra factor $n$ in front of each term. The trick is to produce such a factor by multiplying the geometric series with itself, then to set $x=\frac12$. We go slowly, one step at a time.

  1. Step 1: multiply out by hand. Write $$(1+x+x^2+x^3+\dots)\cdot(1+x+x^2+x^3+\dots)$$and collect the terms with $x^0$, $x^1$, $x^2$, $x^3$. How many ways are there to get $x^2$?

    Answer

    Pick one power from each bracket so that the exponents add up:

    • $x^0$: only $1\cdot1$, so coefficient $1$.
    • $x^1$: $1\cdot x$ and $x\cdot1$, so coefficient $2$.
    • $x^2$: $1\cdot x^2$, $x\cdot x$, $x^2\cdot1$, so coefficient $3$.
    • $x^3$: four ways, so coefficient $4$.

    So the product starts $1+2x+3x^2+4x^3+\dots$: the coefficient of $x^n$ is $n+1$, one for each way to split $n=k+(n-k)$ with $k=0,1,\dots,n$.

  2. Step 2: say it with the Cauchy product. Why may we multiply two infinite series like this, and what does the Cauchy product formula $c_n=\sum_{k=0}^na_kb_{n-k}$ give here?

    Answer

    For $|x|<1$ the geometric series converges absolutely, so the Cauchy product (section 4) applies. Here $a_k=b_k=1$ for all $k$, so

    $$c_n=\sum_{k=0}^n1\cdot1=n+1.$$

    The left side is $\frac1{1-x}\cdot\frac1{1-x}$. So, for $|x|<1$,

    $$\frac1{(1-x)^2}=\sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)x^n.$$

  3. Step 3: from $n+1$ to $n$. We want $\sum nx^n$, but we have $\sum(n+1)x^n$. What do you subtract?

    Answer

    $(n+1)x^n-x^n=nx^n$, so subtract the geometric series itself. Both series converge, so we may subtract them term by term:

    $$\begin{aligned}\sum_{n\ge0}nx^n&=\sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)x^n-\sum_{n\ge0}x^n\\&=\frac1{(1-x)^2}-\frac1{1-x}\\&=\frac{1-(1-x)}{(1-x)^2}=\frac x{(1-x)^2}.\end{aligned}$$

    (The term with $n=0$ is $0$, so the sum may as well start at $n=1$.) Check the start: $$\begin{aligned}\frac x{(1-x)^2}&=x(1+2x+3x^2+\dots)\\&=x+2x^2+3x^3+\dots\end{aligned}$$✓

  4. Step 4: plug in $x=\frac12$. Is that allowed, and what comes out?

    Answer

    Yes, $\left|\frac12\right|<1$. Then $x^n=\frac1{2^n}$, so

    $$\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac n{2^n}=\frac{1/2}{(1/2)^2}=\frac{1/2}{1/4}=2.$$

    Sanity check with partial sums: $$\begin{aligned}\frac12+\frac24+\frac38+\frac4{16}&=0.5+0.5+0.375+0.25\\&=1.625\end{aligned},$$and the next terms $\frac5{32},\frac6{64},\dots$ keep adding less and less, towards $2$.

In short: square the geometric series (coefficients $n+1$), subtract the geometric series (coefficients $n$), plug in. Lesson 19 shows a faster route: differentiate $\sum x^n$ term by term (Proof C there, Rung 2).

5. The exponential series

Section 7.5 and Satz 7.68 (p. 324–325). For every $z\in\mathbb C$,

$$\exp(z)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{z^k}{k!}=1+z+\frac{z^2}2+\frac{z^3}6+\dots$$

converges absolutely ($R=\infty$, Rung 1 of Proof B), defines a continuous function $\exp:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$, and agrees with $\lim\left(1+\frac xn\right)^n$ for real $x$. Moreover $\exp(z+w)=\exp(z)\exp(w)$ and $|\exp(z)|=\exp(\operatorname{Re}z)$.

Why the two definitions agree: by the binomial theorem, $\left(1+\frac xn\right)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{x^k}{k!}\cdot\left(1-\frac1n\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{k-1}n\right)$, and each bracket tends to $1$. The careful $\varepsilon$-argument (p. 324–325) is on the skip list. From now on, $\exp$ is the series, and $e^z$ means $\exp(z)$.

Proof C: the addition formula $e^{z+w}=e^ze^w$ (Section 7.5.3, p. 326)

You'll need these
  • The Cauchy product (section 4), for absolutely convergent series.
  • The binomial theorem (from school): $(z+w)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nkz^kw^{n-k}$ with $\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$.

The idea. Multiply the two series with the Cauchy product. The $n$-th term is $\sum_k\frac{z^k}{k!}\frac{w^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}$. Multiply and divide by $n!$: the binomial coefficients appear, and the binomial theorem folds the sum into $\frac{(z+w)^n}{n!}$, the $n$-th term of $e^{z+w}$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Both series converge absolutely, so the Cauchy product applies.” Last line: “$=\sum\frac{(z+w)^n}{n!}=\exp(z+w)$.”
  • The gap: turn $\sum_{k}\frac{z^kw^{n-k}}{k!(n-k)!}$ into $\frac{(z+w)^n}{n!}$.
The storyWhat you write

Both growth spells work everywhere, absolutely, so they may be multiplied word by word in any order.

Let $z,w\in\mathbb C$. The series $\sum\frac{z^k}{k!}$ and $\sum\frac{w^j}{j!}$ converge absolutely, so by the Cauchy product

$$e^ze^w=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\ \sum_{k=0}^n\frac{z^k}{k!}\cdot\frac{w^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}.$$

Group the words of total degree $n$, and recognise the binomial counting.

For each $n$:

$$\begin{aligned}\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{z^kw^{n-k}}{k!\,(n-k)!}&=\frac1{n!}\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}z^kw^{n-k}\\&=\frac1{n!}\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nkz^kw^{n-k}\\&=\frac{(z+w)^n}{n!}\end{aligned}.$$

So the product of the two spells is the spell at the summed distance.

Hence $e^ze^w=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(z+w)^n}{n!}=e^{z+w}$. ∎

6. Sine, cosine and Euler's formula

Definition and Satz 7.72 (p. 328). For $z\in\mathbb C$,

$$\begin{aligned}&\sin z=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nz^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}=z-\frac{z^3}{3!}+\frac{z^5}{5!}-\dots\\&\cos z=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nz^{2n}}{(2n)!}=1-\frac{z^2}{2!}+\frac{z^4}{4!}-\dots\end{aligned}$$

Both have $R=\infty$, are continuous, real on $\mathbb R$; $\sin$ is odd, $\cos$ is even. And:

$$\begin{aligned}&e^{iz}=\cos z+i\sin z\\&\cos z=\frac{e^{iz}+e^{-iz}}2\\&\sin z=\frac{e^{iz}-e^{-iz}}{2i}\end{aligned}.$$

Worked example 6: Euler's formula from the series

  1. What are the powers $i^0,i^1,i^2,i^3,i^4,\dots$?

    Answer

    $1,i,-1,-i,1,\dots$, repeating with period 4. So $i^{2n}=(-1)^n$ and $i^{2n+1}=(-1)^ni$.

  2. Split $e^{iz}=\sum\frac{(iz)^k}{k!}$ into even $k=2n$ and odd $k=2n+1$. (Allowed: both parts converge absolutely.)

    Answer

    $$e^{iz}=\sum_n\frac{(-1)^nz^{2n}}{(2n)!}+i\sum_n\frac{(-1)^nz^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}=\cos z+i\sin z.$$

  3. Replace $z$ by $-z$ and solve for $\cos z$ and $\sin z$.

    Answer

    $e^{-iz}=\cos z-i\sin z$ (cos even, sin odd). Adding: $\cos z=\frac{e^{iz}+e^{-iz}}2$. Subtracting: $\sin z=\frac{e^{iz}-e^{-iz}}{2i}$.

Proof D: the addition formulas for sine and cosine (Section 7.6.1, p. 329)

Claim. For all $z,w\in\mathbb C$:

$$\begin{aligned}&\cos(z+w)=\cos z\cos w-\sin z\sin w\\&\sin(z+w)=\sin z\cos w+\cos z\sin w\end{aligned}.$$

The idea. In school these were geometry. Here they are one line of algebra: $e^{i(z+w)}=e^{iz}e^{iw}$, and expand both sides with Euler's formula. For real $z,w$, compare real and imaginary parts. For complex $z,w$ (where $\cos z$ may not be real) use the same equation with $-z,-w$ too, and add or subtract.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Start: “By Proof C, $e^{i(z+w)}=e^{iz}e^{iw}$ and $e^{-i(z+w)}=e^{-iz}e^{-iw}$.” End: the two formulas.
  • The gap: expand with Euler and separate the $\cos$ and $\sin$ parts.
The storyWhat you write

Turning by $z$ and then by $w$ is turning by $z+w$.

By Proof C and Euler's formula:

$$\begin{aligned}\cos(z+w)+i\sin(z+w)&=e^{iz}e^{iw}\\&=(\cos z+i\sin z)(\cos w+i\sin w)\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}&=(\cos z\cos w-\sin z\sin w)+i(\sin z\cos w+\cos z\sin w)\end{aligned}.$$

Turning backwards gives the same with the signs of the sine parts flipped.

Likewise with $-z,-w$ (cos even, sin odd):

$$\begin{aligned}\cos(z+w)-i\sin(z+w)&=(\cos z\cos w-\sin z\sin w)-i(\sin z\cos w+\cos z\sin w)\end{aligned}.$$

Average the two turns and take half their difference.

Adding the two equations and dividing by $2$ gives the formula for $\cos(z+w)$. Subtracting and dividing by $2i$ gives the one for $\sin(z+w)$. ∎

Moves used: add and subtract two versions

The proof in one piece

By the addition formula for $\exp$ and Euler's formula,

$$\begin{aligned}\cos(z+w)\pm i\sin(z+w)&=e^{\pm iz}e^{\pm iw}=(\cos z\pm i\sin z)(\cos w\pm i\sin w)\\&=(\cos z\cos w-\sin z\sin w)\pm i(\sin z\cos w+\cos z\sin w).\end{aligned}$$

Adding the “+” and “−” versions and dividing by $2$ gives $\cos(z+w)$; subtracting and dividing by $2i$ gives $\sin(z+w)$. ∎

$\pi$ (Satz 7.74, p. 330). There is exactly one $\pi\in(0,4)$ with $\sin\pi=0$. With it:

$$\begin{aligned}&e^{i\pi/2}=i\\&e^{i\pi}=-1\\&e^{2\pi i}=1\\&e^{i\pi/4}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}+\tfrac i{\sqrt2}\end{aligned}.$$

Periodicity (Korollar 7.75): $\sin(z+2\pi)=\sin z$, $\cos(z+2\pi)=\cos z$, $\sin(z+\pi)=-\sin z$, $\cos z=\sin\left(z+\frac\pi2\right)$. Zeros (Übung 7.76): $\sin z=0$ ⇔ $z\in\pi\mathbb Z$; $\cos z=0$ ⇔ $z\in\frac\pi2+\pi\mathbb Z$. Tangent: $\tan z=\frac{\sin z}{\cos z}$ where $\cos z\neq0$.

How the script finds $\pi$ (p. 330–331, proof on the skip list): the series give $\sin x>0$ and $\cos x>0$ on $(0,1]$ and $\sin1>\frac1{\sqrt2}$, so by the intermediate value theorem there's $p\in(0,1)$ with $\sin p=\cos p=\frac1{\sqrt2}$. Then $\pi:=4p$. No circles, no geometry: $\pi$ comes out of a power series.

Polar coordinates (Lemma 7.81, p. 333). Every $z\in\mathbb C$ is $z=re^{i\theta}$ with $r=|z|\ge0$ and an angle $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$, unique if $z\neq0$. Then multiplication is easy: $re^{i\theta}\cdot se^{i\psi}=rs\,e^{i(\theta+\psi)}$. Multiply the lengths, add the angles. This is the geometric meaning of complex multiplication you saw in lesson 5, now with a proof.

Hyperbolic functions (Section 7.7.1, p. 340). $\cosh z=\frac{e^z+e^{-z}}2$ and $\sinh z=\frac{e^z-e^{-z}}2$: the same series as $\cos$ and $\sin$, without the alternating signs. They satisfy $\cosh^2z-\sinh^2z=1$ (a hyperbola instead of a circle) and $\cosh z+\sinh z=e^z$.

7. Integrating power series term by term

Proof E: term-by-term integration (Satz 7.85, p. 339)

Theorem. Let $f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_nx^n$ have radius of convergence $R>0$. Then $F(x)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{c_n}{n+1}x^{n+1}$ has the same radius $R$, and

$$\begin{aligned}&\int_a^bf(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)\\&\text{for all }a,b\in(-R,R)\end{aligned}.$$

“Integrate each term separately; the answer is right.”

You'll need these (lessons 9, 14 and 15)
  • Integral of a power (lesson 9): $\int_a^bx^n\,dx=\frac{b^{n+1}-a^{n+1}}{n+1}$, and the integral is linear.
  • Comparison and ratio test (lesson 14); a convergent series has bounded terms (they tend to $0$).
  • Uniform convergence on $|x|\le S<R$ (Proof A), and integral and uniform limit can be swapped (lesson 15).

The idea. The integral: on $[a,b]$ the partial sums (polynomials) converge uniformly, and for polynomials term-by-term integration is just lesson 9; lesson 15 lets the limit pass through the integral. The radius: dividing by $n+1$ only helps convergence, so $F$'s radius is at least $R$. Conversely, the factor $n+1$ is too small to destroy geometric decay: beyond any point where $F$ converges, $f$ converges too.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Radius (call $F$'s radius $S$): show $S\ge R$ and $R\ge S$. Each direction: “if one series converges at $x$, the other converges at $x$ (or a little inside)”.
  • Integral: must end with “$\int_a^bf=\lim_N\int_a^bf_N$ $=\lim_N(F_N(b)-F_N(a))$ $=F(b)-F(a)$”. The gap: justify the first equality.
The storyWhat you write

Dividing each word by $n+1$ makes it weaker, so the new spell works at least wherever the old one works.

$S\ge R$. Let $|x|<R$. Then $\sum|c_nx^n|$ converges, and $\left|\frac{c_n}{n+1}x^{n+1}\right|\le|x|\,|c_nx^n|$. By comparison, $F(x)$ converges absolutely. So $S\ge R$.

Where the weakened spell still works at distance $|y|$, its words are bounded there; a bit closer in, the original words fade geometrically, and the extra factor $n+1$ can't stop that.

$R\ge S$. Let $|x|<S$ and choose $y$ with $|x|<|y|<S$. Since $F(y)$ converges, its terms are bounded: $\frac{|c_n|}{n+1}|y|^{n+1}\le M$ for all $n$. With $q=\frac{|x|}{|y|}<1$:

$$|c_nx^n|=\frac{|c_n|\,|y|^{n+1}}{n+1}\cdot\frac{n+1}{|y|}\,q^n\le\frac M{|y|}(n+1)q^n.$$

$\sum(n+1)q^n$ converges (ratio test: $\frac{n+2}{n+1}q\to q<1$), so $\sum c_nx^n$ converges absolutely. So $R\ge S$, and $S=R$.

For finitely many words, integrating word by word is just school.

Integral. Let $a,b\in(-R,R)$ and $f_N(x)=\sum_{n=0}^Nc_nx^n$, $F_N(x)=\sum_{n=0}^N\frac{c_n}{n+1}x^{n+1}$. By lesson 9, $\int_a^bf_N\,dx=F_N(b)-F_N(a)$.

On the stretch from $a$ to $b$ the partial spells converge uniformly, so the integral follows the limit.

With $S'=\max\{|a|,|b|\}<R$, Proof A gives $f_N\to f$ uniformly on $[a,b]\subseteq[-S',S']$. By lesson 15, $f$ is integrable and

$$\begin{aligned}\int_a^bf\,dx&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_a^bf_N\,dx\\&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\bigl(F_N(b)-F_N(a)\bigr)\\&=F(b)-F(a)\end{aligned},$$

since $F$ converges at $a$ and $b$ (they lie inside its radius $R$). ∎

Moves used: find a majorant go to partial sums bound independently of x

The proof in one piece

Radius. Let $S$ be the radius of $F$. If $|x|<R$, then $\left|\frac{c_n}{n+1}x^{n+1}\right|\le|x||c_nx^n|$, so $F(x)$ converges absolutely: $S\ge R$. If $|x|<|y|<S$, the terms of $F(y)$ are bounded by some $M$, so with $q=\frac{|x|}{|y|}<1$: $|c_nx^n|\le\frac M{|y|}(n+1)q^n$, and $\sum(n+1)q^n$ converges by the ratio test. So $f(x)$ converges absolutely: $R\ge S$.

Integral. For $a,b\in(-R,R)$, the partial sums $f_N$ converge uniformly to $f$ on $[a,b]$ (Proof A with $\max\{|a|,|b|\}<R$), and $\int_a^bf_N=F_N(b)-F_N(a)$. By lesson 15,

$$\begin{aligned}\int_a^bf&=\lim_N\int_a^bf_N\\&=\lim_N\bigl(F_N(b)-F_N(a)\bigr)\\&=F(b)-F(a)\end{aligned}.$$

∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. $S\ge R$: $\left|\frac{c_n}{n+1}x^{n+1}\right|\le|x|\,|c_nx^n|$, comparison.
  2. $R\ge S$: go a bit further out to $y$; terms of $F(y)$ bounded by $M$; $|c_nx^n|\le\frac M{|y|}(n+1)q^n$; ratio test on $\sum(n+1)q^n$.
  3. Polynomials: $\int_a^bf_N=F_N(b)-F_N(a)$.
  4. Uniform on $[a,b]$ (inside the radius) ⇒ swap limit and integral.

In short: “compare, step out and bound, polynomials, uniform swap”. This was the 2019 winter exam's question 11: part (a) is steps 1–2 (2 points), part (b) is steps 3–4 (3 points).

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

Exercise 2 (Einsiedler FS22, question 2(a), 2 points)

Open the original: FS22 exam

Determine the radius of convergence $\rho$ of $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(2^n+n^3\right)z^n$.

Solution

Frame: a sum with an $n$-th power, so the dominant term and the root formula. Moves: sandwich.

$\rho=\frac12$. For $n\ge10$, $n^3\le2^n$ (check $n=10$: $1000\le1024$; then by induction the left side grows by a factor $\left(\frac{n+1}n\right)^3\le1.34$, the right side by $2$). So $2^n\le2^n+n^3\le2\cdot2^n$, and taking $n$-th roots:

$$2\le\sqrt[n]{2^n+n^3}\le2\sqrt[n]2\to2.$$

By the sandwich, $\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}\to2$, so $\rho=\frac12$.

Radius of convergence of $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(2n)^{n-1}+3^n}{3^n+n^n}x^n$? (A) $\frac12$. (B) $\frac23$. (C) $\frac32$. (D) $1$.

Solution

Answer (A). Dominant terms: $(2n)^{n-1}$ on top, $n^n$ at the bottom. For large $n$, $(2n)^{n-1}\le\text{top}\le2(2n)^{n-1}$ and $n^n\le\text{bottom}\le2n^n$. So $a_n$ lies between $\frac12\cdot\frac{(2n)^{n-1}}{n^n}$ and $2\cdot\frac{(2n)^{n-1}}{n^n}$, where $\frac{(2n)^{n-1}}{n^n}=\frac{2^{n-1}}n$. The $n$-th root of $\frac{2^{n-1}}n$ is $\frac{2}{\sqrt[n]{2n}}\to2$. So $R=\frac12$.

Exercise 9 (Einsiedler HS21, question 2(b), 3 points)

Open the original: HS21 exam · solutions

Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1-\frac5n\right)^{n+\sqrt n}$.

Solution

Frame: write the power with $\exp$ and $\log$, find the limit of the exponent, use continuity of $\exp$. Moves: sandwich.

Rewrite (1 point). For $n>5$:

$$\begin{aligned}\left(1-\frac5n\right)^{n+\sqrt n}&=\exp\Bigl((n+\sqrt n)\log\bigl(1-\tfrac5n\bigr)\Bigr)\end{aligned}.$$

The exponent (1 point). Worked example 2 with $y=-\frac5n$ gives $\frac{-5/n}{1-5/n}\le\log\left(1-\frac5n\right)\le-\frac5n$, that is $-\frac5{n-5}\le\log\left(1-\frac5n\right)\le-\frac5n$. Multiply by $n+\sqrt n>0$:

$$\begin{aligned}-\frac{5(n+\sqrt n)}{n-5}&\le (n+\sqrt n)\log\left(1-\tfrac5n\right)\\&\le -\frac{5(n+\sqrt n)}n\end{aligned}.$$

Both bounds tend to $-5$ (divide by $n$; $\frac{\sqrt n}n\to0$). By the sandwich, the exponent tends to $-5$.

Conclusion (1 point). $\exp$ is continuous, so the limit is $e^{-5}$.

Exercise 10 (Einsiedler FS22, question 2(b) ii, 2 points)

Open the original: FS22 exam

Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\ln(4n+1)}{\ln(n+2)}$.

Solution

$1$. Pull out $\ln n$ with $\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$: $\ln(4n+1)=\ln n+\ln\left(4+\frac1n\right)$ and $\ln(n+2)=\ln n+\ln\left(1+\frac2n\right)$. Divide top and bottom by $\ln n$ (for $n\ge2$, $\ln n>0$):

$$\frac{1+\frac{\ln(4+1/n)}{\ln n}}{1+\frac{\ln(1+2/n)}{\ln n}}\to\frac{1+0}{1+0}=1,$$

since the small logarithms stay bounded (between $0$ and $\ln5$) while $\ln n\to\infty$.

Bonus: $e$ is irrational

Use $e=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac1{k!}$. Suppose $e=\frac pq$ with $p,q\in\mathbb N$, $q\ge2$. Consider $x=q!\left(e-\sum_{k=0}^q\frac1{k!}\right)$. Show that $x$ is an integer and that $0<x<1$.

Solution

Frame: assume the opposite, find an integer strictly between $0$ and $1$. Moves: assume the opposite find a majorant.

Integer. $q!\,e=q!\frac pq=(q-1)!\,p$ is an integer, and so is each $\frac{q!}{k!}$ for $k\le q$. So $x$ is an integer.

Between $0$ and $1$. $x=\sum_{k=q+1}^\infty\frac{q!}{k!}>0$. And $\frac{q!}{k!}=\frac1{(q+1)(q+2)\cdots k}\le\frac1{(q+1)^{k-q}}$, so by a geometric majorant

$$\begin{aligned}x&\le \sum_{j=1}^\infty\frac1{(q+1)^j}\\&=\frac{1/(q+1)}{1-1/(q+1)}\\&=\frac1q\\&<1\end{aligned}.$$

No integer lies strictly between $0$ and $1$, a contradiction. So $e$ is irrational. ∎

9. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

$e^{i\pi}+1=0$. Five of the most important numbers ($0$, $1$, $e$, $i$, $\pi$) in one equation, with addition, multiplication and powers. It isn't a coincidence or a trick. It says: the growth spell, spoken at an imaginary distance, turns instead of grows. $e^{i\theta}$ walks around the unit circle at speed $1$ (since $|e^{i\theta}|=1$), and after the distance $\pi$ it has gone halfway round, to $-1$.

Why bankers met $e$ first. Jacob Bernoulli found $e$ in 1683, studying exactly the compound interest of section 2: what happens to $\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n$ as interest is paid more and more often? He showed it stays between $2$ and $3$. Euler named it $e$, computed it to 23 digits, and found $e^{ix}=\cos x+i\sin x$.

A logarithm with too many answers. On $\mathbb C$, $e^{2\pi i}=1=e^0$, so $\exp$ is not injective and “$\log1$” could be $0$ or $2\pi i$ or $-4\pi i$… Section 7.6.5 picks one value by cutting the plane along the negative real axis, and the cut can never be avoided. This is the start of Riemann surfaces, a topic of the third year.
