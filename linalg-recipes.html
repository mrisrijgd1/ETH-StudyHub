Vectors and subspaces

The first half of the course: almost every exam has at least one of these.

Is it a subspace?

When: “Show that $U$ is a subspace” (“Zeigen Sie, dass … ein Unterraum ist”), or a true/false statement about a set.

  1. Check $0\in U$. If $0\notin U$, stop: it is not a subspace.
  2. Take arbitrary $u,w\in U$ and show $u+w\in U$, using only what membership in $U$ means.
  3. Take arbitrary $u\in U$, $\lambda\in K$ and show $\lambda u\in U$.
  4. To show it is not a subspace: one concrete counterexample to one of the three conditions is enough.

Check: Kernels, preimages of subspaces, intersections and spans are always subspaces; a union of two subspaces almost never is.

Example

$U=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb R^3: x+2y-z=0\}$.

  1. $0+0-0=0$, so $0\in U$.
  2. If $x+2y-z=0$ and $x'+2y'-z'=0$, adding gives $(x+x')+2(y+y')-(z+z')=0$.
  3. Multiplying $x+2y-z=0$ by $\lambda$ gives $\lambda x+2\lambda y-\lambda z=0$. So $U$ is a subspace.

Learn it in: lesson 5, section 6.

Independent? A basis? In the span?

When: “Show that $v_1,\dots,v_k$ form a basis”, “is $w$ in the span?”, “are these linearly independent?”

  1. Write the vectors as the columns of a matrix (polynomials or matrices: use their coordinate vectors in a standard basis).
  2. Row reduce to echelon form.
  3. Independent $\iff$ every column has a pivot. In the span of the columns: append $w$ as an extra column and check that it gets no pivot.
  4. A basis of $K^n$: $n$ vectors that are independent (or that span). With exactly $n=\dim V$ vectors, one of the two is enough.

Check: If a column has no pivot, read off the dependence from the reduced form and test it on the original vectors.

Example

$v_1=(1,2,1)$, $v_2=(2,1,0)$, $v_3=(0,3,2)$ in $\mathbb R^3$.

  1. Columns $v_1,v_2,v_3$; after elimination only two pivots, so the rank is 2.
  2. The third column is not a pivot column: $v_3=2v_1-v_2$ (check: $(2,4,2)-(2,1,0)=(0,3,2)$).
  3. Dependent, so not a basis of $\mathbb R^3$.

Learn it in: lesson 6, sections 3–4, lesson 16, section 6.

Coordinates in a basis

When: “Determine $[v]_B$”, or the first step of almost every matrix-of-a-map question.

  1. Write $v=a_1b_1+\dots+a_nb_n$ with unknown $a_i$.
  2. Compare components: this is a linear system in $a_1,\dots,a_n$.
  3. Solve it. Because $B$ is a basis, the solution exists and is unique.

Check: Put the numbers back: $a_1b_1+\dots+a_nb_n$ must give $v$.

Example

$B=((1,1),(1,-1))$, $v=(3,1)$.

  1. $a(1,1)+b(1,-1)=(3,1)$ gives $a+b=3$, $a-b=1$.
  2. $a=2$, $b=1$, so $[v]_B=(2,1)$. Check: $2(1,1)+(1,-1)=(3,1)$.

Learn it in: lesson 6, section 5.

Extend to a basis

When: “Ergänzen Sie zu einer Basis”: complete given independent vectors to a basis of $V$.

  1. Count: you need $\dim V-k$ more vectors.
  2. Try standard basis vectors (or $1,x,x^2,\dots$) one at a time; keep a vector if it is not in the span of the ones you have.
  3. Faster: write your $k$ vectors as rows, row reduce; the columns without a pivot tell you which standard vectors $e_j$ to add.

Check: With $n$ vectors in an $n$-dimensional space, independence is enough: check the determinant of the square matrix is not $0$.

Example

Extend $(1,1,0),(0,1,1)$ to a basis of $\mathbb R^3$.

  1. One vector is missing. As rows, the two vectors are already in echelon form, with pivots in columns 1 and 2. Column 3 has none, so add $e_3=(0,0,1)$.
  2. Check: the three rows form a triangular matrix with diagonal $1,1,1$, so the determinant is $1\neq0$ and they are a basis.
  3. Also fine: $e_1=(1,0,0)$, since $\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}=1\neq0$.

Learn it in: lesson 7, section 4.

Sum and intersection of two subspaces

When: “Bestimmen Sie eine Basis von $U\cap W$ und $U+W$.”

  1. Intersection: put the equations of $U$ and of $W$ together and solve the joint system. If a subspace is given by a spanning set, first turn it into equations, or solve $\sum a_iu_i=\sum b_jw_j$.
  2. Sum: put all spanning vectors of $U$ and $W$ together and thin them out to a basis (recipe “Independent?”).
  3. Use the dimension formula $\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W)$ to check, or to skip one of the two computations.

Check: The dimensions must satisfy the formula, and $\dim(U+W)\le\dim V$.

Example

$U:x+y+z=0$ and $W:x-y=0$ in $\mathbb R^3$.

  1. Joint system: $x=y$ and $z=-2x$, so $U\cap W=\langle(1,1,-2)\rangle$, dimension 1.
  2. Formula: $\dim(U+W)=2+2-1=3$, so $U+W=\mathbb R^3$ without any further work.

Learn it in: lesson 8, sections 2–3.

Linear maps and matrices

The middle of the course: the matrix of a map is the most frequent single exam task.

Is the map linear?

When: “Zeigen Sie, dass $T$ linear ist.” Usually 2–4 points, often the first part of a longer problem.

  1. Quick test first: if $T(0)\neq0$, it is not linear, done.
  2. Take arbitrary $v,w$ and show $T(v+w)=T(v)+T(w)$ by writing out the definition of $T$.
  3. Take arbitrary $v$, $\lambda$ and show $T(\lambda v)=\lambda T(v)$.
  4. Write each step as a chain of equalities and say which rule you use (derivative of a sum, matrix product distributes, …).

Check: Squares, products of two coordinates, constants and absolute values usually break linearity.

Example

$T:\mathbb R[x]_{\le3}\to\mathbb R[x]_{\le3}$, $T(p)=x\,p'$ (FS17, problem 3).

  1. $T(p+q)=x(p+q)'=x\,p'+x\,q'=T(p)+T(q)$, since the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives.
  2. $T(\lambda p)=x(\lambda p)'=\lambda\,x\,p'=\lambda T(p)$.

Learn it in: lesson 11, section 2.

The value of a map known on a basis

When: “$T$ is the linear map with $T(b_1)=\dots$, $T(b_2)=\dots$. Determine $T(v)$.” (HS20, 2 (d))

  1. Write $v$ in the basis: $v=a_1b_1+\dots+a_nb_n$ (recipe “Coordinates”).
  2. By linearity, $T(v)=a_1T(b_1)+\dots+a_nT(b_n)$.

Check: Test with $v=b_1$: you must get $T(b_1)$ back.

Example

$T(1,1)=(2,0)$ and $T(1,-1)=(0,4)$. Find $T(3,1)$.

  1. $(3,1)=2(1,1)+1(1,-1)$.
  2. $T(3,1)=2(2,0)+(0,4)=(4,4)$.

Learn it in: lesson 10, section 3, lesson 11, section 3.

The matrix of a linear map

When: “Bestimmen Sie die Darstellungsmatrix $[T]^B_C$.” In almost every exam, 4–6 points.

  1. Apply $T$ to the first basis vector $b_1$ of $B$.
  2. Write $T(b_1)$ in the basis $C$ (recipe “Coordinates”). These coordinates are the first column.
  3. Repeat for $b_2,\dots,b_n$: column $j$ is $[T(b_j)]_C$.
  4. The matrix has $\dim W$ rows and $\dim V$ columns.

Check: Pick one vector $v$ and test $[T]^B_C[v]_B=[T(v)]_C$.

Example

$T(p)=p'$ on $\mathbb R[x]_{\le2}$, $B=C=(1,x,x^2)$.

  1. $T(1)=0$, $T(x)=1$, $T(x^2)=2x$.
  2. In $C$: $(0,0,0)$, $(1,0,0)$, $(0,2,0)$. So $$[T]^B_B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&2\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

Learn it in: lesson 11, section 6.

Change of basis

When: “Bestimmen Sie die Basiswechselmatrix”, or the matrix of $T$ in a new basis.

  1. The transition matrix $Q=[\mathrm{Id}]^{B'}_B$ has the new basis vectors, written in the old basis, as its columns.
  2. For a map $T:V\to V$: $[T]_{B'}=Q^{-1}[T]_BQ$. Read right to left: translate in, apply, translate out.
  3. Between two spaces: $[T]^{B'}_{C'}=[\mathrm{Id}_W]^C_{C'}[T]^B_C[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B$.

Check: The trace and the determinant do not change: $[T]_{B'}$ and $[T]_B$ must have the same ones.

Example

$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ in the standard basis, new basis $B'=((1,1),(1,-1))$.

  1. $Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$ and $Q^{-1}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$.
  2. $Q^{-1}AQ=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Trace $4$ and determinant $3$ on both sides.

Learn it in: lesson 13, sections 2–4.

Kernel, image and rank

When: “Bestimmen Sie Kern und Bild”, “den Rang”, “eine Basis des Kerns”. In every exam.

  1. Row reduce $A$ to (reduced) echelon form.
  2. Rank = number of pivots.
  3. Kernel: solve $Ax=0$ from the echelon form; one basis vector per free variable (set it to 1, the other free ones to 0).
  4. Image: the columns of the original $A$ at the pivot positions are a basis of $\mathrm{Im}\,A$.

Check: Rank–nullity: $\dim\mathrm{Ker}+\mathrm{rank}=$ number of columns. And $A$ times each kernel vector must be $0$.

Example

$A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&1\\2&4&0\end{pmatrix}$.

  1. Reduced form $\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$: pivots in columns 1 and 3, rank 2.
  2. Free variable $x_2=1$: kernel $\langle(-2,1,0)\rangle$.
  3. Image: columns 1 and 3 of $A$, $(1,2)$ and $(1,0)$, so $\mathrm{Im}\,A=\mathbb R^2$. Check: $1+2=3$ columns.

Learn it in: lesson 15, section 3, lesson 16, section 6.

Dual basis and annihilator

When: “Bestimmen Sie die duale Basis”, “the basis $B$ whose dual basis is …”, “a basis of $U^0$”.

  1. Dual basis of a basis of $K^n$: put the basis vectors as the columns of $P$. The rows of $P^{-1}$ are the dual basis functionals.
  2. Given functionals, find the basis they are dual to: put the functionals as the rows of a matrix $F$; the columns of $F^{-1}$ are the basis.
  3. Annihilator of $U=\langle u_1,\dots,u_k\rangle\subseteq K^n$: functionals $f(x)=a_1x_1+\dots+a_nx_n$ with $f(u_i)=0$ for all $i$. Solve for $a$; $\dim U^0=n-\dim U$.

Check: Each dual functional must give $1$ on its own basis vector and $0$ on the others.

Example

Dual basis of $((1,1),(1,-1))$.

  1. $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $P^{-1}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$.
  2. $f_1(x,y)=\tfrac12(x+y)$, $f_2(x,y)=\tfrac12(x-y)$. Check: $f_1(1,1)=1$, $f_1(1,-1)=0$.

Learn it in: lesson 10, section 6, lesson 14, section 5.

Linear systems

Pure calculation, so these are the safest points in the exam.

A system or a rank with a parameter

When: “Für welche Werte von $\lambda$ ist das System lösbar / eindeutig lösbar?”, “den Rang in Abhängigkeit von $t$”. 7–15 points.

  1. Write the augmented matrix $(A\,|\,b)$ and eliminate. Swap rows so the parameter ends up as late as possible.
  2. Never divide by an expression that can be $0$ (like $t-1$) without splitting into cases first.
  3. Solvable $\iff$ no row $(0\cdots0\,|\,c)$ with $c\neq0$, that is $\mathrm{rank}\,A=\mathrm{rank}(A|b)$. Unique $\iff$ also rank $=$ number of unknowns.
  4. All solutions $=$ one particular solution $+$ the kernel of $A$.
  5. Answer each case separately, in a sentence.

Check: Plug the special values back into the original system.

Example

$x+y=1$, $x+ty=2$.

  1. Second row minus first: $(t-1)y=1$.
  2. Case $t=1$: $0=1$, no solution.
  3. Case $t\neq1$: $y=\frac1{t-1}$, $x=1-y=\frac{t-2}{t-1}$, exactly one solution.

Learn it in: lesson 17, section 2, lesson 16, section 3.

The inverse matrix (Gauss–Jordan)

When: “Bestimmen Sie die Inverse”, often as a 4-point short answer.

  1. Write $(A\,|\,I_n)$.
  2. Row reduce the left half to $I_n$, doing every operation on the whole row.
  3. The right half is now $A^{-1}$. If a zero row appears on the left, $A$ is not invertible.

Check: Multiply: $AA^{-1}=I_n$. For $2\times2$: $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\frac1{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$.

Example

$A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&5\end{pmatrix}$.

  1. $R_2-3R_1$: $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&|&1&0\\0&-1&|&-3&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$; then $R_2\cdot(-1)$ and $R_1-2R_2$.
  2. $A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-5&2\\3&-1\end{pmatrix}$. Check with the $2\times2$ formula: $ad-bc=-1$.

Learn it in: lesson 17, Proof F.

LR decomposition

When: “Bestimmen Sie eine LR-Zerlegung und lösen Sie damit $Av=b$.” (FS18, problem 5)

  1. Eliminate below the diagonal without swapping rows. $R$ is the upper triangular result.
  2. $L$ is lower triangular with 1 on the diagonal; below it, entry $(i,j)$ is the multiple of row $j$ you subtracted from row $i$.
  3. To solve $Av=b$: first $Ly=b$ from the top down, then $Rv=y$ from the bottom up.

Check: Multiply $LR$: you must get $A$ back.

Example

$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\4&5\end{pmatrix}$.

  1. $R_2-2R_1$ gives $R=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$; the multiplier 2 goes into $L=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$.
  2. Check: $LR=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\4&5\end{pmatrix}=A$.

Learn it in: lesson 17, section 4.

Determinants and eigenvalues

The end of the course; every exam has a determinant, about half have eigenvalues.

Computing a determinant

When: “Berechnen Sie $\det A$”, a short answer, or an $n\times n$ pattern to prove.

  1. Small matrix: $2\times2$ formula, or Sarrus for $3\times3$.
  2. Larger: row reduce to triangular form. Adding a multiple of a row changes nothing; a swap changes the sign; multiplying a row by $c$ multiplies the determinant by $c$. Then multiply the diagonal.
  3. A row or column with many zeros: expand along it, with signs $(-1)^{i+j}$.
  4. Pattern “$a$ on the diagonal, $b$ elsewhere”: add all columns to the first (every entry becomes $a+(n-1)b$), pull that out, subtract the first row from the others.
  5. $n\times n$ with $n$ in it: compute $n=1,2,3$, guess, prove by induction with an expansion.

Check: $\det(AB)=\det A\det B$, $\det A^T=\det A$, $\det(A^{-1})=1/\det A$; a matrix with two equal rows has determinant 0.

Example

$A=\begin{pmatrix}a&1&1\\1&a&1\\1&1&a\end{pmatrix}$.

  1. Add columns 2 and 3 to column 1: every entry of column 1 is $a+2$; pull it out.
  2. Subtract row 1 from rows 2 and 3: the triangular matrix has diagonal $1,\,a-1,\,a-1$.
  3. $\det A=(a+2)(a-1)^2$. Check $a=1$: three equal rows, determinant 0.

Learn it in: lesson 18, section 3, lesson 20, section 3.

Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, diagonalising

When: “Bestimmen Sie das charakteristische Polynom, die Eigenwerte und Eigenvektoren”, “ist $A$ diagonalisierbar?”

  1. Characteristic polynomial: $p_A(x)=\det(A-xI_n)$. Its zeros in $K$ are the eigenvalues.
  2. For each eigenvalue $\lambda$: the eigenspace is $\mathrm{Ker}(A-\lambda I_n)$ (recipe “Kernel”).
  3. Diagonalisable $\iff$ there is a basis of eigenvectors, that is the eigenspace dimensions add up to $n$. $n$ different eigenvalues is enough.
  4. Then $P$ = eigenvectors as columns, and $P^{-1}AP$ is diagonal with the eigenvalues in the same order.

Check: Sum of the eigenvalues (with multiplicity) $=$ trace, product $=$ determinant. And $Av=\lambda v$ for each eigenvector you found.

Example

$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$.

  1. $p_A(x)=(2-x)^2-1=(x-1)(x-3)$, eigenvalues 1 and 3.
  2. $\mathrm{Ker}(A-I_2)=\langle(1,-1)\rangle$, $\mathrm{Ker}(A-3I_2)=\langle(1,1)\rangle$.
  3. Two different eigenvalues, so diagonalisable. Check: $1+3=4$ is the trace, $1\cdot3=3$ the determinant.

Learn it in: lesson 21, the recipe.
