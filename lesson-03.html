Recap of lesson 2 (5 min)

  • Injective: $f(x_1)=f(x_2)\Rightarrow x_1=x_2$. Surjective: $\forall y\ \exists x:\ f(x)=y$. Bijective: both; then $f^{-1}$ exists.
  • Templates: “Let $x_1,x_2$ with $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$ … hence $x_1=x_2$” and “Let $y\in Y$. Set $x:=\dots$ Then $f(x)=y$.”
  • Relays: $g\circ f$ injective ⇒ $f$ injective; $g\circ f$ surjective ⇒ $g$ surjective.
  • Preimage respects $\cup$, $\cap$, complement; the image only $\cup$ (unless $f$ is injective).

  1. Is $f:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z$, $f(n)=2n$ injective? Surjective?

    Answer

    Injective: $2n_1=2n_2\Rightarrow n_1=n_2$. Not surjective: $1$ is not of the form $2n$ with $n\in\mathbb Z$.

  2. What is $f^{-1}(\{1,3,5\})$ for this $f$?

    Answer

    $\emptyset$: no integer is sent to an odd number. Preimages can be empty, and they exist even though $f$ is not invertible.

1. The story, part one: the Great Houses

The realm $X$ is full of people, and the old law sorts them into Great Houses. Not every rule for “kinship” produces Houses that make sense. The Heralds' Charter says a kinship rule is valid exactly when it obeys three laws: everyone is kin to themselves; kinship goes both ways; and the kin of my kin is my kin.

When all three hold, the realm splits cleanly: your House is everyone kin to you, any member can speak for the House, and the Council of Houses has one seat per House, not per person.

In maths: the kinship rule is a relation $\sim$, the three laws are the axioms below, your House is the class $[x]$, a member who speaks for it is a representative, and the Council is the quotient $X/\!\sim$.

2. Equivalence relations

Definition (p. 41). A relation $\sim$ on $X$ is an equivalence relation if it is

  1. reflexive: $\forall x\in X:\ x\sim x$;
  2. symmetric: $\forall x,y\in X:\ x\sim y\Rightarrow y\sim x$;
  3. transitive: $$\forall x,y,z\in X:\ (x\sim y\wedge y\sim z)\Rightarrow x\sim z.$$

Definition (p. 44). The class of $x$ is $[x]=\{y\in X\mid y\sim x\}$, and the quotient $X/\!\sim\ =\{[x]\mid x\in X\}$ is the set of all classes. The script's picture is pots: equivalent things go in the same pot, and $X/\!\sim$ is the set of pots.

Why all three laws are needed. Each one stops a specific kind of chaos:

  • Without reflexivity, someone could be in no House at all (not even their own).
  • Without symmetry, “I'm in your House” wouldn't mean “you're in mine”.
  • Without transitivity, Houses overlap and blur. Think of “lives less than 1 km from”: you might be close to me and to someone else who is 2 km from me. That chain of closeness never closes into separate Houses.

Rule of thumb: “$x$ and $y$ have the same ___” is almost always an equivalence relation. “$x$ is smaller than / near / disjoint from $y$” almost never is.

Proof A: “same remainder mod 3” (the three-bullet template)

Claim. On $\mathbb Z$ define $a\sim b\iff 3\mid a-b$ (that is, $a-b=3k$ for some $k\in\mathbb Z$). Then $\sim$ is an equivalence relation.

The idea. Each axiom becomes a small fact about multiples of 3: $0$ is a multiple of 3, the negative of a multiple of 3 is one, and the sum of two multiples of 3 is one.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Three bullets, one per axiom. Each starts with “Let …” (the $\forall$ of that axiom) and its assumption, unfolds “$\sim$” into its definition, and ends with the conclusion.
  • Unfolding: $a\sim b$ always becomes “$a-b=3k$ for some $k\in\mathbb Z$”, with a fresh letter for each new assumption.
The storyWhat you write

Everyone is kin to themselves: the difference is nothing.

Reflexive. Let $a\in\mathbb Z$. Then $a-a=0=3\cdot0$, so $a\sim a$.

Kinship both ways: turning the difference around just flips its sign.

Symmetric. Let $a\sim b$, so $a-b=3k$ with $k\in\mathbb Z$. Then $b-a=3(-k)$ and $-k\in\mathbb Z$, so $b\sim a$.

Kin of my kin: two steps of multiples of 3 add up to one.

Transitive. Let $a\sim b$ and $b\sim c$, so $a-b=3k$ and $b-c=3l$ with $k,l\in\mathbb Z$. Adding gives $a-c=3(k+l)$ with $k+l\in\mathbb Z$, so $a\sim c$. ∎

Moves used: the three-bullet check

The proof in one piece

Reflexive: for $a\in\mathbb Z$, $a-a=3\cdot0$. Symmetric: if $a-b=3k$, then $b-a=3(-k)$. Transitive: if $a-b=3k$ and $b-c=3l$, then $a-c=(a-b)+(b-c)=3(k+l)$. In each case the factor is an integer, so $\sim$ is an equivalence relation. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Three labelled bullets: reflexive, symmetric, transitive.
  2. In each: “Let …”, unfold $\sim$ with a fresh letter, compute, fold back.
  3. Transitivity is the only one with content: add the two unfolded equations.

The classes here are $[0]=\{\dots,-3,0,3,6,\dots\}$, $[1]=\{\dots,-2,1,4,\dots\}$, $[2]=\{\dots,-1,2,5,\dots\}$. The quotient has exactly three elements, and $[1]=[4]=[-2]$: the same House with different representatives.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). In the transitivity bullet, why do we need two letters $k$ and $l$, not one?

    Answer

    The two assumptions give two possibly different multiples of 3. Using the same $k$ for both would secretly assume $a-b=b-c$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Replace 3 by any $m\in\mathbb N$. Does the proof change?

    Answer

    No: replace every 3 by $m$. “Same remainder mod $m$” is an equivalence relation with $m$ classes.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the three bullets from the frame alone.

Proof B: two equivalence classes are equal or disjoint (two Houses; the heart of Proposition 1.67)

Claim. Let $\sim$ be an equivalence relation on $X$ and $x,y\in X$. Then $x\sim y\iff[x]=[y]\iff[x]\cap[y]\neq\emptyset$. In particular, two classes are either equal or disjoint.

The idea. Prove the circle (1) ⇒ (2) ⇒ (3) ⇒ (1). If $x$ and $y$ are kin, everyone kin to one is kin to the other (transitivity). Equal classes are non-empty, because $x\in[x]$ (reflexivity). And a shared member $z$ links $x$ to $y$ (symmetry, then transitivity).

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Three equivalent statements: prove a circle of three implications instead of six.
  • $[x]=[y]$ is a set equality: show $[x]\subseteq[y]$ (“let $z\in[x]$ … so $z\in[y]$”) and the reverse.
  • $z\in[x]$ means $z\sim x$. Every step is unfolding membership and using one axiom.
The storyWhat you write

If $x$ and $y$ are kin, anyone in $x$'s House is in $y$'s.

(1) ⇒ (2). Let $x\sim y$ and $z\in[x]$, so $z\sim x$. By transitivity $z\sim y$, so $z\in[y]$. Hence $[x]\subseteq[y]$.

And the other way round, since kinship goes both ways.

By symmetry $y\sim x$, and the same argument gives $[y]\subseteq[x]$. So $[x]=[y]$.

A House is never empty: its founder lives there.

(2) ⇒ (3). By reflexivity $x\in[x]=[y]$, so $x\in[x]\cap[y]\neq\emptyset$.

Someone in both Houses links the two founders.

(3) ⇒ (1). Let $z\in[x]\cap[y]$, so $z\sim x$ and $z\sim y$. By symmetry $x\sim z$, and by transitivity $x\sim y$. ∎

Moves used: prove a circle of implications set equality by two inclusions

The proof in one piece

(1) ⇒ (2): let $x\sim y$. If $z\in[x]$, then $z\sim x$ and $x\sim y$ give $z\sim y$, so $z\in[y]$; thus $[x]\subseteq[y]$. Since $y\sim x$ by symmetry, also $[y]\subseteq[x]$. (2) ⇒ (3): $x\in[x]=[y]$ by reflexivity. (3) ⇒ (1): if $z\sim x$ and $z\sim y$, then $x\sim z$ by symmetry and $x\sim y$ by transitivity. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Each axiom is used at least once. Where is each one used?

    Answer

    Reflexivity: (2) ⇒ (3). Symmetry: the reverse inclusion in (1) ⇒ (2), and in (3) ⇒ (1). Transitivity: (1) ⇒ (2) and (3) ⇒ (1).

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Why does the claim show that the classes form a partition?

    Answer

    Every class is non-empty ($x\in[x]$), every $x$ lies in some class (its own), and two different classes can't intersect, by (3) ⇒ (2). That's exactly a partition: non-empty, pairwise disjoint pieces covering $X$.

  3. Rung 3 (4 min). Write the circle from the frame alone.

Classes = territories (Proposition 1.67, p. 44). A partition of $X$ (Definition 1.51, p. 34) is a collection of non-empty, pairwise disjoint subsets that together cover $X$, like a map of territories. Proof B shows that the classes of an equivalence relation form a partition. Conversely, given a partition, “$x\sim y\iff x$ and $y$ lie in the same piece” is an equivalence relation. So the two notions are the same thing seen from two sides.

Why mathematicians love this. Equivalence relations let you build new worlds by deciding what counts as “the same”. Fractions are the famous example (Beispiel 1.71, p. 46): $(1,2)$ and $(2,4)$ are different pairs of integers, but we declare $(a,b)\sim(c,d)\iff ad=bc$, and each class is a rational number. Exercise 2 builds the integers from $\mathbb N$ the same way.

3. The story, part two: the Endless Roll Call

The Herald's job is to count the army. For a finite army, the Herald pairs the soldiers with the numbers $1,\dots,n$. If there are more ravens than roosts, two ravens share a roost.

Now the army is infinite. The Herald can't finish, but can they at least promise that everyone will be called eventually? Some armies that look bigger than $\mathbb N$ can be called. One army can't, and a shapeshifter proves it.

4. Finite and countable sets

Definitions (p. 49 and p. 51). $X$ has $n$ elements if there is a bijection $\{1,\dots,n\}\to X$. $X$ is countable (countably infinite) if there is a bijection $\mathbb N\to X$. An infinite set that is not countable is uncountable.

Pigeonhole principle (Proposition 1.75, p. 49). If $|X|>|Y|$ (both finite), there is no injective map $X\to Y$.

The pigeonhole principle sounds obvious, but it is what makes “size” well-defined, and it solves surprising puzzles (Exercise 4). Some infinite sets look bigger than $\mathbb N$ but aren't:

  • $\mathbb Z$: call them as $0,1,-1,2,-2,3,\dots$ Everyone gets called (Worked example 2 writes this as a formula).
  • $\mathbb N\times\mathbb N$ (a grid of soldiers): you can't go row by row, because the first row never ends. Go diagonal by diagonal instead: $(1,1)$; $(1,2),(2,1)$; $(1,3),(2,2),(3,1)$; … Every diagonal is finite, so everyone gets called (Übung 1.81, p. 52).
  • $\mathbb Q$: each positive fraction $\frac pq$ sits at the grid point $(p,q)$, so $\mathbb Q$ is “at most a grid” and countable.
  • Subsets of a countable set are finite or countable (Lemma 1.80, p. 51). Thinning out an army never makes it harder to count.

Proof C: $\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ is uncountable (the army that can't be counted; Übung 1.83, p. 54)

Claim. The set $\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ of all infinite sequences (banners) of the letters A and B, like ABBABAA…, is uncountable.

The idea. Suppose a Herald claims a complete roll call: banner 1, banner 2, banner 3, … A shapeshifter builds a new banner: at position $n$ it uses the opposite letter of banner $n$ at position $n$. It differs from every banner on the list, at least at one position, but it's a valid banner. So no list is complete.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • “Uncountable” is a “there is no” statement: there is no bijection $\mathbb N\to\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$. So argue by contradiction: “Suppose $f:\mathbb N\to\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ is a bijection.”
  • The contradiction will be that $f$ is not surjective: “there is a banner $s$ with $f(n)\neq s$ for all $n$” (the negation of surjectivity, from lesson 2's recap).
The storyWhat you write

A Herald claims a complete roll call of banners.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $f:\mathbb N\to\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ is a bijection. Write $f(n)=(f(n)_1,f(n)_2,\dots)$.

The shapeshifter walks down the diagonal and flips each letter.

Define $s\in\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ by $s_n=B$ if $f(n)_n=A$, and $s_n=A$ if $f(n)_n=B$.

The new banner differs from banner $n$ at position $n$, for every $n$.

For every $n\in\mathbb N$, $s_n\neq f(n)_n$, so $s\neq f(n)$.

So it was never called, and the roll call wasn't complete.

Hence $s$ is not in the image of $f$, so $f$ is not surjective, contradicting that $f$ is a bijection. So $\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ is uncountable (it is infinite, since it contains AAA…, BAA…, BBA…, …). ∎

Moves used: assume the opposite the diagonal argument

The proof in one piece

Suppose $f:\mathbb N\to\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ is a bijection. Define $s$ by $s_n=B$ if $f(n)_n=A$ and $s_n=A$ otherwise. Then $s_n\neq f(n)_n$, so $s\neq f(n)$ for every $n$, and $f$ is not surjective. This contradiction shows that $\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ is uncountable. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Assume a bijection (or just a surjection) $f:\mathbb N\to$ the set.
  2. Build $s$ by changing the $n$-th entry of $f(n)$, for every $n$.
  3. Conclude $s\neq f(n)$ for all $n$: not surjective, contradiction.

In short: “list, flip the diagonal, the flipped one is missing”. The same trick shows that $\mathbb R$ is uncountable, because decimal expansions are banners too (lesson 6 does it with nested intervals instead).

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why does $s$ differ from $f(5)$?

    Answer

    At position 5: $s_5$ is the opposite of $f(5)_5$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). The proof only used that $f$ is surjective, not injective. What does that mean for the result?

    Answer

    Even a surjection $\mathbb N\to\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ is impossible: no list, even with repetitions, contains every banner.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the proof from the frame alone.

5. Worked examples

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

Worked example 1: Felder FS23, MC1 (2 points)

Let $M$ be a finite non-empty set and $\mathcal P(M)$ its power set. Which is an equivalence relation on $\mathcal P(M)$? (A) $A\sim B\iff A\subseteq B$. (B) $A\sim B\iff|A|=|B|$. (C) $A\sim B\iff A\cap B=\emptyset$. (D) $A\sim B\iff A\cup B=M$.

  1. Which axiom is usually the quickest to test? Test it on (C) and (D).

    Answer

    Reflexivity: just put $B=A$. (C) fails: $A\cap A=A\neq\emptyset$ for non-empty $A$. (D) fails: $A\cup A=A\neq M$ for $A\neq M$ (e.g. $A=\emptyset$).

  2. (A) is reflexive and transitive. Which axiom fails?

    Answer

    Symmetry: $\emptyset\subseteq M$, but $M\not\subseteq\emptyset$ since $M$ is non-empty.

  3. Why is (B) an equivalence relation?

    Answer

    “Same size” is “equal in one aspect”: all three axioms follow from those of $=$ on numbers. Answer: B.

Worked example 2: $\mathbb Z$ is countable, as a formula

Define $f:\mathbb N\to\mathbb Z$ (with $\mathbb N=\{1,2,3,\dots\}$) by $f(n)=\frac n2$ for even $n$ and $f(n)=-\frac{n-1}2$ for odd $n$.

  1. Compute $f(1),\dots,f(5)$. Which roll call is this?

    Answer

    $0,1,-1,2,-2$: the roll call $0,1,-1,2,-2,\dots$ from section 4.

  2. Surjective: given $z\in\mathbb Z$, which $n$ do you choose? (Two cases.)

    Answer

    If $z>0$, take $n=2z$ (even), so $f(n)=z$. If $z\le0$, take $n=1-2z$ (odd, and $\ge1$), so $f(n)=-\frac{-2z}2=z$.

  3. Injective: why can't an even $n$ and an odd $m$ collide? And why can't two of the same parity?

    Answer

    Even $n$ give values $>0$, odd $n$ give values $\le0$, so different parities never collide. Within one parity, $n\mapsto\frac n2$ and $n\mapsto-\frac{n-1}2$ are injective. So $f$ is a bijection and $\mathbb Z$ is countable. ∎

Why it's cool. Equivalence classes are how new number systems are built: a rational number literally is the class of all fractions $\frac ab$ with $\frac ab\sim\frac cd\iff ad=bc$, and “same remainder mod $m$” gives clock arithmetic. The diagonal argument is one of the most famous ideas in mathematics: the same trick shows that $\mathbb R$ is uncountable (lesson 6).

6. Exercises

Each solution is folded under its exercise. Exercises 1, 2 (i) and 3 count for scoring.

Exercise 1 (Einsiedler-style true/false block, 3 points)

Which of these are equivalence relations on $\mathbb R$? (a) $x\sim y\iff x-y\in\mathbb Z$. (b) $x\sim y\iff|x-y|<1$. (c) $x\sim y\iff xy>0$. (d) $x\sim y\iff x\le y$.

Solution
  • (a) True. Reflexive: $x-x=0\in\mathbb Z$. Symmetric: $y-x=-(x-y)\in\mathbb Z$. Transitive: $x-z=(x-y)+(y-z)\in\mathbb Z$. The classes are “same fractional part”, and the quotient is a circle.
  • (b) False: not transitive, since $0\sim0.6$ and $0.6\sim1.2$, but not $0\sim1.2$.
  • (c) False: not reflexive at $x=0$, since $0\cdot0=0$. (On $\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$ it would be one, with two classes: positives and negatives. Trap: always check the full set.)
  • (d) False: not symmetric ($1\le2$ but not $2\le1$).

So T, F, F, F.

Exercise 2 (proof, like the weekly sheets): building $\mathbb Z$ from $\mathbb N$

On $\mathbb N\times\mathbb N$ define $(a,b)\sim(c,d)\iff a+d=b+c$. (i) Prove that $\sim$ is an equivalence relation (three-bullet template; transitivity is the only step with real content). (ii) List three elements of the class $[(3,1)]$ and three of $[(1,1)]$. Which integer does each class “want to be”?

Solution

(i) Frame: three labelled bullets; in each, “Let …”, unfold $\sim$ into its equation, compute, fold back. Moves: the three-bullet check.

Reflexive: $a+b=b+a$, so $(a,b)\sim(a,b)$. Symmetric: if $a+d=b+c$, then $c+b=d+a$, so $(c,d)\sim(a,b)$. Transitive: let $(a,b)\sim(c,d)$ and $(c,d)\sim(e,f)$, so $a+d=b+c$ and $c+f=d+e$. Adding gives $a+d+c+f=b+c+d+e$. Cancelling $c+d$ (allowed in $\mathbb N$) gives $a+f=b+e$, so $(a,b)\sim(e,f)$. ∎

(ii) $[(3,1)]\ni(3,1),(4,2),(5,3)$; it “wants to be” $3-1=2$. $[(1,1)]\ni(1,1),(2,2),(7,7)$; it wants to be $0$. The pair $(a,b)$ secretly means $a-b$. Negative numbers appear as classes like $[(1,3)]=-2$, even though $\mathbb N$ has no negatives. This is how $\mathbb Z$ can be built from $\mathbb N$.

Exercise 3 (Felder FS23, MC2, the parts that belong to this lesson)

Open the original: FS23 exam with solutions

True or false? (a) $\mathbb R$ has no countably infinite subsets. (b) Every countably infinite subset of $\mathbb R$ is unbounded.

Solution
  • (a) False: $\mathbb N\subseteq\mathbb R$ is countably infinite.
  • (b) False: $\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]$ is countably infinite (an infinite subset of the countable set $\mathbb Q$, and subsets of a countable set are finite or countable) and bounded, by $0$ and $1$.

In the actual exam, MC2 had two more options about sequences. The correct answer was “every Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb R$ is bounded”, which you'll meet in lesson 12.

Exercise 4 (pigeonhole principle)

Show that among any 5 integers, two of them leave the same remainder when divided by 4. Name the set you distribute, the set of boxes, and the map.

Solution

The set: the 5 integers (the ravens). The boxes: the 4 possible remainders $\{0,1,2,3\}$ (the roosts). The map: each integer $\mapsto$ its remainder. Since $5>4$, by the pigeonhole principle this map is not injective, so two of the integers share a remainder. ∎ (In the language of Proof A with 4 instead of 3: two of them are in the same class mod 4.)

Bonus

True or false? (a) $\mathbb N\times\mathbb N$ is countable. (b) Every subset of $\mathbb Q$ is finite or countable. (c) $\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ is countable. (d) The “proof” in Übung 1.64 (p. 43), that symmetry and transitivity imply reflexivity, is correct.

Solution
  • (a) True: list the pairs diagonal by diagonal, $(1,1)$; $(1,2),(2,1)$; $(1,3),(2,2),(3,1)$; … Every diagonal is finite, so every pair gets a number (Section 4).
  • (b) True: $\mathbb Q$ is countable, and a subset of a countable set is finite or countable (Section 4).
  • (c) False: it is uncountable, by the diagonal argument of Proof C.
  • (d) False: the “proof” assumes there is some $y$ with $x\sim y$, but an $x$ might be related to nothing. Counterexample: the empty relation on a non-empty set is symmetric and transitive (vacuously, as in lesson 1) but not reflexive.

Scoring yourself: Exercise 1 (4 answers), Exercise 2 (i) (3 axioms) and Exercise 3 (2 answers) make 9. If you have 7 of 9, you're done. Exercise 4 and the Bonus are for depth and confidence and don't count.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Your phone runs on classes mod $n$. The “same remainder” relation from Proof A is modular arithmetic. It's how clocks work (13 o'clock is in the same class as 1 o'clock, mod 12). It's how the check digit on your IBAN or ISBN catches typos. And it's the core of RSA encryption, which protects every HTTPS connection and banking app: there you compute in the quotient $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ for an $n$ with hundreds of digits.

Infinity comes in sizes. Cantor's diagonal argument shocked mathematicians in 1891: there are more real numbers than fractions, even though both sets are infinite. Then came a stranger question: is there a size strictly between $\mathbb N$ and $\mathbb R$? This is the Continuum Hypothesis. In 1963 Paul Cohen proved that the usual axioms of mathematics can neither prove nor disprove it, and he won a Fields Medal for it.
