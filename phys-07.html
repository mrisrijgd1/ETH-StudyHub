Why it matters for the exam

Angular momentum and the centre of mass run through the whole second half of the course: rigid bodies, rolling, collisions, planets. The multiple-choice part asks about conservation of angular momentum almost every year (the skater in HS25, Kepler's second law in HS25 and FS25, the general statement in FS24), and the long problems ask for a centre of mass (the barbell in HS25) or a torque from $\dot{\vec L}$ (the space elevator in FS26). Recap of lesson 6 (3 min)

  1. When is mechanical energy $T+U$ conserved?

    Answer

    When only conservative forces do work. Forces perpendicular to the motion (normal force, string tension) may be present; friction may not.

  2. From what height must a ball start to make a loop of radius $R$?

    Answer

    $h\ge\frac52R$: at the top, $v^2\ge gR$, and energy conservation gives $mgh=\frac12mv^2+2mgR$.

  3. Which law corresponds to the symmetry “physics is the same at every place”?

    Answer

    Conservation of momentum (Noether). Today: the same for directions gives conservation of angular momentum.

1. The story: the dancer's spin

On the last evening of the festival, a dancer performs in the courtyard. She turns on one heel, slowly, with her arms wide, the bells on her ankles barely ringing. Then she pulls her arms to her chest, and suddenly she spins so fast that her skirt flies out in a disc and the bells blur into one sound. Nobody pushed her. When she opens her arms again, she slows down.

Beside her, a juggler throws a club high into the air, spinning end over end. The children watch the handle and the head tumble around, but the old engineer points at a mark painted on the club's middle: “Watch only this,” he says. That mark flies a perfectly smooth arc, as calm as a thrown stone.

In physics: the dancer's angular momentum $\vec L$ stays constant, because the floor exerts almost no torque about her axis. Pulling her arms in reduces her moment of inertia, so her rotation speeds up. The juggler's mark is the club's centre of mass: whatever the parts do among themselves, the centre of mass moves as if the whole mass sat there and only the external force (gravity) acted on it. Rotation and the motion of the centre separate cleanly.

2. Angular momentum and torque of a point mass

Definition (section 5.2.1, page 129). Fix a reference point O and let $\vec r$ point from O to the mass $m$. The angular momentum about O is

$$\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p=m\,\vec r\times\vec v.$$
  • Only the part of $\vec p$ perpendicular to $\vec r$ counts: $|\vec L|=r\,p_\perp=r_\perp\,p$, where $r_\perp$ is the distance of the line of motion from O. A mass moving straight at O has $\vec L=0$.
  • The direction is given by the right-hand rule: curl the fingers in the direction of motion around O, the thumb points along $\vec L$. In the $xy$-plane, $L_z=xp_y-yp_x$: positive for counter-clockwise motion.
  • $\vec L$ depends on the choice of O. Always say about which point. A body moving in a straight line has angular momentum about a point beside its path (and it's constant, as we'll see).
  • Unit: $\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}=\mathrm{N\,m\,s}$.

The angular momentum theorem (Drallsatz, page 130). Differentiate with the product rule:

$$\dot{\vec L}=\underbrace{\dot{\vec r}\times\vec p}_{=m\,\vec v\times\vec v=0}+\vec r\times\dot{\vec p}=\vec r\times\vec F=:\vec M.$$

$\vec M=\vec r\times\vec F$ is the torque of the force about O, $|\vec M|=rF\sin\varphi$, unit $\mathrm{N\,m}$. Rupp and the formula sheet write $\vec M$; many books write $\vec\tau$. The theorem $\dot{\vec L}=\vec M$ is Newton's second law for rotation, and like $\dot{\vec p}=\vec F$ it hands you a conservation law:

Conservation of angular momentum

If the total torque about O vanishes, $\vec L$ about O is constant, in size and direction. This happens for a free particle ($\vec F=0$), and for every central force, a force along $\vec r$ (towards or away from O): then $\vec r\times\vec F=0$. Gravity of the sun on a planet is central, so the planet's $\vec L$ about the sun is constant.

Worked example 1: angular momentum of a straight line

A puck of mass $m$ slides with constant velocity $v\,\hat x$ along the line $y=b$. Find its angular momentum about the origin.

  1. Compute $\vec L=m\,\vec r\times\vec v$ with $\vec r=(vt,\,b,\,0)$.

    Answer

    $\vec r\times\vec v=(vt,b,0)\times(v,0,0)$ $=(0,\,0,\,vt\cdot0-b\,v)$ $=(0,0,-bv)$. So $\vec L=-mbv\,\hat z$.

  2. Interpret the sign and why it doesn't change.

    Answer

    Seen from above, the puck passes the origin clockwise (to the right, above it): $L_z<0$. No force, no torque, so $\vec L$ is constant; $|\vec L|=mvb$, momentum times the distance $b$ of the line from O.

Circular motion (section 5.2.2, page 131). On a circle around O, $\vec v=\vec\omega\times\vec r$ and $\vec r\perp\vec\omega$, so

$$\vec L=m\,\vec r\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r)=m\,r^2\,\vec\omega.$$

(The middle step is the “bac–cab” rule, $\vec a\times(\vec b\times\vec c)=\vec b(\vec a\cdot\vec c)-\vec c(\vec a\cdot\vec b)$, with $\vec r\cdot\vec\omega=0$.) The factor $I=mr^2$ is the moment of inertia of the point mass: $L=I\omega$, just like $p=mv$. Rupp writes $L_z=I\dot\varphi$ and the rotational energy $T_{\text{rot}}=\frac12I\omega^2$, which is the ordinary $\frac12mv^2$ with $v=r\omega$. Extended bodies, with $I=\int r_\perp^2\,dm$, are lessons 10–11.

Try it: pulling the arms in

A dancer of moment of inertia $1.2\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ (body) holds a $3\,\mathrm{kg}$ weight in each hand. She starts with arms out at $0.8\,\mathrm m$, turning once per two seconds. Pull the arms in.

$L=I\omega$ stays fixed, so $\omega$ grows as $I$ shrinks. The kinetic energy $T=\frac{L^2}{2I}$ grows too: the dancer's arm muscles do work pulling the weights inwards against the centrifugal pull.

The area law (section 5.2.3, page 132). In the time $dt$ the position vector sweeps the thin triangle $dA=\frac12|\vec r\times d\vec r|=\frac12|\vec r\times\vec v|\,dt$. So the areal velocity is

$$\dot A=\frac12|\vec r\times\vec v|=\frac{L}{2m}.$$

Constant angular momentum means equal areas in equal times: Kepler's second law. It holds for any central force, not only gravity, and it explains why a planet is fastest at the point closest to the sun.

Try it: equal areas in equal times

A planet on an ellipse around the sun (at a focus). The coloured sectors are swept in equal times. Change the eccentricity.

Near the sun the sectors are short and fat, far away long and thin, but every sector has the same area. The planet moves through each in the same time, so it's fastest near the sun.

3. Systems of particles and the centre of mass

Internal and external forces (section 6.2, page 135). For particle $i$ of a system of $N$,

$$m_i\ddot{\vec r}_i=\sum_{k\neq i}\vec F_{ik}+\vec F_{\text{ext},i},$$

where $\vec F_{ik}$ is the force of particle $k$ on $i$. Sum over all $i$: by Newton's third law $\vec F_{ki}=-\vec F_{ik}$, so the internal forces cancel in pairs, and

$$\sum_im_i\ddot{\vec r}_i=\sum_i\vec F_{\text{ext},i}=\vec F_{\text{ext}}.$$

The centre of mass is the mass-weighted average position,

$$\vec r_S=\frac1{m_{\text{tot}}}\sum_im_i\vec r_i,\qquad m_{\text{tot}}=\sum_im_i,$$

and the sum above becomes the centre-of-mass theorem (Schwerpunktsatz):

Centre-of-mass theorem $$m_{\text{tot}}\,\ddot{\vec r}_S=\vec F_{\text{ext}},\qquad \vec P=\sum\vec p_i=m_{\text{tot}}\,\vec v_S.$$

The centre of mass moves like a single point carrying the total mass, pushed only by the external forces. Without external forces it moves in a straight line at constant speed, and the total momentum is conserved. Where on the body an external force acts doesn't matter for $\vec r_S$ (it matters for the rotation).

Centre of mass = centre of gravity (page 138). In a uniform field $\vec g$, the total torque of gravity about $S$ is $\sum\vec r_i\,'\times m_i\vec g=\left(\sum m_i\vec r_i\,'\right)\times\vec g=0$, since $\sum m_i\vec r_i\,'=0$ about the centre of mass. So a body supported at its centre of mass balances in every orientation; that's how you find $S$ experimentally. For a continuous body, $\vec r_S=\frac1M\int\vec r\,dm$ (lesson 10).

Worked example 2: the juggler's club

Model the club as a light rod of length $d$ with a mass $m_1$ (handle) at one end and $m_2=3m_1$ (head) at the other. It is thrown spinning. Where is the centre of mass, and what path does it follow?

  1. Put the handle at $x=0$, the head at $x=d$. Find $x_S$.

    Answer

    $x_S=\frac{m_1\cdot0+3m_1\cdot d}{4m_1}=\frac34d$: three quarters of the way to the head, closer to the heavier end.

  2. Which external forces act in flight, and what does the centre of mass do?

    Answer

    Only gravity, $\vec F_{\text{ext}}=4m_1\vec g$ (neglecting air). So $\ddot{\vec r}_S=\vec g$: the centre of mass flies a parabola, exactly like a stone (lesson 1). The spinning is internal and doesn't affect it.

Try it: the thrown club

Two masses on a light rod, thrown spinning. Choose the mass ratio and press Throw. The dot in the middle is the centre of mass.

4. Lab frame and centre-of-mass frame

Write each position relative to the centre of mass (section 6.3, page 138): $\vec r_i=\vec r_S+\vec r_i\,'$, and so $\vec v_i=\vec v_S+\vec v_i\,'$. The frame moving with the centre of mass is the centre-of-mass frame (SPS′, Schwerpunktsystem); the original one is the lab frame (LS). The formula sheet and Rupp write primes; the script writes stars, $\vec r_i^{\,*}$. By definition of $S$,

$$\sum_im_i\vec r_i\,'=0,\qquad\sum_i\vec p_i\,'=0:$$

in the centre-of-mass frame the total momentum is zero. This is often used as its definition.

Kinetic energy (section 6.4.3, page 140). Expanding $\frac12m_i(\vec v_S+\vec v_i\,')^2$, the mixed terms add up to $\vec v_S\cdot\sum m_i\vec v_i\,'=0$:

$$T=\sum_i\frac{p_i'^2}{2m_i}+\frac12m_{\text{tot}}v_S^2.$$

Internal kinetic energy plus that of the centre of mass. A system at rest as a whole can still have kinetic energy: a spinning wheel, or a gas (its temperature, lesson 14).

Angular momentum (section 6.4.4, page 141). In the same way,

$$\vec L=\underbrace{\vec r_S\times m_{\text{tot}}\vec v_S}_{\vec L_S\ \text{(orbital)}}+\underbrace{\sum_i\vec r_i\,'\times\vec p_i\,'}_{\vec L\,'\ \text{(spin)}}.$$

The earth has orbital angular momentum about the sun and spin about its own axis.

Torques of internal forces cancel (section 6.4.5, page 141). For a pair, $\vec r_i\times\vec F_{ik}+\vec r_k\times\vec F_{ki}=(\vec r_i-\vec r_k)\times\vec F_{ik}=0$, because the force acts along the line joining the two particles. So for the whole system

$$\dot{\vec L}=\vec M_{\text{ext}}.$$

This holds if $\vec L$ and $\vec M_{\text{ext}}$ are taken about the same point, and that point is either fixed in an inertial frame or the centre of mass itself. The dancer can change her shape as she likes; with no external torque her total $\vec L$ stays.

The conservation laws for N particles (section 6.5, pages 143–144)
  • Centre of mass: $m_{\text{tot}}\ddot{\vec r}_S=\vec F_{\text{ext}}$; with $\vec F_{\text{ext}}=0$ the centre of mass moves uniformly.
  • Momentum: $\frac{d\vec P}{dt}=\vec F_{\text{ext}}$; with $\vec F_{\text{ext}}=0$, $\sum\vec p_i$ before $=\sum\vec p_i$ after, also if particles split or merge.
  • Angular momentum: $\frac{d\vec L}{dt}=\vec M_{\text{ext}}$; with $\vec M_{\text{ext}}=0$, $\vec L$ is constant in size and direction.
  • Mechanical energy: $E_{\text{tot}}=\sum\frac12m_iv_i^2+U_{\text{int}}+U_{\text{ext}}$ is constant if all forces, internal and external, are conservative.

Recipe: a system of bodies

  1. Draw the system boundary. Everything inside exerts internal forces: forget them.
  2. List the external forces (and their torques about a fixed point or about $S$).
  3. External force zero (in some direction)? Total momentum (that component) is conserved. External torque zero (about some axis)? Angular momentum about it is conserved.
  4. For the motion as a whole, use the centre of mass: $m_{\text{tot}}\ddot{\vec r}_S=\vec F_{\text{ext}}$.
  5. For energies, split $T$ into the centre-of-mass part and the internal part.

5. Two bodies: the reduced mass

A two-particle system (section 6.6, pages 144–147) separates into the motion of the centre of mass and the relative motion $\vec r_{12}=\vec r_1-\vec r_2$. Divide each equation of motion by its mass and subtract: with $\vec F_{21}=-\vec F_{12}$,

$$\ddot{\vec r}_{12}=\frac{\vec F_{12}}{m_1}+\frac{\vec F_{12}}{m_2}\iff\mu\,\ddot{\vec r}_{12}=\vec F_{12},\qquad\mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}.$$

The relative motion is that of a single particle of the reduced mass $\mu$ in the force $\vec F_{12}$. For very different masses, $\mu$ is almost the smaller one. The energy and the internal angular momentum become $T=\frac12m_{\text{tot}}v_S^2+\frac12\mu v_{12}^2$ and $\vec L\,'=\vec r_{12}\times\mu\vec v_{12}$.

  • Earth and moon (page 147): $m_{\text{Mo}}\approx0.012\,m_E$, so $\mu\approx0.99\,m_{\text{Mo}}$, and the common centre of mass lies about $4700\,\mathrm{km}$ from the earth's centre, still inside the earth. Both circle that point once a month.
  • Hydrogen: $m_p=1836\,m_e$, so $\mu=0.9995\,m_e$. The proton barely moves.

Why it's cool. Astronomers find planets around other stars with exactly this: the planet and its star both circle their common centre of mass, so the star wobbles. The sun wobbles too, by about its own radius, mostly because of Jupiter. Measuring tiny wobbles of starlight found the first planet around a sun-like star in 1995, from Geneva (Nobel Prize 2019).

6. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed.

Exercise 1 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.2(b), 1 point, original)

Which statement about angular momentum is correct? (A) Conservation of angular momentum holds only if the body moves on a circle. (B) Momentum and angular momentum of a body cannot be conserved at the same time. (C) Conservation of angular momentum holds only with respect to one particular point. (D) Angular momentum is conserved only if the sum of the torques on the body is zero.

Solution

D. That's the angular momentum theorem $\dot{\vec L}=\vec M$. (A) A free particle on a straight line has constant $\vec L$ (Worked example 1). (B) The same free particle has both constant. (C) For a free particle $\vec L$ is conserved about every point; in general it's conserved about every point about which the torque vanishes.

Exercise 2 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.2(a), 1 point, original)

Which condition on a satellite orbiting the earth is necessary and sufficient for Kepler's second law (equal areas in equal times)? (A) The orbit must be elliptic. (B) The orbit must be hyperbolic. (C) The total angular momentum must stay constant. (D) The speed must be constant along the orbit.

Solution

C. $\dot A=\frac L{2m}$: the areal velocity is constant exactly when $L$ is. The shape doesn't matter (A, B), and on an ellipse the speed varies (D).

Exercise 3 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.2(b), 1 point, original)

A figure skater spins with outstretched arms at angular velocity $\omega_0$; her moment of inertia is $I_0$. She pulls her arms in, so that her moment of inertia becomes $I_0/2$. How does her angular velocity change? (A) It stays the same. (B) It halves. (C) It doubles. (D) It becomes zero.

Solution

C. No external torque about the vertical axis, so $L=I_0\omega_0=\frac{I_0}2\omega$, hence $\omega=2\omega_0$. (Her kinetic energy $\frac{L^2}{2I}$ doubles: the work of her arms.)

Exercise 4 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.2(b), 1 point, original)

A planet moves on an ellipse around the sun. At which point of its orbit is its angular momentum (about the sun) largest? (A) At aphelion (farthest from the sun). (B) The angular momentum is constant. (C) Halfway between perihelion and aphelion. (D) At perihelion (closest to the sun).

Solution

B. Gravity is a central force: zero torque about the sun, so $\vec L$ is constant along the whole orbit. What changes is the speed: at perihelion $r$ is small, so $v$ is large, keeping $L=mrv$ (at the turning points $\vec v\perp\vec r$) the same.

Exercise 5 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.1(c), 1 point, original)

A force $\vec F$ acts for a short time $\delta t$ on a dumbbell at rest (two masses $m$ on a light rod): in case (a) on one of the masses, perpendicular to the rod; in case (b) at the middle of the rod, in the same direction. In which case does the dumbbell's centre of mass get the larger velocity? Neglect friction. (A) Case (a). (B) Case (b). (C) There is no difference. (D) Too little information.

Solution

C. The centre-of-mass theorem: $2m\,\Delta\vec v_S=\vec F\,\delta t$ in both cases. Where the force acts only decides whether the dumbbell also starts to spin: in case (a) it does, so it gets more kinetic energy in total (the force acts over a longer path there), but the same centre-of-mass velocity.

Exercise 6 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.3(d), 1 point, original)

Two skaters of different mass push off from each other. Which statement describes the result? (A) The skaters exert forces of equal size in opposite directions on each other. (B) All the energy goes to the lighter skater. (C) The heavier skater exerts no force on the lighter one. (D) The forces are not equal, because the masses are different.

Solution

A. Newton's third law; these are internal forces of the two-skater system, so its total momentum stays zero: $m_1v_1=-m_2v_2$. (B) The lighter skater gets more of the energy ($T=\frac{p^2}{2m}$ with equal $p$), but not all. (C), (D) contradict the third law.

Exercise 7 (HS25, problem 3 “Langhantel”, part (a), 1 point, original)

A barbell bar of mass $m_H$ and length $L$ (uniform) lies along $x$ from $0$ to $L$. Point masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ are fixed at $x=\frac17L$ and $x=\frac67L$. Find the centre of mass of the system in terms of $m_1$, $m_2$ and $m_H$.

Solution

The uniform bar counts as its mass at its middle, $\frac L2$. So

$$x_S=\frac{m_1\frac L7+m_H\frac L2+m_2\frac{6L}7}{m_1+m_2+m_H}=\frac{L\,(2m_1+7m_H+12m_2)}{14\,(m_1+m_2+m_H)}.$$

Check: for $m_1=m_2$ it gives $\frac L2$, by symmetry.

Exercise 8 (FS26, problem 3 “Weltraumlift”, part (f), 2 points, original)

A rigid rod sticks radially out from the equator of a planet rotating with constant $\omega$, and turns with it. A test mass $m$ climbs the rod with constant radial speed $v=\frac{dr}{dt}$. Find its angular momentum $L(r)$ about the planet's centre, and from it the torque $\tau(r)$ that the rod must exert sideways on the mass so that it keeps rotating with the rod.

Solution

The velocity has a radial part $v\,\hat r$ and a tangential part $\omega r\,\hat\varphi$. Only the tangential part contributes to $\vec r\times\vec p$:

$$L(r)=m\,r\cdot\omega r=m\omega r^2.$$

The torque is the rate of change, $\tau=\frac{dL}{dt}=m\omega\cdot2r\frac{dr}{dt}$:

$$\tau(r)=2m\omega rv.$$

The rod pushes sideways (in the direction of rotation) with the force $\frac\tau r=2m\omega v$: exactly the size of the Coriolis force of lesson 4, which the mass would feel in the rotating frame.

Exercise 9 (the puck on a string, exam style)

A puck of mass $m$ circles on a frictionless table at radius $r_0$ with speed $v_0$, held by a string that passes through a hole at the centre. From below, the string is slowly pulled until the radius is $\frac{r_0}2$. (a) What is the new speed? (b) How much work did the pulling do?

Solution

(a) The string force points at the hole: a central force, zero torque about the hole, so $L=mrv$ is constant: $mr_0v_0=m\frac{r_0}2v$, hence $v=2v_0$ (and $\omega=\frac v r$ grows by a factor 4).

(b) The string force is the only force doing work (the table's normal force and gravity are vertical). Work–energy theorem: $W=\frac12m(2v_0)^2-\frac12mv_0^2=\frac32mv_0^2$. The string force is not perpendicular to the motion while the radius shrinks, so it does do work, even though it exerts no torque.

Exercise 10 (the earth–moon centre of mass, a quick one)

The moon's mass is about $\frac1{81}$ of the earth's, and its distance is $d\approx384\,000\,\mathrm{km}$. How far from the earth's centre is the centre of mass of the pair? Is it inside the earth ($R_E\approx6400\,\mathrm{km}$)?

Solution

With the earth at $0$: $x_S=\frac{m_{\text{Mo}}\,d}{m_E+m_{\text{Mo}}}=\frac{d}{81+1}$ $\approx\frac{384\,000}{82}\,\mathrm{km}$ $\approx4700\,\mathrm{km}$. Yes: about three quarters of the way from the centre to the surface.

Scoring yourself: Exercises 1–8 make 9 points. With 6 you're done. Exercises 9 and 10 are practice.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Why the earth's day gets longer. The moon raises tides, and the tidal bulges are dragged ahead by the earth's rotation. Their pull is an internal torque of the earth–moon system: the earth's spin slows (the day grows by about 2 milliseconds per century) while the moon gains exactly the angular momentum the earth loses, moving outwards by about 3.8 cm per year. Laser mirrors left on the moon by Apollo astronauts measure it.

The falling cat. A cat dropped upside down lands on its feet, starting with zero angular momentum and keeping it. How? It turns the front half while the back half barely moves (front legs in, back legs out), then the reverse. Total $\vec L$ stays zero the whole time, but the shape changes and the cat ends up rotated: possible because the body is not rigid.
