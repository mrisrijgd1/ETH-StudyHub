Serie 5, explained

A whirling mass on a spring, the geostationary satellite, how strong gravity and tides really are, and a coffee cup on the Bernina railway. For every task: the idea in plain words, a sketch, up to three hints that each give away a little more, then the solution with every step explained next to what you write, and the trap people fall into.

How to use this page.

  1. Read the task and try it on paper for 10 minutes. Draw a sketch first: in physics it is half the work.
  2. Stuck on where to start? Read The idea and open You need.
  3. Still stuck? Open Hint 1, try again, then Hint 2, then Hint 3. Hint 3 is almost the whole solution.
  4. Only then open the solution. The left column says why each step is there, the right column is what you write.

Aufgabe 1 · A combined model

A mass $m=0.1\,$kg hangs on a massless spring with spring constant $D=2\,$N/m, and the whole thing is set rotating about the vertical axis through the top. Without mass and rotation the spring is $l=0.5\,$m long; with the mass rotating it stretches to $L=2.5\,$m.

  1. Sketch both cases (with and without rotation) with all relevant forces. What balances the spring force in each case?
  2. Find the angular velocity $\omega$.
The idea

Only two real forces act on the mass: gravity and the spring. Without rotation they simply cancel. When it whirls round, the mass needs a net force towards the axis, and the only thing that can supply it is the spring, so the spring tilts. Its vertical part still carries the weight; its horizontal part is the centripetal force $m\omega^2R$. The trick in b): the force triangle and the spring's own geometry have the same angle, so $\sin\alpha$ appears twice and the unknown radius cancels.

Rotating: the spring's horizontal part is the centripetal force, its vertical part (dashed) carries the weight. (Drawn with the real numbers: the spring tilts by about $76^\circ$.)

You need

Hooke's law $F=D\,\Delta L$; centripetal force $m\omega^2R$ for a circle of radius $R$; splitting a force into components (Physics lesson 2, lesson 3).

Hint 1

a) Without rotation: spring up, weight down, nothing else. With rotation: the spring pulls along its own direction, towards the top. Split that pull into a vertical part and a horizontal part. Which one balances gravity, and what does the other one do?

Hint 2

b) Call $\alpha$ the angle of the spring from the vertical. In the force picture, $\sin\alpha=\frac{F_Z}{F_{\text{spring}}}$ (horizontal part over the whole pull). In the geometry, $\sin\alpha=\frac RL$. Write $F_Z=m\omega^2R$ and $F_{\text{spring}}=D(L-l)$.

Hint 3

Set the two expressions for $\sin\alpha$ equal: $\frac RL=\frac{m\omega^2R}{D(L-l)}$. $R$ cancels. Solve for $\omega$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Without rotation there is nothing else to balance the spring.

No rotation: the spring force (up, along the spring) is balanced by gravity $mg$ (down).

With rotation the spring tilts. Split its pull: the vertical part still meets gravity; the horizontal part is new and is exactly what the circle needs.

Rotating: the vertical component of the spring force balances $mg$; its horizontal component is the centripetal force $$F_Z=m\omega^2R=m\omega^2L\sin\alpha,$$ with $\alpha$ the angle from the vertical. The spring force is $F_{\text{spring}}=D(L-l)$.

b) The same angle appears twice: in the force triangle and in the geometry of the spring.

$\sin\alpha=\dfrac{F_Z}{F_{\text{spring}}}$ and $\sin\alpha=\dfrac RL$.

Set them equal; $R$ cancels.

$$\begin{aligned}\frac RL&=\frac{m\omega^2R}{D(L-l)}\\ \Rightarrow\ \omega^2&=\frac{D(L-l)}{mL}.\end{aligned}$$

Numbers. The sign only says which way round it turns.

$$\omega=\sqrt{\frac{2\cdot2.0}{0.1\cdot2.5}}\ \frac1{\text s}=4\,\frac1{\text s}.$$ (The official solution writes $\pm4\,\text s^{-1}$: both senses of rotation work.)

Sense check. The spring pulls with $D(L-l)=4\,$N, but the weight is only about $1\,$N. So most of the pull goes into the circle, and the spring is nearly horizontal: $\cos\alpha=\frac{mg}{4\,\text N}\approx0.25$, $\alpha\approx76^\circ$.

Trap

Drawing a “centrifugal force” outwards in the sketch from outside. Seen from outside, only gravity and the spring act; the centripetal force is their sum, not a third force. And use the stretched length $L-l=2\,$m in Hooke's law, not $L$.

Aufgabe 2 · The geostationary satellite

A geostationary satellite circles the Earth above the equator and turns with the Earth, so it always stays above the same point. Assume a spherically symmetric gravitational field and a circular orbit.

  1. How far is it from the centre of the Earth?
  2. How much energy does the launch need to bring it into this orbit?
  3. How precisely must its distance $r$ be kept so that its position relative to a point $P$ on the ground changes by less than $0.1\,$km per day?
The idea

a) Gravity is the centripetal force. Since it gets weaker with distance, there is exactly one radius where the orbit takes one day. b) Energy is the difference between the start (at rest on the ground, turning with the Earth) and the orbit: more kinetic energy and more potential energy. c) A slightly wrong radius means a slightly wrong orbital period, so the satellite drifts a bit each day. Use the relation $\omega^2r^3=\text{const}$ and turn “small change in $\omega$” into “small change in $r$” with a derivative.

To scale: the orbit is about $6.6$ Earth radii from the centre (about $36\,000\,$km above the ground).

You need

Gravitational force $F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}$; potential energy $U=-\frac{GMm}r$; $GM_E=gR_E^2$ (Physics lesson 9). The Earth turns once per sidereal day, $T=23.93\,$h. Linear approximation: $\Delta\omega\approx\frac{d\omega}{dr}\,\Delta r$.

Hint 1

a) $m\omega^2r=\frac{GM_Em}{r^2}$ gives $r^3=\frac{GM_E}{\omega^2}$ with $\omega=\frac{2\pi}{T}$. b) Kinetic energy at the start: the launch site moves at $\omega R_E$. In orbit: $\omega r$. Same $\omega$!

Hint 2

b) $\Delta E=\frac m2\omega^2(r^2-R_E^2)+GM_Em\big(\frac1{R_E}-\frac1r\big)$. Divide by $m$ (the satellite's mass isn't given) and use $GM_E=gR_E^2$. c) A drift of $0.1\,$km on the ground is an angle $\Delta\varphi=\frac{0.1\,\text{km}}{R_E}$ per day, so the relative error allowed is $\frac{\Delta\omega}\omega=\frac{\Delta\varphi}{2\pi}$.

Hint 3

c) From $\omega=\sqrt{GM_E}\,r^{-3/2}$: $\frac{d\omega}{dr}=-\frac32\frac\omega r$, so $\frac{\Delta r}r=-\frac23\frac{\Delta\omega}\omega$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Gravity provides the centripetal force; the satellite's mass cancels.

$m\omega^2r=\dfrac{GM_Em}{r^2}\ \Rightarrow\ r^3=\dfrac{GM_E}{\omega^2}$.

“Turns with the Earth” means one sidereal day per orbit (the Earth's rotation relative to the stars).

$T=23.93\cdot3600\,$s, $\ \omega=\frac{2\pi}T=7.292\cdot10^{-5}\,\text s^{-1}$.

Numbers, with $GM_E=3.99\cdot10^{14}\,$m³/s².

$r\approx4.22\cdot10^7\,\text m\approx42\,200\,\text{km}$.

b) Energy needed = change in kinetic + change in potential energy. At the start the satellite already moves with the ground at $\omega R_E$.

$$\begin{aligned}\Delta E&=\frac{m\omega^2(r_S^2-R_E^2)}{2}\\&\quad+GM_Em\Big(\frac1{R_E}-\frac1{r_S}\Big).\end{aligned}$$

The mass isn't given, so give the energy per kilogram. Replace $GM_E$ by $gR_E^2$.

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\Delta E}{m}&=\tfrac12\omega^2(r_S^2-R_E^2)\\&\quad+gR_E\Big(1-\frac{R_E}{r_S}\Big)\\&\approx57.7\,\frac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}.\end{aligned}$$

c) Turn the allowed drift on the ground into an angle (seen from the Earth's centre).

$\Delta\varphi=\dfrac{0.1\,\text{km}}{6370\,\text{km}}\approx16\cdot10^{-6}\,$rad per day.

In one day the satellite turns $2\pi$. A drift of $\Delta\varphi$ per day means its $\omega$ is off by the same fraction.

$\dfrac{\Delta\omega}{\omega}=\dfrac{\Delta\varphi}{2\pi}\approx2.5\cdot10^{-6}$.

Link a change in $r$ to a change in $\omega$ with the derivative of $\omega=\sqrt{GM_E}\,r^{-3/2}$.

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{d\omega}{dr}&=-\frac32\sqrt{GM_E}\,r^{-5/2}\\&=-\frac32\,\frac\omega r\\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{\Delta r}{r}&=-\frac23\,\frac{\Delta\omega}{\omega}.\end{aligned}$$

Numbers.

$|\Delta r|\approx\frac23\cdot2.5\cdot10^{-6}\cdot4.22\cdot10^7\,\text m\approx70\,\text m$.

Note on a). The official solution writes $r=42.25\,$Mm. With $GM_E=3.99\cdot10^{14}\,$m³/s² the formula gives $42.16\,$Mm. At this precision the two agree. The usual textbook value is $42\,164\,$km.

Another way for c) (from the official solution)

Compute the drift rate directly: $\omega_{\text{drift}}=\frac{v}{R_E}=\frac{100\,\text m}{86\,400\,\text s\cdot6.378\cdot10^6\,\text m}\approx1.8\cdot10^{-10}\,\text s^{-1}$. Put $\omega\pm\omega_{\text{drift}}$ into $r=\sqrt[3]{GM_E/\omega^2}$ and subtract the $r$ from a): the difference is $\pm70\,$m.

Trap

In b), forgetting the starting kinetic energy, or using $mg\,\Delta h$ for the potential energy. Over $36\,000\,$km, $g$ is far from constant: use $-\frac{GMm}r$. In a), using $24\,$h: a solar day is longer than one rotation. The difference is small here but matters for precision questions like c).

Aufgabe 3 · How strong are gravity and tidal forces?

Look up the masses of Earth, Sun and Moon and the Earth–Moon and Earth–Sun distances.

  1. Gravitational force between two people of $80.0\,$kg each, $1.0\,$m apart. How many times the weight of a mosquito ($2.0\,$mg) is that? More or less than you expected?
  2. Gravitational force of the Sun on you ($m=80.0\,$kg). How many mosquito weights?
  3. Gravitational force of the Moon on you.
  4. Ratio of the Sun's and the Moon's pull.
  5. Tidal forces from Moon and Sun on you, on the side of the Earth facing them and the side facing away. Compare with d).
The idea

Gravity between everyday objects is absurdly weak: two people pull on each other with a fiftieth of a mosquito's weight. The Sun pulls on you about 180 times harder than the Moon. But tides don't come from the pull itself. They come from the difference in pull between the near and the far side of the Earth. That difference falls off like $\frac1{r^3}$, one power faster than the pull, and so the close Moon wins over the distant Sun.

All forces on an $80\,$kg person, on a logarithmic scale (each tick is a factor 10). The Sun pulls 180 times harder than the Moon, but the Moon's tidal force is twice the Sun's.

You need

$F=G\frac{m_1m_2}{r^2}$ with $G=6.67\cdot10^{-11}\,$N m²/kg² (Physics lesson 9). Data: $M_\odot\approx1.99\cdot10^{30}\,$kg, $d_\odot\approx1.49\cdot10^{11}\,$m; $M_{\text{Moon}}\approx7.35\cdot10^{22}\,$kg, $d_{\text{Moon}}\approx3.84\cdot10^{8}\,$m; $R_E\approx6.37\cdot10^6\,$m. Linear approximation $f(d-x)-f(d)\approx-f'(d)\,x$.

Hint 1

a)–d) Plug into the law of gravitation; for the mosquito, $F=mg$ with $m=2.0\cdot10^{-6}\,$kg.

Hint 2

e) The tidal force is the difference between the pull on you (on the near side, at distance $d-R_E$) and the pull at the Earth's centre (distance $d$). With $f(d)=\frac{GMm}{d^2}$, how big is $f(d-R_E)-f(d)$ for $R_E\ll d$?

Hint 3

$f'(d)=-\frac{2GMm}{d^3}$, so the difference is about $\frac{2GMm}{d^3}R_E$. Same size (pointing outwards) on the far side.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Law of gravitation, then compare with the mosquito.

$F=G\dfrac{(80.0\,\text{kg})^2}{(1.0\,\text m)^2}=4.3\cdot10^{-7}\,$N.
Mosquito: $2.0\cdot10^{-6}\,\text{kg}\cdot9.81\,\frac{\text m}{\text s^2}=2.0\cdot10^{-5}\,$N.
Ratio $0.022$: about $2\,\%$ of a mosquito's weight.

b) The Sun: huge mass, huge distance.

$F_\odot=G\dfrac{80.0\cdot1.99\cdot10^{30}}{(1.49\cdot10^{11})^2}\,\text N=0.478\,$N, about $24\,000$ mosquito weights.

c) The Moon.

$F_{\text{Moon}}=G\dfrac{80.0\cdot7.35\cdot10^{22}}{(3.84\cdot10^{8})^2}\,\text N=2.66\cdot10^{-3}\,$N, about $140$ mosquito weights.

d) Compare.

$F_\odot/F_{\text{Moon}}\approx180$: the Sun pulls much harder.

e) Tidal force = how much stronger the pull is on you than on the Earth's centre. Linear approximation of $\frac1{d^2}$.

$$F_{\text{tide}}\approx\frac{2GMm}{d^3}\,R_E.$$

Numbers for the Moon and the Sun.

$$\begin{aligned}F_{\text{tide,Moon}}&=\frac{2G\cdot7.35\cdot10^{22}\cdot80}{(3.84\cdot10^8)^3}\\&\quad\cdot6.37\cdot10^6\,\text N\\&=8.8\cdot10^{-5}\,\text N,\\ F_{\text{tide,Sun}}&=4.1\cdot10^{-5}\,\text N.\end{aligned}$$

Compare with d): the order flips.

The Moon's tidal force is about twice the Sun's, although the Sun's pull is 180 times larger: the pull falls off like $\frac1{r^2}$, the tidal force like $\frac1{r^3}$.

Trap

Kilometres in the formula: $3.84\cdot10^5\,$km must become $3.84\cdot10^8\,$m, and with $d^3$ a factor-1000 slip becomes a factor $10^9$. And “2.0 mg” is milligrams, $2.0\cdot10^{-6}\,$kg, not $2.0\cdot10^{-3}\,$kg.

Aufgabe 4 · The Bernina railway

On the Bernina train near Brusio, your coffee cup starts to slide on the horizontal table although the speedometer shows a constant speed. (The train is on the circular viaduct: three curves with radii $R_1=45\,$m, $R_2=50\,$m, $R_3=70\,$m.)

  1. Which (fictitious?) force sets the cup moving?
  2. You hold the cup so that it doesn't fall off the table. Does your hand exert a centrifugal or a centripetal force on the cup? Explain.
  3. With static friction $\mu=0.20$ between cup and table, what are the maximum speeds in the three curves at which the cup only just doesn't slide?
  4. In the second curve the train goes a little too fast, $v=12\,$m/s. What force $F_{\text{Hand}}$ must your hand supply so that the cup ($m=150\,$g) only just doesn't slide?
The idea

Constant speed on a curve is still acceleration, because the direction changes. Seen from the platform: the cup would like to go straight on (Newton's first law), and only friction drags it round the curve with the train. Seen from inside the train, it looks as if a force pushes the cup outwards: the centrifugal force, a fictitious force that only exists in the turning frame. Both views give the same equation: friction (plus your hand) must supply $\frac{mv^2}r$.

From outside, friction pulls the cup towards the centre; without it the cup goes straight on. From inside the train it feels like an outward (centrifugal) push.

You need

Centripetal force $\frac{mv^2}r$; static friction $F_R\le\mu F_N=\mu mg$ on a horizontal table; fictitious forces in a rotating frame (Physics lesson 4).

Hint 1

a), b) Answer once from inside the train, once from outside. Your hand pulls the cup towards the inside of the curve or towards the outside?

Hint 2

c) At the limit, maximum friction equals the needed centripetal force: $\mu mg=\frac{mv_{\max}^2}{r}$. The cup's mass cancels.

Hint 3

d) Now friction alone is too small: $F_{\text{Hand}}+\mu mg=\frac{mv^2}{r}$ with $r=R_2=50\,$m, $m=0.15\,$kg.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) In the train's (turning) frame, a fictitious force seems to push things outwards.

The centrifugal force, a fictitious force: seen from the train, it accelerates the cup outwards.

b) Seen from outside, the cup must be forced onto the curve. That takes a force towards the centre.

A centripetal force: the hand pulls the cup towards the centre of the curve, onto the same curved path as the train. Without it the cup would move straight on (seen from outside) instead of following the train.

c) The cup only just doesn't slide when maximum friction exactly supplies the centripetal force.

$\dfrac{mv_{\max}^2}{r}=\mu mg\ \Rightarrow\ v_{\max}=\sqrt{r\,g\,\mu}$.

Numbers for the three radii.

$R_1=45\,$m: $9.4\,$m/s $=34\,$km/h
$R_2=50\,$m: $9.9\,$m/s $=36\,$km/h
$R_3=70\,$m: $12\,$m/s $=42\,$km/h

d) Too fast for friction alone: the hand adds what's missing, in the same (inward) direction.

$$F_{\text{Hand}}=\frac{mv^2}{r}-\mu mg.$$ Here $\frac{mv^2}{r}=\frac{0.15\cdot12^2}{50}\,\text N=0.43\,$N and $\mu mg=0.20\cdot0.15\cdot9.81\,\text N=0.29\,$N, so $F_{\text{Hand}}\approx0.14\,$N.

Both views agree. In the train: the cup stays put when friction balances the centrifugal force. Outside: the cup follows the curve when friction supplies the centripetal force. Same equation, $\mu mg=\frac{mv^2}r$.

Trap

Mixing the two frames in one equation, e.g. writing centrifugal and centripetal force in the same free-body sketch. Pick one frame and stay in it. In the exam, the outside (inertial) frame is usually safer: there, only real forces appear.
