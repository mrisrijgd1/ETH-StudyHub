1. Warm-up (5 min)

Three quick questions. Answer each in your head, then open it.

  1. Compute $\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(\frac12\right)^k$.

    Answer

    A geometric series with $q=\frac12$, $|q|<1$: $\frac1{1-q}=2$.

  2. True or false: if $a_k\to0$, then $\sum a_k$ converges.

    Answer

    False: $\frac1k\to0$, but the harmonic series diverges (Proof 2). The true direction is the other one: if $\sum a_k$ converges, then $a_k\to0$.

  3. Is the value $f(x_0)$ used in the definition of $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)$?

    Answer

    No. The definition only looks at $x$ with $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$, so $x_0$ itself is left out; $f$ need not even be defined there.

2. Lesson 13 again, in plain words (15 min)

Lesson 13 packs four topics into one lesson. Here they are again, slowly, each tied to something you already know. Read it once, then answer the three questions at the end.

The one idea: a function limit is many sequence limits at once

You already know limits of sequences. A limit of a function at $x_0$ asks the same question for every way of walking towards $x_0$. That is Lemma 6.40 (lesson 13, Proof A):

$$\begin{aligned}&\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=A\iff\\&f(a_n)\to A\ \text{for every sequence}\\&a_n\to x_0\ \text{with}\ a_n\in D\setminus\{x_0\}.\end{aligned}$$

So you get two working methods for free:

  • To show a limit exists, use the rules you know from sequences: sums, products, quotients (non-zero denominator) and the sandwich lemma all work for function limits too.
  • To show a limit does not exist, find two sequences $a_n\to x_0$ and $b_n\to x_0$ with different limits of $f(a_n)$ and $f(b_n)$. For $\frac x{|x|}$: $f(\frac1n)=1$ and $f(-\frac1n)=-1$.

The definition, word by word

Let $f:D\to\mathbb R$ and let $x_0$ be an accumulation point of $D$ (so there are points of $D$ to walk on). Then $$\begin{aligned}&\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=A\iff\\&\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x\in D:\\&0<|x-x_0|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-A|<\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$

  • It is ε-δ continuity with two changes: $A$ instead of $f(x_0)$, and $x_0$ itself left out ($0<|x-x_0|$).
  • Link to continuity (Lemma 6.39): if $x_0\in D$, then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ exactly when $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=f(x_0)$.
  • One-sided limits: $x\to x_0^+$ uses only $x>x_0$, and $x\to x_0^-$ only $x<x_0$. If $D$ reaches $x_0$ from both sides, the limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal.
  • At infinity: $\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)=A$ means $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists R>0\ \forall x\in D:\ x>R\Rightarrow|f(x)-A|<\varepsilon$. For a quotient of polynomials, divide by the highest power.

Riemann sums in one sentence

Cut $[a,b]$ by a partition $Z$: $a=x_0<\dots<x_n=b$, pick a point $z_k$ in each piece, and add up rectangles: $R(f,Z,z)=\sum_{k=1}^nf(z_k)(x_k-x_{k-1})$. The mesh $|Z|$ is the widest piece. For continuous $f$, these sums tend to $\int_a^bf\,dx$ as the mesh goes to $0$ (Proof 1 below). The form you'll meet in exams is $$\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^nf\Big(\frac kn\Big)\longrightarrow\int_0^1f(x)\,dx.$$

Landau's $O$ and $o$ without fear

  • $f=O(g)$ as $x\to x_0$: near $x_0$, $|f|\le C|g|$ for one fixed $C$. In words: “$f$ is at most a constant times $g$.”
  • $f=o(g)$ as $x\to x_0$: for every $\varepsilon>0$, near $x_0$, $|f|\le\varepsilon|g|$. In words (when $g\ne0$): $\frac fg\to0$.
  • The movement is part of the statement: $x^2=o(x)$ as $x\to0$, but $x=o(x^2)$ as $x\to+\infty$.

Three questions to check you've got it.

  1. Does $\lim_{x\to0}h(x)$ exist for $h(x)=0$ ($x<0$), $h(x)=1$ ($x\ge0$)? Answer with two sequences.

    Answer

    No. With $a_n=\frac1n$: $h(a_n)=1\to1$. With $b_n=-\frac1n$: $h(b_n)=0\to0$. Two different limits, so by Lemma 6.40 there is no limit at $0$. (The one-sided limits are $0$ and $1$.)

  2. Compute $\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{2x^2+1}{x^2+3}$.

    Answer

    Divide by $x^2$: $\frac{2+1/x^2}{1+3/x^2}\to\frac21=2$, by the limit rules and $\frac1{x^2}\to0$.

  3. As $x\to0$: is $x^3=o(x^2)$? Is $5x^2=O(x^2)$? Is $5x^2=o(x^2)$?

    Answer

    $\frac{x^3}{x^2}=x\to0$: yes, $o$. $\frac{5x^2}{x^2}=5$ is bounded: yes, $O$. It does not tend to $0$: no, not $o$.

3. Recap card: lesson 14 (10 min)

Read this once, slowly. Everything in bold must come out exactly in the exam.

Lesson 14: series

  • $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k$ converges if the partial sums $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^na_k$ converge; the value is $\lim s_n$.
  • Terms tend to $0$: if $\sum a_k$ converges, then $a_k\to0$. The converse is false.
  • Benchmarks: $\sum_{k=0}^\infty q^k=\frac1{1-q}$ for $|q|<1$; the harmonic series $\sum\frac1k$ diverges; $\sum\frac1{k^p}$ converges exactly for $p>1$ (condensation).
  • Comparison test (for $0\le a_k\le b_k$): $\sum b_k$ converges $\Rightarrow\sum a_k$ converges; $\sum a_k$ diverges $\Rightarrow\sum b_k$ diverges.
  • Leibniz criterion: if $a_k>0$ is decreasing with $a_k\to0$, then $\sum(-1)^{k+1}a_k$ converges (Proof 3).
  • Absolute convergence: $\sum|a_k|$ converges $\Rightarrow\sum a_k$ converges (by the Cauchy criterion for series).
  • Root test: with $\rho=\limsup\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}$: $\rho<1$ gives absolute convergence, $\rho>1$ divergence, $\rho=1$ no information. The ratio test is similar with $\frac{|a_{k+1}|}{|a_k|}$.
  • Rearranging: an absolutely convergent series has the same value in every order; a conditionally convergent one can be rearranged to any value (Riemann).

5. Write it on paper: three exam proofs (25 min)

For each proof: read the claim and the frame, close your eyes for ten seconds, then write the whole proof on paper without looking at the lesson. Only then open the fold and mark yourself with the grader's list.

Proof 1: Riemann sums converge, for continuous $f$ (Satz 6.47)

Claim. Let $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ be continuous. For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that for every partition $Z$ with mesh $|Z|<\delta$ and every choice of points $z_k\in[x_{k-1},x_k]$: $\left|\int_a^bf\,dx-R(f,Z,z)\right|<\varepsilon$. (Lesson 13, Proof B.)

What the definitions force

First line: “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” Then produce $\delta$, and only then take an arbitrary $Z$ with $|Z|<\delta$ and arbitrary points $z_k$. Last line: “so $\left|\int_a^bf-R(f,Z,z)\right|<\varepsilon$.” The gap: compare piece by piece, $\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}f$ against $f(z_k)(x_k-x_{k-1})$, with an error of at most $\eta\cdot$width per piece where $\eta(b-a)<\varepsilon$.

The proof and the grader's list

Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $\eta=\frac\varepsilon{2(b-a)}$. By Heine, $f$ is uniformly continuous on $[a,b]$, so there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)-f(y)|<\eta$ whenever $|x-y|<\delta$. Let $Z$ be a partition with $|Z|<\delta$ and let $z_k\in[x_{k-1},x_k]$. For $x\in[x_{k-1},x_k]$ we have $|x-z_k|\le x_k-x_{k-1}<\delta$, so $|f(x)-f(z_k)|<\eta$, and therefore $$\begin{aligned}&\left|\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}f\,dx-f(z_k)(x_k-x_{k-1})\right|\\&\quad\le\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}|f(x)-f(z_k)|\,dx\\&\quad\le\eta\,(x_k-x_{k-1}).\end{aligned}$$ Summing over $k$ and using additivity of the integral: $$\begin{aligned}\left|\int_a^bf\,dx-R(f,Z,z)\right|&\le\eta(b-a)\\&=\frac\varepsilon2<\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$ ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • Heine (uniform continuity) named, with the tolerance $\eta=\frac\varepsilon{2(b-a)}$ chosen before the partition;
  • “let $Z$ with $|Z|<\delta$ and points $z_k$ be arbitrary”, and why $|x-z_k|<\delta$ on each piece;
  • the per-piece estimate with the triangle inequality for integrals;
  • summing with additivity: the widths add up to $b-a$.

In short: “Heine, per piece error η·width, the widths sum to b − a”. It is lesson 9's “continuous ⇒ integrable” estimate in new clothes.

Proof 2: the harmonic series diverges (Beispiel 7.4)

Claim. $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1k$ diverges; its partial sums are unbounded, so $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1k=\infty$. (Lesson 14, Proof B.)

What the definitions force

Goal: the partial sums $s_n$ are unbounded (then they cannot converge, since convergent sequences are bounded). Last line: “$s_{2^\ell}\ge1+\frac\ell2$ for every $\ell$, so $(s_n)_n$ is unbounded.” The gap: a lower bound for blocks of terms.

The proof and the grader's list

For $j\ge1$, the block $\sum_{k=2^{j-1}+1}^{2^j}\frac1k$ has $2^{j-1}$ terms, each at least $\frac1{2^j}$, so the block is at least $2^{j-1}\cdot\frac1{2^j}=\frac12$. Hence $$\begin{aligned}s_{2^\ell}&=1+\sum_{j=1}^\ell\ \sum_{k=2^{j-1}+1}^{2^j}\frac1k\\&\ge1+\frac\ell2,\end{aligned}$$ which exceeds any given $K$ once $\ell>2K$. So the partial sums are unbounded and the series diverges (to $\infty$, since the partial sums increase). ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • the blocks from $2^{j-1}+1$ to $2^j$, with the count $2^{j-1}$ terms;
  • each term at least the last one, $\frac1{2^j}$, so each block at least $\frac12$;
  • the bound $s_{2^\ell}\ge1+\frac\ell2$;
  • the conclusion: unbounded partial sums, hence divergent.

In short: “blocks of $2^{j-1}$ terms, each block at least a half”.

Proof 3: the Leibniz criterion (Proposition 7.25, Felder FS23)

Claim. Let $(a_k)_k$ satisfy $a_k>0$, $a_{k+1}\le a_k$ and $a_k\to0$. Then $\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^{k+1}a_k$ converges. (Lesson 14, Proof E. Felder FS23 asked exactly this, as part (a) of a 7-point question.)

What the definitions force

First line: “Let $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{k+1}a_k$.” Last line: “so $s_n\to S$.” All three assumptions must be used. The gap: there is no candidate $S$. Find one by splitting into odd and even partial sums, which are monotone.

The proof and the grader's list

Let $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{k+1}a_k$. Since $(a_k)_k$ is decreasing, $$\begin{aligned}s_{2n+1}&=s_{2n-1}-(a_{2n}-a_{2n+1})\\&\le s_{2n-1}\end{aligned}$$ and $$\begin{aligned}s_{2n+2}&=s_{2n}+(a_{2n+1}-a_{2n+2})\\&\ge s_{2n}.\end{aligned}$$ Since $a_{2n}>0$, $s_{2n}=s_{2n-1}-a_{2n}\le s_{2n-1}$. Hence $s_2\le s_{2n}\le s_{2n-1}\le s_1$ for all $n$. By monotone convergence, $s_{2n}\to S$ and $s_{2n-1}\to S'$, and $S'-S=\lim_{n\to\infty}(s_{2n-1}-s_{2n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{2n}=0$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N_1$ with $|s_{2n}-S|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N_1$ and $N_2$ with $|s_{2n-1}-S|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N_2$, and let $N=2\max\{N_1,N_2\}$. Then $|s_m-S|<\varepsilon$ for every $m\ge N$, whether $m$ is even or odd. So the series converges to $S$. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • odd partial sums decrease and even ones increase, each in one line using “decreasing”;
  • the squeeze $s_2\le s_{2n}\le s_{2n-1}\le s_1$, using $a_k>0$;
  • monotone convergence for both subsequences;
  • the same limit, using $a_k\to0$;
  • gluing: one $N$ for all $m$, even and odd.

In short: “odd down, even up, squeezed between $s_2$ and $s_1$, the gap is $a_{2n}\to0$, glue”. Each assumption is used exactly once.

6. Spot the mistake (5 min)

A student is asked whether $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}k$ converges absolutely and hands in this. The conclusion is wrong. Where exactly does it break?

“Solution.” We have $\left|\frac{(-1)^k}k\right|=\frac1k\to0$. Since the absolute values of the terms tend to $0$, the series $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left|\frac{(-1)^k}k\right|$ converges, so $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}k$ converges absolutely.

The mistake

“Terms tend to $0$” is necessary for convergence, not sufficient. Here $\sum\left|\frac{(-1)^k}k\right|=\sum\frac1k$ is the harmonic series, which diverges (Proof 2). So the series does not converge absolutely.

It does converge, but only conditionally, by Leibniz (Proof 3): $\frac1k$ is positive, decreasing and tends to $0$. Exam lesson: $a_k\to0$ can only ever rule convergence out (when it fails); it never proves it.

Worth a second look

  • To kill a function limit, find two sequences: for $\frac x{|x|}$, $f(\frac1n)=1$ but $f(-\frac1n)=-1$, so there is no limit at $0$.
  • Only approaches inside the domain count: $\lim_{x\to0}\sqrt x=0$ makes sense on $[0,\infty)$, although you can only come from the right.
  • The movement belongs to the Landau statement: $x^2=o(x)$ as $x\to0$, but $x=o(x^2)$ as $x\to+\infty$. $O$ allows a fixed factor; $o$ means the ratio tends to $0$. So $3x+2=3x+O(1)=3x+o(x)$, but not $3x+o(1)$. Two $O$-terms never cancel: they stand for unknown functions, not numbers.
  • Spot a Riemann sum: $\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^nf\big(\frac kn\big)\to\int_0^1f\,dx$ for continuous $f$ (Proof 1).
  • Telescoping: $\frac1{n(n+1)}=\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}$, so $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n(n+1)}=1$.
  • Leibniz also bounds the error: after $n$ terms, $|S-s_n|\le a_{n+1}$.
  • Root or ratio test with value $1$ decides nothing: both give $1$ for $\sum\frac1n$ (diverges) and $\sum\frac1{n^2}$ (converges).

Quick checks from the Lernblatt

Translated from the Lernblatt's exercise list. The Lernblatt has no official answers, so the answers here are StudyHub-written.

  1. Compute $\lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}$. Must the expression be defined at $x=2$?

    Answer

    For $x\ne2$ it equals $x+2$, so the limit is $4$. No: $x=2$ itself is excluded from the definition.

  2. Find the limits of $\frac{|x|}x$ as $x\to0^-$ and $x\to0^+$. Does the limit as $x\to0$ exist?

    Answer

    $-1$ from the left, $1$ from the right. The two-sided limit does not exist, because the one-sided limits differ.

  3. Compute $\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{3x^2+1}{x^2+2}$ and $\lim_{x\to0}\frac1{x^2}$.

    Answer

    Divide by $x^2$: $\frac{3+1/x^2}{1+2/x^2}\to3$. And $\frac1{x^2}\to+\infty$ (an improper limit).

  4. Which hold as $x\to0$: $x^2=o(x)$, $2x=o(x)$, $2x=O(x)$?

    Answer

    $x^2=o(x)$: yes, $\frac{x^2}x=x\to0$. $2x=o(x)$: no, the ratio is $2$. $2x=O(x)$: yes, the ratio is bounded.

  5. Let $F(x)=3x+2$. As $x\to+\infty$, which hold: $F=3x+O(1)$, $F=3x+o(1)$, $F=3x+o(x)$?

    Answer

    The error is $F-3x=2$. Bounded: $O(1)$ yes. Tends to $0$: $o(1)$ no. Small compared with $x$: $\frac2x\to0$, so $o(x)$ yes.

  6. What is $\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n\left(\frac kn\right)^2$?

    Answer

    A Riemann sum of $x^2$ on $[0,1]$ with right endpoints, so the limit is $\int_0^1x^2\,dx=\frac13$.

  7. Find the partial sums and the value of $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n(n+1)}$.

    Answer

    $s_N=\sum_{n=1}^N\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\right)=1-\frac1{N+1}\to1$.

  8. Is $1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\dots$ convergent? Absolutely convergent? How large is the error after four terms at most?

    Answer

    Convergent by Leibniz ($\frac1n$ is positive, decreasing, tends to $0$). Not absolutely: the absolute values give the harmonic series. Error after four terms at most $a_5=\frac15$.

  9. May you rearrange the alternating harmonic series without changing its value? And an absolutely convergent series?

    Answer

    No: it converges only conditionally, and a rearrangement can give any value (Riemann). An absolutely convergent series: yes, every rearrangement has the same value.
