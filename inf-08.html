1. The story: the golem counts with lamps

Deep inside, the golem's boxes are not boxes at all. They are rows of tiny lamps, each either dark or lit. A box for an int is a row of 32 lamps. Everything the golem stores, numbers, letters, colours, is a pattern of dark and lit.

The scribes worked out how to read a row of lamps as a number: the rightmost lamp counts 1, the next 2, then 4, 8, 16, each twice the one before. Lit lamps are added up. With 32 lamps that gives every number from 0 to a little over four billion. When a counter runs past the last pattern, it jumps back to all dark, like a clock after twelve.

Fractions were harder. The surveyors needed both the width of a grain of sand and the distance to the next kingdom. So the scribes split a long row of lamps in two: one part for the digits, one part to say where the point goes. The digits are always the same number of lamps, so a grain and a kingdom both get three good digits; but between two neighbouring numbers there is always a gap, and the bigger the numbers, the wider the gap.

In C++: the lamps are bits, reading them with powers of 2 is the binary representation, the clock is arithmetic modulo $2^B$, the trick for negative numbers is the two's complement, and the split row is a floating point number: a mantissa and an exponent. The script opens with a surprise (p. 134): the eighth power of 16, computed with int, comes out as 0. By the end of section 4 you'll see why.

2. Binary numbers

Binary representation (p. 134): every digit is a bit, 0 or 1, and $$ b_n b_{n-1} \dots b_1 b_0 \quad\text{stands for}\quad b_n\cdot 2^n + \dots + b_1\cdot 2^1 + b_0\cdot 2^0. $$ Example 11.1: 101011 is 32 + 8 + 2 + 1 = 43. The leftmost bit is the most significant bit (MSB), the rightmost the least significant bit (LSB). In C++, a binary literal starts with 0b: 0b101011 is 43 (p. 135).

Converting a natural number between bases

Everything below works for any base b: 2, 3, 5, 10, 16.

  1. From base b to decimal? Multiply each digit by its power of b and add. Fastest by Horner: start with 0; for each digit from the left, value = value · b + digit. 1202 in base 3: ((1·3 + 2)·3 + 0)·3 + 2 = 47.
  2. From decimal to base b? Divide by b repeatedly; the remainders are the digits, last digit first. Stop at 0. (This is the "peel off the last digit" loop of lesson 3, with b instead of 10.)
  3. Between binary and hexadecimal? Group the bits in fours from the right; each group is one hex digit (section 3). No decimal needed.

3. Hexadecimal numbers

Long rows of bits are hard to read, and converting them to decimal in your head is slow. Hexadecimal numbers have base 16 (p. 135): $h_n \dots h_1 h_0$ stands for $h_n\cdot 16^n + \dots + h_1\cdot 16 + h_0$. Since 16 = $2^4$, every hex digit is exactly four bits, a nibble. The digits above 9 are letters (Table 11.1):

hex0123456789abcdef
dec0123456789101112131415
bin0000000100100011010001010110011110001001101010111100110111101111

In C++ a hex literal starts with 0x: 0xff is 15·16 + 15 = 255 (p. 135). A 32-bit number is eight hex digits, from 0x00000000 to 0xffffffff. Some landmarks (p. 136): 0x400 = 1024, 0x80000000 has only the highest of 32 bits set, 0xffffffff all of them. Colours on websites are three bytes, red, green, blue: #FF0050 is full red, no green, a bit of blue (Example 11.3).

4. Negative integers: two's complement (script 11.1.1, marked *)

With B bits, the computer can only store $2^B$ patterns. Adding, subtracting and multiplying bit patterns, and throwing away whatever doesn't fit, is arithmetic modulo $m = 2^B$ (p. 136–138). The script shows it on a clock of 4 bits, the numbers 0 to 15:

  • 2 + 3: 0010 + 0011 = 0101 = 5. Nothing special.
  • 7 + 10: 0111 + 1010 = (1)0001. The fifth bit doesn't fit and is lost: the result is 1, which is 17 mod 16. The clock went once round.
  • 5 − 10: borrowing leaves infinitely many 1s on the left; keep four: 1011 = 11, which is −5 mod 16.

So the pattern 1011 behaves exactly like −5 in every addition, subtraction and multiplication, as long as we compute modulo 16. Since $-a \equiv m - a \pmod m$, we may choose which numbers the patterns stand for. The choice used in practically every computer is the "sweet spot in the middle" (p. 138): the patterns with MSB 0 are 0 to $2^{B-1} - 1$, those with MSB 1 are the negative numbers $-2^{B-1}$ to −1. This is the two's complement. For 3 bits (Figure 11.1, p. 139):

bits000001010011100101110111
unsigned01234567
two's complement0123−4−3−2−1

In short: with B bits, the pattern of −a is the pattern of $2^B - a$. The MSB tells the sign. A practical shortcut (equivalent to $2^B - a$): invert all bits of a, then add 1. −3 in 4 bits: 3 = 0011, inverted 1100, plus 1 = 1101, and indeed $16 - 3 = 13$ = 1101. "It does not matter if we identify 1101 as 13 or as −3" (p. 138): 1 + 13 and 1 + (−3) give the same pattern 1110.

This explains the ranges of lesson 2: a 32-bit int goes from $-2^{31}$ to $2^{31} - 1$, an unsigned int from 0 to $2^{32} - 1$, and both are the same $2^{32}$ patterns. And the opening surprise: $16^8 = 2^{32}$, which is 0 modulo $2^{32}$. (For int, C++ calls such an overflow undefined behaviour; in practice you see the wrapped value. For unsigned int, the wrap-around is guaranteed.)

5. Floating point number systems

Fixed point numbers have a fixed number of digits before and after the point (p. 140). With 7 + 3 decimal digits, 0.0824 loses its last digit; with 3 + 7, 1000 doesn't fit. Small and large numbers need the point to move. The same number has many forms, $0.0824 = 0.824\cdot 10^{-1} = 8.24\cdot 10^{-2} = 824\cdot 10^{-4}$, but always the same three significant digits, the mantissa. A floating point number stores a fixed number of significant digits and an exponent.

Floating point number system $F(\beta, p, e_{\min}, e_{\max})$ (p. 140–141), given by four natural numbers: the base β ≥ 2, the precision p ≥ 1 (number of digits), and the smallest and largest exponent. It contains the numbers $$ \pm\, d_0.d_1 \dots d_{p-1} \cdot \beta^e,\qquad d_i \in \{0, \dots, \beta - 1\},\quad e \in \{e_{\min}, \dots, e_{\max}\}. $$

Normalized (p. 141): $d_0 \ne 0$, so there is exactly one non-zero digit before the point. This makes the representation unique. The set of normalized numbers is written $F^*(\beta, p, e_{\min}, e_{\max})$. The price: 0 is not in $F^*$, and neither is any number with absolute value below $\beta^{e_{\min}}$; the smallest positive number is $1.0\dots0\cdot\beta^{e_{\min}}$.

The script's example, $F^*(2, 3, -2, 2)$ (Figure 11.2, p. 142). In base 2, $d_0$ must be 1, so the mantissas are 1.00, 1.01, 1.10, 1.11, that is 1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75:

mantissae = −2e = −1e = 0e = 1e = 2
1.000.250.5124
1.010.31250.6251.252.55
1.100.3750.751.536
1.110.43750.8751.753.57

Look at the gaps: 0.0625 between neighbours for e = −2, 1 for e = 2. "The exponent influences the range and the spread of the numbers" (p. 141): the larger the numbers, the further apart.

A small floating point system in five steps

  1. How many positive numbers? In base 2 the first digit is 1, so there are $2^{p-1}$ mantissas; times $e_{\max} - e_{\min} + 1$ exponents. (Base β: $(\beta - 1)\,\beta^{p-1}$ mantissas.) The negative numbers are as many; 0 is not in $F^*$.
  2. Largest and smallest positive? Largest: all digits maximal, $e_{\max}$: in base 2, $1.1\dots1 \cdot 2^{e_{\max}}$. Smallest: $1.0\dots0\cdot 2^{e_{\min}}$.
  3. Is x in the system? Write x normalized, $1.d_1d_2\dots\cdot 2^e$. It is in $F^*$ exactly when its binary mantissa needs at most p digits (counting the 1 before the point) and $e_{\min} \le e \le e_{\max}$.
  4. How to add? (p. 142) Denormalize one number so both have the same exponent; add the mantissas; renormalize; round to p digits.
  5. How to round? Look at the first digit after the p-th: 1 → round up, 0 → round down (the rule the exams give). Round after every single operation: the order of operations changes the result.

Worked example 3: an addition in $F^*(2, 4, -2, 2)$ (script Figure 11.3, p. 142)

Compute $1.111\cdot 2^{-2} + 1.011\cdot 2^{-1}$.

  1. Step 4a: bring both to the same exponent.
    Answer

    Rewrite the second with exponent −2: $1.011\cdot 2^{-1} = 10.110\cdot 2^{-2}$ (point one place to the right, exponent one smaller).

  2. Add and renormalize.
    Answer

    $1.111 + 10.110 = 100.101$, so $100.101\cdot 2^{-2} = 1.00101\cdot 2^{0}$.

  3. Round to p = 4 digits.
    Answer

    $1.001|01$: the next digit is 0, round down: $1.001\cdot 2^0$ = 1.125. The exact sum was 1.15625: rounding lost 0.03125.

6. IEEE 754: float and double

The IEEE standard 754 fixes the floating point systems and their rounding for practically every programming language (p. 142–143):

typesystembitssignexponentmantissa stored
float$F^*(2, 24, -126, 127)$321823 (+ the leading 1 = 24)
double$F^*(2, 53, -1022, 1023)$6411152 (+ the leading 1 = 53)

The leading 1 of a normalized binary mantissa is always 1, so it is not stored: one bit saved. Every arithmetic operation computes the exact result and then rounds it to the nearest representable number (p. 143). That one sentence answers many true/false questions: a * b == b * a is always true (both round the same exact product), but a + b + c == c + b + a can be false (the intermediate sums are rounded differently).

Special values (marked *, p. 143–144): the exponent patterns all-0 and all-1 are reserved. All-1 encodes ±∞ (mantissa 0) and NaN, "not a number" (otherwise). All-0 encodes ±0 (mantissa 0) and the denormalized numbers $0.d_1\dots d_{23}\cdot 2^{-126}$ below the smallest normalized float. So C++ has both 0 and −0. Between, the exponent bits as a number, minus 127 (for float), give the exponent: bits 1 give −126, bits 254 give 127.

7. Decimal fractions in binary (script 11.3.2)

We write decimal literals; the computer stores binary. How do you find the bits of $x = b_0.b_1b_2\dots$ for 0 < x < 2 (p. 144)? $b_0$ is 1 exactly when x ≥ 1. Subtracting $b_0$ and doubling shifts the rest one place to the left: $2(x - b_0) = b_1.b_2b_3\dots$, again a number in [0, 2). Repeat. In code (p. 145):

int current_bit;
while (x != 0) {
  if (x >= 1) current_bit = 1;
  else current_bit = 0;
  std::cout << current_bit;
  x = 2 * (x - current_bit);
}

  3. What does this mean for 1.1 in C++?
    Answer

    A periodic number can't be stored in finitely many bits: 1.1 is rounded to the nearest double, 1.1000000000000000888…, and 1.1f to the nearest float, 1.1000000238… (p. 146). So 1.1 - 1.0 == 0.1 is false, and so is 0.1f == 0.1 (January 2017, task 1 (f)).

For fractions p/q, the bits of x can only take finitely many values (x is always some k/q), so the expansion is finite or periodic. Spotting the first repeated x gives the exact period, which is what January 2018, task 5 asks for.

8. The three floating point rules (script 11.3.3)

Rule 1: do not test floating point numbers for equality (p. 146). This loop never ends, because i never becomes exactly 1.0:

for (float i = 0.1; i != 1.0; i += 0.1)
  std::cout << i << "\n";

Test a range instead, for example i < 1.0 - 1e-5f, or loop with an int counter and compute 0.1f * k.

Rule 2: do not add two floating point numbers of very different orders of magnitude (p. 146–148). In base 2 with p = 4: $32 + 1 = 1.000\cdot 2^5 + 1.000\cdot 2^0 = 1.00001\cdot 2^5 \approx 1.000\cdot 2^5 = 32$. The 1 falls into a hole. Hence: add many terms from small to large.

Rule 3: do not subtract two floating point numbers with a very similar value. The leading digits cancel and only the rounding errors remain ("cancellation", not discussed further in the script).

The script's demonstration of Rule 2, the harmonic numbers $H_n = \sum_{i=1}^n 1/i \approx \ln n$, summed forwards and backwards in float (p. 147):

#include <iostream>

int main() {
  int n;
  std::cin >> n;
  float fs = 0;
  for (int i = 1; i <= n; ++i)
    fs += 1.0f / i;                 // large terms first
  std::cout << "Forward sum = " << fs << "\n";
  float bs = 0;
  for (int i = n; i >= 1; --i)
    bs += 1.0f / i;                 // small terms first
  std::cout << "Backward sum = " << bs << "\n";
  return 0;
}
Forward sum = 15.4037
Backward sum = 16.686

The true value is about 16.695. Forwards, the partial sum grows quickly to about 15, and then each new $1/i$ is too small to change it: the sum stagnates. Backwards, small terms are added to a small sum first, and every term counts.

  1. The four questions.
    My answer

    (1) Subproblem: n / 2, which is n without its last bit. (2) Trivial: n < 2, one bit, the character '0' or '1'. (3) Progress: n / 2 < n for n ≥ 2. (4) Combine: the bits of n / 2, then the last bit n % 2 on the right.

  2. By hand: to_binary(6).
    My answer

    to_binary(6) = to_binary(3) + "0" = (to_binary(1) + "1") + "0" = "1" + "1" + "0" = "110". The calls go up to the leading bit; on the way back down each call appends its own bit.

  3. Why is the base case n < 2 and not n == 0?
    My answer

    With n == 0 as base case returning "", to_binary(0) would return the empty string, and with "0" every number would get a leading zero. Stopping at one bit gives both cases right.

  4. Write it.

  5. Tests.
    My answer

    0 and 1 (the base case), 2 (the first two-bit number), a power of 2 (1024: one 1 and ten 0s), all ones (255), a large number. 1000000 has 20 bits, as worked example 1 predicted.

  6. How deep does the recursion go?
    My answer

    One call per bit: at most 32 for an unsigned int. Recursion is harmless here.

Patterns used: base case first rest first, then the last digit peel off the last digit

How to write this under exam pressure
  1. The last digit is n % b, the rest is n / b: write that down first.
  2. Recursion: the rest goes on the left, so call first, append after.
  3. Check n = 0 separately: it is the only number whose representation is a 0.

In short: "rest on the left, last digit on the right".

    My answer

  1. What does one round do?
    My answer

    The current bit is 1 exactly when x ≥ 1. Print it, subtract it, double: x = 2 * (x - bit). The new x again lies in [0, 2) and starts with the next bit.

  2. How many rounds, and where does the point go?
    My answer

    k + 1 rounds: one for $b_0$, then the point, then k. A for loop with a counter replaces the script's while (x != 0); the counter guarantees the end (Rule 1: never rely on a floating point value becoming exactly 0).

  3. Is the subtraction and doubling exact in double?
    My answer

    Yes: subtracting 1 from a number in [1, 2) and multiplying by 2 only shift bits, nothing has to be rounded. So the program prints the true bits of the stored x, which for 1.1 is the rounded double; its first 50 bits are those of 1.1.

Worked examples: a mixed set before the exercises

Worked example 5: why int to float can change a value (IFMP January 2015, task 3, first statement)

"Each number of type int is convertible into the type float, without change in value." True or false?

  1. How many significant bits does a float have, and an int?
    Answer

    float: p = 24. int: 31 bits for the value plus the sign.

  2. Find an int that needs more than 24 significant bits.
    Answer

    $2^{24} + 1 = 16\,777\,217$ = 1 followed by 23 zeros and a 1: 25 significant bits. As a float it is rounded to $2^{24}$ = 16 777 216. Try it:

    #include <iostream>

int main() {
  int i = 16777217;
  float f = i;
  std::cout << (f == 16777216.0f) << "\n";
  return 0;
}
    1

    So the statement is false (official). Every int fits into a double (53 bits), though.

Worked example 6: the script's surprise, $16^8 = 0$ (p. 134)

  1. Write 16 in binary. What does multiplying by 16 do to the bits?
    Answer

    16 = 10000 = $2^4$. Multiplying by 16 shifts all bits four places to the left and fills with zeros.

  2. Which bits are set in $16^8$, and what is left in 32 bits?
    Answer

    $16^8 = 2^{32}$: a single 1 at position 32, one place beyond the 32 bits (positions 0 to 31). All 32 stored bits are 0. Modulo $2^{32}$ the result is 0; $16^7 = 2^{28}$ still fits.

  3. And in hex?
    Answer

    Each factor 16 appends one hex digit 0: $16^7$ = 0x10000000 (eight hex digits), $16^8$ = 0x100000000 needs nine. The ninth digit, the 1, is cut off.

Exercise 1 (IFMP January 2015, task 3, official exam) · 6 points

Assume IEEE 754. True or false? (1 point each)

  1. Each number of type int is convertible into the type float, without change in value.
  2. For any four floating point variables a, b, c and d: a * b + c * d == d * c + b * a.
  3. The expression 1.1 - 1.0 == 0.1 has value true.
  4. A normalized floating point number has a value of at least 1.
  5. There are values a of type double for which a + 1 == a.
  6. The two literals 1.1e1 and 1.1e1f have the same value.
Solution

Frame: each statement: is there a rounding step that could change something? IEEE rounds the exact result of every single operation.

1. False (official): worked example 5. 2. True (official): a * b and b * a round the same exact product, and so do the two sums, whose operands are the same rounded products in swapped order. 3. False (official): 1.1 is periodic in binary (worked example 4). 4. False (official): normalized only means $d_0 \ne 0$; $1.0\cdot 2^{-1} = 0.5$ is normalized. 5. True (official): for large a, say $10^{20}$, the 1 falls into a hole (Rule 2). 6. True (official): both are 11, an integer with few bits, exact in both types.

  1. The literal 1e7f has the same value as the literal 8e4f.
  2. For any two floating point variables a and b: a * b == b * a.
  3. For any three floating point variables a, b and c: a + b + c == c + b + a.
  4. For any floating point variable a: a + 1/2 == a + 0.5.

(A purist's footnote: if a is NaN, a NaN is never equal to anything, even itself.) 3. Exercise 3 (IFMP August 2016, task 5, official exam) · 8 points

Consider the normalized floating point system $F^*(2, 3, -3, 3)$. Round as follows: a 1 directly behind the last significant digit rounds up, a 0 rounds down. (Example: 1.010… becomes 1.01, 1.011… becomes 1.10.)

  1. How many different positive values does $F^*$ contain? (1 point)
  2. Give the largest number and the smallest positive number in $F^*$, in decimal. (2 points)
  3. Compute (0.75 + 0.5) + 10 and (10 + 0.75) + 0.5 as the parentheses say, representing every intermediate result in $F^*$, binary and decimal. (5 points)
Hint 1

Write every number as $1.d_1d_2\cdot 2^e$ first: 0.75 = $1.10\cdot 2^{-1}$, 0.5 = $1.00\cdot 2^{-1}$, 10 = $1.01\cdot 2^3$.

Hint 2

To add $1.10\cdot 2^{-1}$ to $1.01\cdot 2^3$, write the small one with exponent 3: shift its point four places left, $0.000110\cdot 2^3$. Add, then look at the fourth digit to round.

Solution

Frame: the five steps: count, extremes, then add with denormalize, add, renormalize, round after every operation.

(a) 4 mantissas (1.00, 1.01, 1.10, 1.11) times 7 exponents (−3 … 3): 28 (official).

(b) Largest: $1.11\cdot 2^3 = 1.75\cdot 8 =$ 14. Smallest positive: $1.00\cdot 2^{-3} =$ 1/8 (official).

(c) Left: $1.10\cdot 2^{-1} + 1.00\cdot 2^{-1} = 10.10\cdot 2^{-1} = 1.01\cdot 2^0$ (1.25, exact). Then $+\,1.01\cdot 2^3$: $0.00101\cdot 2^3 + 1.01000\cdot 2^3 = 1.01101\cdot 2^3$; the digit after 1.01 is 1, round up: $1.10\cdot 2^3$ = 12.

Then $+\,1.00\cdot 2^{-1}$: $1.0100 + 0.0001 = 1.0101$, round down: $1.01\cdot 2^3$ = 10 (official). The exact value is 11.25; one order gives 12, the other 10. That is Rule 2 at work: small numbers added one at a time to a large one vanish.

Summary: what to keep from lesson 8

  • Base b: to decimal by Horner (value · b + digit); from decimal by dividing by b, remainders last digit first. Binary ↔ hex: groups of four bits. Literals 0b… and 0x….
  • B bits give $2^B$ patterns, and the arithmetic is modulo $2^B$. In two's complement, −a is stored as $2^B - a$ (invert and add 1); the MSB is the sign. That is why int runs from $-2^{31}$ to $2^{31} - 1$.
  • $F(\beta, p, e_{\min}, e_{\max})$: p significant digits, an exponent in a range. Normalized $F^*$: $d_0 \ne 0$; no 0, smallest positive $\beta^{e_{\min}}$. In base 2: $2^{p-1}$ mantissas per exponent.
  • Adding: same exponent, add, renormalize, round; round after every operation, so the order matters.
  • IEEE 754: float = $F^*(2, 24, -126, 127)$ in 32 bits, double = $F^*(2, 53, -1022, 1023)$ in 64 bits; every operation rounds the exact result.
  • Decimal fractions are often periodic in binary (0.1 = $0.0\overline{0011}$), so 0.1 is never stored exactly.
  • Rules: no equality tests; don't add very different magnitudes (sum small to large); don't subtract nearly equal numbers.

For wonder: the Patriot missile and 0.1 (optional, 5 min)

In February 1991, during the Gulf War, a Patriot air defence battery in Dhahran failed to intercept an incoming missile; 28 soldiers died. The investigation (US General Accounting Office, 1992) found a number representation error. The system counted time in tenths of a second and multiplied by 0.1, stored in a 24-bit fixed point register. Since 0.1 is periodic in binary, the stored value was off by about 0.000000095. After 100 hours of continuous operation, that tiny error had grown to about 0.34 seconds, and a missile flies more than half a kilometre in that time. The tracking looked in the wrong place. A software update that fixed it arrived the day after. The bits of worked example 4, $0.0\overline{0011}$, are not only an exam topic.
