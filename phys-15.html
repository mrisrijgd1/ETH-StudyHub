Recap of lesson 14 (3 min)

  1. Write the first law with the script's arrows. Which way does each arrow point?

    Answer

    $dU=\delta W^\swarrow+\delta Q^\swarrow$. $\swarrow$ is into the gas, $\nearrow$ is out of it, and $W^\nearrow=-W^\swarrow$.

  2. What is $\Delta U$ for an ideal gas, and for which processes does it hold?

    Answer

    $\Delta U=\nu C_V\Delta T$ for every process, because $U=\frac f2\nu RT$ depends only on $T$.

  3. What are $C_p-C_V$ and $\kappa$ for air?

    Answer

    $C_p-C_V=R$; air has $f=5$, so $\kappa=\frac75=1.4$.

1. The story: the engine room

Deep under the castle, the engineer runs a great piston engine. A furnace heats the gas in the cylinder; the gas pushes the piston out and turns the mill wheel; then cold water from the river cools the gas, and the piston slides back in, ready for the next stroke. Round and round.

The lord asks: “Why throw away all that heat into the river? Use it all!” The engineer shakes her head. “No engine can. Some heat must always go to the cold side, or the piston never comes back. The only question is how much, and that is decided by how hot the furnace is and how cold the river is.”

In physics: a heat engine runs a gas through a cyclic process, a closed loop in the $p$–$V$ diagram. It takes heat from a hot reservoir, turns part of it into work, and must give the rest to a cold reservoir: that is the second law. The best possible engine between $T_w$ and $T_k$ is the Carnot engine, with efficiency $1-\frac{T_k}{T_w}$. Today we build the loops from four simple pieces: the changes of state at constant $V$, constant $p$, constant $T$, and without heat.

2. The four changes of state

For each one, the recipe from lesson 14: $\Delta U=\nu C_V\Delta T$ always, $W^\swarrow=-\int p\,dV$, and $Q^\swarrow=\Delta U-W^\swarrow$.

Isochoric ($V$ constant). No work, so all the heat goes into $U$: $Q^\swarrow=\Delta U=\nu C_V\Delta T$. Pressure and temperature rise together, $\frac pT=$ const.

Isobaric ($p$ constant, page 297). $W^\swarrow=-p\,(V_e-V_a)=-\nu R\Delta T$, and $Q^\swarrow=\nu C_p\Delta T$, as in lesson 14. $\frac VT=$ const.

Isothermal ($T$ constant, pages 298–299). The gas sits in a heat bath. Then $\Delta U=0$, so all the work comes from heat: $Q^\swarrow=-W^\swarrow$. With $p=\frac{\nu RT}V$:

$$Q^\swarrow=W^\nearrow=\int_{V_1}^{V_2}\frac{\nu RT}V\,dV=\nu RT\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}.$$

An isothermal expansion turns heat completely into work, but only once: the gas ends up at a larger volume and must be pushed back before it can do it again.

Adiabatic (no heat, pages 299–302). The gas is insulated, or the process is so fast that no heat can flow. Then $Q^\swarrow=0$ and $\Delta U=W^\swarrow$: work done on the gas heats it, work done by the gas cools it. Setting $\nu C_V\,dT=-p\,dV=-\frac{\nu RT}V\,dV$ gives $\frac{dT}T=-\frac R{C_V}\frac{dV}V=-(\kappa-1)\frac{dV}V$, and integrating gives the Poisson equations:

$$TV^{\kappa-1}=\text{const},\qquad pV^\kappa=\text{const},\qquad T^\kappa p^{1-\kappa}=\text{const}.$$

Since $\kappa>1$, an adiabat is steeper than an isotherm in the $p$–$V$ diagram (page 301): compressing without letting heat out raises the pressure twice, once for the smaller volume and once for the higher temperature.

Try it: four ways to change a gas

One mole of air starts at $300\,\mathrm K$ and $25\,\mathrm L$. Choose a process and how far to go, then press Go. The shaded area under the path is the work. For the isochoric process the slider sets the pressure ratio instead.

Compare isothermal and adiabatic compression to half the volume: the adiabat ends at a higher pressure and a higher temperature ($396\,\mathrm K$), and it takes more work. In the isothermal case the bath swallows the heat.

Worked example 1: the bicycle pump (the script's example)

Air ($\kappa=1.4$) at $p_a=1\,\mathrm{bar}$ and $T_a=300\,\mathrm K$ is compressed adiabatically to a tenth of its volume (page 301). Find the final pressure and temperature. ($10^{0.4}\approx2.51$.)

  1. Which Poisson equation gives the pressure directly?

    Answer

    $p_eV_e^\kappa=p_aV_a^\kappa$, so $p_e=p_a\left(\frac{V_a}{V_e}\right)^\kappa=1\cdot10^{1.4}\,\mathrm{bar}$ $=10\cdot2.51\,\mathrm{bar}=25.1\,\mathrm{bar}$.

  2. And the temperature?

    Answer

    $T_eV_e^{\kappa-1}=T_aV_a^{\kappa-1}$, so $T_e=T_a\cdot10^{0.4}=753\,\mathrm K\approx480\,^\circ\mathrm C$.

  3. Check with $pV=\nu RT$, which holds in every state.

    Answer

    $\frac{T_e}{T_a}=\frac{p_eV_e}{p_aV_a}=25.1\cdot\frac1{10}=2.51$. ✓

Result: $25.1\,\mathrm{bar}$ and $753\,\mathrm K$. An isothermal compression would give only $10\,\mathrm{bar}$ at $300\,\mathrm K$. This heating is how a Diesel engine ignites its fuel without a spark plug.

3. The second law in words

The first law says energy is conserved. It does not say which way energy may flow. The second law does, in two equivalent forms (section 10.7.1, pages 303–304):

  • Thomson (Kelvin): no periodically working machine can take heat from one reservoir and turn it completely into work, with no other change. (Work into heat completely is easy: friction.)
  • Clausius: no periodically working machine can move heat from a colder to a hotter reservoir without work (or other energy) being put in. (Hot to cold happens by itself.)

The word “periodically” matters. A single isothermal expansion does turn heat completely into work, but the machine is not back where it started. To repeat, it needs a cycle, and a cycle must dump some heat at a lower temperature.

4. Cyclic processes and the Carnot engine

In a cycle the gas returns to its start, so $\Delta U_{\text{cycle}}=0$ and the net work done by the gas equals the net heat it takes in: $W^\nearrow_{\text{net}}=\sum Q^\swarrow$. In the $p$–$V$ diagram $W^\nearrow_{\text{net}}$ is the enclosed area. If the loop runs clockwise, the gas expands at high pressure and is compressed at low pressure: it does net work, an engine. If it runs counterclockwise, net work is done on the gas: a refrigerator or heat pump.

The efficiency of an engine is what you get over what you pay:

$$\eta=\frac{W^\nearrow_{\text{net}}}{Q^\swarrow_{\text{in}}},$$

where $Q^\swarrow_{\text{in}}$ is only the heat taken in (the sum of the positive $Q^\swarrow$), not the heat given off.

The Carnot cycle (pages 306–309) uses only the two reservoirs and no other heat exchange: two isotherms joined by two adiabats.

StepProcessTemperatureHeat
A: 1 → 2isothermal expansion$T_w$$Q^\swarrow_1=\nu RT_w\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}$ taken in
B: 2 → 3adiabatic expansion$T_w\to T_k$$0$
C: 3 → 4isothermal compression$T_k$$Q^\nearrow_2=\nu RT_k\ln\frac{V_3}{V_4}$ given off
D: 4 → 1adiabatic compression$T_k\to T_w$$0$

The two adiabatic steps exchange the same work $\nu C_V(T_w-T_k)$ in opposite directions, so they cancel. And the Poisson equation on both adiabats gives $\frac{V_2}{V_1}=\frac{V_3}{V_4}$ (page 309), so the two logarithms are equal. What is left:

$$\eta_C=\frac{Q^\swarrow_1-Q^\nearrow_2}{Q^\swarrow_1}=\frac{T_w-T_k}{T_w}=1-\frac{T_k}{T_w}.$$

It depends only on the two temperatures (in kelvin), not on the gas or the volumes. Every real engine between the same two temperatures does worse: $\eta_{\text{real}}<\eta_C$. The script's proof (page 311): if a “super-Carnot” engine existed, it could drive a Carnot engine running backwards and pump heat from cold to hot for free, which Clausius forbids.

Running it backwards (page 310). Put work $W^\swarrow$ in and the Carnot cycle pumps heat from the cold reservoir to the hot one. Again, what you get over what you pay:

$$\eta_W=\frac{Q^\nearrow_{\text{hot}}}{W^\swarrow}=\frac{T_w}{T_w-T_k}>1\quad\text{(heat pump)},\qquad\eta_K=\frac{Q^\swarrow_{\text{cold}}}{W^\swarrow}=\frac{T_k}{T_w-T_k}\quad\text{(refrigerator)}.$$

A heat pump delivers more heat than the work you put in, because it also brings the heat it took from outside. And $\eta_K\to0$ as $T_k\to0$: the colder the inside of the fridge already is, the harder it is to cool further.

Try it: the Carnot cycle

Set the two reservoir temperatures for one mole of helium (a one-atom gas, $\kappa=\frac53$; with air the loop would be even thinner). Press Go to run the cycle: heat flows in on the hot isotherm and out on the cold one.

Bring $T_k$ close to $T_w$: the loop gets thin and the efficiency goes to zero. A big temperature difference makes a fat loop and a good engine.

Recipe: a cyclic process (problem 5)

  1. States. Make a table of $p$, $V$, $T$ at each corner. Fill it with $pV=\nu RT$ and the law of each step ($p$, $V$ or $T$ constant, or Poisson).
  2. Steps. For each step: $\Delta U=\nu C_V\Delta T$, $W^\swarrow$ from the table in section 2, $Q^\swarrow=\Delta U-W^\swarrow$. Say which arrow you use.
  3. Check. $\sum\Delta U=0$ around the loop.
  4. Net work. $W^\nearrow_{\text{net}}=-\sum W^\swarrow=\sum Q^\swarrow$. Positive (clockwise): engine. Negative (counterclockwise): refrigerator or heat pump.
  5. Efficiency. $\eta=\frac{W^\nearrow_{\text{net}}}{Q^\swarrow_{\text{in}}}$, with only the heat taken in. It must come out below $1-\frac{T_{\min}}{T_{\max}}$.

Worked example 2: a Carnot engine and its reverse

A Carnot engine runs between $600\,\mathrm K$ and $300\,\mathrm K$ and takes $1000\,\mathrm J$ of heat per cycle from the hot reservoir. (a) Find the work and the heat given off. (b) Run backwards as a heat pump, how much heat does it deliver per joule of work? (c) And as a refrigerator, how much heat does it remove per joule?

  1. Efficiency first.

    Answer

    $\eta_C=1-\frac{300}{600}=0.5$. So $W^\nearrow=\eta_CQ^\swarrow_1=500\,\mathrm J$, and the rest, $Q^\nearrow_2=500\,\mathrm J$, goes to the cold reservoir.

  2. Heat pump and refrigerator?

    Answer

    $\eta_W=\frac{600}{300}=2$: $2\,\mathrm J$ of heat delivered per joule of work. $\eta_K=\frac{300}{300}=1$: $1\,\mathrm J$ removed from the cold side per joule.

Result: (a) $W^\nearrow=500\,\mathrm J$, $Q^\nearrow_2=500\,\mathrm J$; (b) $2\,\mathrm J$; (c) $1\,\mathrm J$. Note $\eta_W=\eta_K+1=\frac1{\eta_C}$: the delivered heat is the removed heat plus the work.

5. Real engines: Otto, Diesel, Stirling

The script sketches three engines (pages 305–313). Each is a cycle of the four pieces from section 2:

  • Otto (petrol): adiabatic compression, isochoric heating (the spark ignites the fuel so fast that the piston doesn't move), adiabatic expansion (the power stroke), isochoric cooling (exhaust). Its efficiency depends only on the compression ratio $r=\frac{V_{\max}}{V_{\min}}$: $\eta=1-r^{1-\kappa}$ (Exercise 8).
  • Diesel: like Otto, but the fuel burns at about constant pressure. It allows a higher compression and a better efficiency.
  • Stirling (hot air): two isotherms joined by two isochores. Ideally, with a heat store (regenerator) between the isochores, it reaches the Carnot efficiency. It appeared at the end of FS23 problem 5 (lesson 16).
Why it's cool

Carnot found his limit in 1824, before anyone knew that heat is energy, by thinking only about which way heat can flow. It is still the law every power plant, car engine and fridge obeys: a coal plant with steam at $800\,\mathrm K$ and a river at $300\,\mathrm K$ can never beat $62\,\%$, whatever the engineers build.

6. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed. Exercises 1–6 are multiple choice, 7 and 8 are long problems.

Exercise 1 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.5(a), 1 point, original)

Which statement about changes of state is correct? (A) An isothermal change happens without heat exchange. (B) An adiabatic change happens without heat exchange. (C) In an isobaric change no work is done. (D) In an adiabatic change no work is done.

Solution

B. That is the definition of adiabatic. (A): an isothermal change needs heat exchange to keep $T$ fixed ($Q^\swarrow=-W^\swarrow\neq0$). (C): isobaric work is $-p\Delta V\neq0$. (D): adiabatic work is $\nu C_V\Delta T\neq0$.

Exercise 2 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.5(d), 1 point, original)

The volume of an ideal gas is reduced at constant temperature. What holds for the heat supplied $Q^\swarrow$, the work supplied $W^\swarrow$ and $\Delta U$? (A) $Q^\swarrow<0$, $W^\swarrow<0$, $\Delta U=0$ (B) $Q^\swarrow=0$, $W^\swarrow>0$, $\Delta U>0$ (C) $Q^\swarrow>0$, $W^\swarrow<0$, $\Delta U>0$ (D) $Q^\swarrow<0$, $W^\swarrow>0$, $\Delta U=0$

Solution

D. Constant $T$: $\Delta U=0$. Compression: work is done on the gas, $W^\swarrow=-\nu RT\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}>0$ since $V_2<V_1$. So $Q^\swarrow=-W^\swarrow<0$: the gas gives that energy off as heat to the bath.

Exercise 3 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.5(c), 1 point, original)

What holds for a Carnot cycle? (A) Run as an engine, its efficiency depends only on the temperature of the cold reservoir. (B) Run as a refrigerator, its efficiency depends only on the temperature of the hot reservoir. (C) The efficiency depends on the temperatures of both the hot and the cold reservoir. (D) The efficiency depends only on the temperature of the hot reservoir.

Solution

C. $\eta_C=1-\frac{T_k}{T_w}$, $\eta_K=\frac{T_k}{T_w-T_k}$, $\eta_W=\frac{T_w}{T_w-T_k}$: all three need both temperatures.

Exercise 4 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.5(c), 1 point, original)

An engine follows a Carnot cycle between $T_w=926.85\,^\circ\mathrm C$ and $T_k=126.85\,^\circ\mathrm C$. What is its efficiency? (A) $33\,\%$ (B) $50\,\%$ (C) $67\,\%$ (D) $86\,\%$

Solution

C. The odd decimals are a hint to convert to kelvin: $T_w=1200\,\mathrm K$, $T_k=400\,\mathrm K$. $\eta_C=1-\frac{400}{1200}=\frac23\approx67\,\%$. (With Celsius you'd get the wrong $86\,\%$, option D: the trap.)

Exercise 5 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.5(a), 1 point, original)

What is the efficiency of an arbitrary heat pump between a cold reservoir $T_K$ and a warm reservoir $T_W$, which uses mechanical work to warm the warm reservoir? (A) The ratio of the heat supplied to the gas to the total work done on the gas. (B) The ratio of the heat given off by the gas to the total work done on the gas. (C) $\frac{T_W-T_K}{T_W}$ (D) $\frac{T_K}{T_W-T_K}$

Solution

B. A heat pump is for heating: what you get is the heat the gas gives off to the warm reservoir, what you pay is the work. (C) is the Carnot engine efficiency and (D) the Carnot refrigerator; neither applies to an arbitrary pump. (A) would be the refrigerator's “get”, the heat taken from the cold side.

Exercise 6 (FS23, Aufgabe 1.4(a)–(b), “Kreisprozess”, 4 points, original)

One mole of ideal gas with $C_V=15\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$ runs through a cycle (open the original for the figure): an isothermal expansion A → B at $T_2=500\,\mathrm K$, an isochoric cooling B → C to $T_1=300\,\mathrm K$, an isothermal step C → D, and an adiabatic compression D → A. (a) What work is done from D to A? (A) $3000\,\mathrm J$ (B) $4500\,\mathrm J$ (C) $6500\,\mathrm J$ (D) $9000\,\mathrm J$ (b) What heat is given off between B and C? (A) $1000\,\mathrm J$ (B) $3000\,\mathrm J$ (C) $5000\,\mathrm J$ (D) $7000\,\mathrm J$

Solution

(a) A. Adiabatic: $Q=0$, so $W^\swarrow_{DA}=\Delta U=\nu C_V(T_2-T_1)$ $=1\cdot15\cdot200\,\mathrm J=3000\,\mathrm J$, done on the gas.

(b) B. Isochoric: $W=0$, so $Q^\nearrow_{BC}=-\Delta U$ $=\nu C_V(T_2-T_1)=3000\,\mathrm J$. The same number for a different reason: both steps go between the same two temperatures, and $\Delta U$ depends only on them.

Exercise 7 (HS23, problem 5, “Thermodynamischer Kreisprozess”, 10 points, original)

A one-atom ideal gas runs through the cycle A → B → C → D → A. A is at $(V_0,p_0)$ with temperature $T_0$. A → B is isothermal down to $p_0/2$; B → C is at $p_0/2$ out to $3V_0$; C → D is at $3V_0$ up to $p_0$; D → A is at $p_0$ back to $V_0$. (a) Find $T_B$, $T_C$, $T_D$ in terms of $T_0$, and $V_B$. (b) Give the sign (+, −, 0) of $\Delta U$, $Q^\swarrow$ and $W^\swarrow$ for every step. (c) Compute the total work $W^\swarrow_{\text{tot}}$ done on the gas in one cycle, in terms of $p_0$ and $V_0$. (d) Compute the heat given off $Q^\nearrow_{D\to A}$. (e) Is this cycle, in this direction, an engine or a refrigerator? One sentence.

Solution

(a) Isothermal A → B: $p_0V_0=\frac{p_0}2V_B$, so $V_B=2V_0$ and $T_B=T_0$. Since $T\propto pV$: $T_C=\frac{\frac{p_0}2\cdot3V_0}{p_0V_0}T_0=\frac32T_0$, and $T_D=\frac{p_0\cdot3V_0}{p_0V_0}T_0=3T_0$.

(b) Using $\Delta U\propto\Delta T$, $W^\swarrow=-\int p\,dV$, $Q^\swarrow=\Delta U-W^\swarrow$:

Step$\Delta U$$Q^\swarrow$$W^\swarrow$
A → B (isothermal expansion)0+−
B → C (isobaric expansion)++−
C → D (isochoric, $p$ rises)++0
D → A (isobaric compression)−−+

(c) With $\nu RT_0=p_0V_0$: $W^\swarrow_{AB}=-\nu RT_0\ln\frac{V_B}{V_A}=-p_0V_0\ln2$. $W^\swarrow_{BC}=-\frac{p_0}2(3V_0-2V_0)=-\frac12p_0V_0$. $W^\swarrow_{CD}=0$. $W^\swarrow_{DA}=-p_0(V_0-3V_0)=+2p_0V_0$. Total: $W^\swarrow_{\text{tot}}=\left(\frac32-\ln2\right)p_0V_0\approx0.81\,p_0V_0>0$.

(d) $\Delta U_{DA}=\frac32\nu R(T_0-3T_0)=-3p_0V_0$, so $Q^\swarrow_{DA}=\Delta U-W^\swarrow$ $=-3p_0V_0-2p_0V_0=-5p_0V_0$. Given off: $Q^\nearrow_{D\to A}=5p_0V_0$. (Check: $\nu C_p\Delta T=\frac52\cdot(-2p_0V_0)$. ✓)

(e) A refrigerator (or heat pump): net work is done on the gas ($W^\swarrow_{\text{tot}}>0$), because the loop runs counterclockwise in the $p$–$V$ diagram.

Exercise 8 (FS26, problem 5, “Otto-Motor Kreisprozess”, 10 points, original)

An ideal Otto engine: $n$ moles of gas with known $C_V$, $C_P$, $\kappa$ run through A → B adiabatic compression, B → C isochoric pressure rise, C → D adiabatic expansion, D → A isochoric pressure drop. (a) Sketch the cycle in the $p$–$V$ diagram (A is at the large volume, low pressure). (b) How do you get $\kappa$ from $C_V$ and $C_P$? What is $C_P-C_V$ for an ideal gas? (c) Find $\frac{V_B}{V_A}$ in terms of the temperatures. (d) Find $Q_{B\to C}$ and $Q_{D\to A}$ in terms of $n$, $C_V$ and the temperatures. In which step is heat supplied? (e) Find the total work $W^\nearrow_{\text{ges}}$ over one cycle. (f) Derive the efficiency from (d) and (e) and express it with the compression ratio $r=\frac{V_A}{V_B}$ and $\kappa$.

Solution

(a) From A (right, low) up the steep adiabat to B (left, higher $p$), straight up to C (isochoric), down the adiabat to D (right), straight down to A. A clockwise loop between two vertical lines: an engine.

(b) $\kappa=\frac{C_P}{C_V}$, and $C_P-C_V=R$ (per mole).

(c) Poisson on A → B: $T_AV_A^{\kappa-1}=T_BV_B^{\kappa-1}$, so $\frac{V_B}{V_A}=\left(\frac{T_A}{T_B}\right)^{\frac1{\kappa-1}}$. The same on C → D, with $V_C=V_B$ and $V_D=V_A$, gives also $\left(\frac{T_D}{T_C}\right)^{\frac1{\kappa-1}}$, so $\frac{T_A}{T_B}=\frac{T_D}{T_C}$.

(d) Isochoric, so $Q^\swarrow=\Delta U$: $Q^\swarrow_{B\to C}=nC_V(T_C-T_B)>0$ (heat supplied: the fuel burns), $Q^\swarrow_{D\to A}=nC_V(T_A-T_D)<0$ (heat given off).

(e) Over a cycle $\Delta U=0$ and the adiabats exchange no heat: $W^\nearrow_{\text{ges}}=Q^\swarrow_{B\to C}+Q^\swarrow_{D\to A}$ $=nC_V(T_C-T_B+T_A-T_D)$.

(f) $\eta=\frac{W^\nearrow_{\text{ges}}}{Q^\swarrow_{B\to C}}=1-\frac{T_D-T_A}{T_C-T_B}$. From (c), $T_A=T_Br^{1-\kappa}$ and $T_D=T_Cr^{1-\kappa}$, so $T_D-T_A=(T_C-T_B)r^{1-\kappa}$ and

$$\eta=1-r^{1-\kappa}=1-\frac1{r^{\kappa-1}}.$$

For a car engine with $r=10$ and air: $1-10^{-0.4}\approx60\,\%$ in theory; real engines reach about half of that.

Scoring yourself: the exercises make 30 points. With 20 you're done. Exercise 7 is the most typical problem 5 there is: if you can do it without looking, you are ready for most of them.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Fire from air. The fire piston, used for centuries in Southeast Asia, is a closed tube with a tight piston. Slam it down and the air inside heats adiabatically to several hundred degrees, enough to light a piece of tinder at the bottom. It is Worked example 1 in your hand.

Why a heat pump beats a heater. An electric heater turns each joule of electricity into one joule of heat. A heat pump between $0\,^\circ\mathrm C$ outside and $35\,^\circ\mathrm C$ in the floor pipes could ideally deliver $\frac{308}{35}\approx9$ joules per joule; real ones reach three to four. That is why Switzerland is replacing oil heating with them.
