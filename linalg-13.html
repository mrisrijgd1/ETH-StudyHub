Why it matters for the exam

Change of basis is the most common computation in Linear Algebra exams after the matrix of a map itself. FS25, multiple choice (1 point; Worked example 3) asks for $[T]^B_B$ from the standard matrix of $T$ and a basis $B$ of $\mathbb R^3$, with $[\mathrm{Id}]$ written the wrong way round among the options. The true/false blocks ask whether “every invertible matrix is a change-of-basis matrix” (HS17, (xv); Proof C) and whether an isomorphism $V\to V^*$ maps a basis to its dual basis (FS17, (xxx); Worked example 5). FS20, problem 5 (a)–(b) (8 points; Exercise 3) asks whether $AB$ and $BA$ are similar. The proof questions of the final exam on diagonalisation (Linear Algebra II) are all change-of-basis questions: “find a basis in which the matrix of $T$ is …” is $Q^{-1}AQ$ in disguise. The mock exam at the end of the course also has a change-of-basis problem.

1. The story: two mapmakers, one city

The Free City has two guilds of mapmakers. The old guild draws its grid along the two great roads that meet at the fountain. The new guild prefers the river and the old wall, which meet at the same fountain but at a slant. Every house now has two addresses: “3 along the road, 2 along the other road” on the old map, and different numbers on the new map. Neither map is wrong; they describe the same houses.

The city clerks write a translation table: for each of the old guild's two reference roads, its address on the new map. With it, any old address can be turned into a new one: walk the old address step by step, and translate each step. Going back needs the other guild's table, and the two tables undo each other.

The couriers of lesson 11 keep their instruction tables in old addresses. A clerk who only reads the new map does not need a new courier: she translates the address into the old map, lets the courier run by his old table, and translates the result back. The courier is the same; only his table changes. And the clerks soon notice that some maps make a courier's table very simple: on the map along the river, the courier who mirrors everything in the river just keeps one road and turns the other round.

In mathematics: one vector space $V$ (the city) with two bases $B$ and $B'$ (the two grids). The transition matrix turns $B$-coordinates into $B'$-coordinates. A linear map $T$ (a courier) has a matrix in each basis, and the two matrices are related by a sandwich with transition matrices. Choosing a good basis makes the matrix simple, which is the central theme of Linear Algebra II.

2. One vector, two coordinate vectors

Throughout, $V$ is a vector space of dimension $n$ over $K$ with two ordered bases $B=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ and $B'=(v'_1,\dots,v'_n)$. Every $v\in V$ has a coordinate vector $[v]_B\in K^n$ and a coordinate vector $[v]_{B'}\in K^n$ (lesson 11). How are they related? The identity map $\mathrm{Id}_V$ does nothing to $v$, but its matrix with respect to two different bases records the translation.

Definition (transition matrix; script, Definition on p. 91). The matrix $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ is called the change-of-basis matrix or transition matrix from $B$ to $B'$. By the definition of the matrix of a map (column $j$ is the coordinate vector of the image of the $j$-th basis vector), its columns are

$$[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}=\Big(\,[v_1]_{B'}\;\Big|\;[v_2]_{B'}\;\Big|\;\cdots\;\Big|\;[v_n]_{B'}\,\Big):$$

the old basis vectors written in the new basis. For an endomorphism $T$ the script writes $[T]_B:=[T]^B_B$ (Note, p. 92).

How to read the indices (the part everyone gets wrong once). In $[T]^B_C$ the upper basis $B$ belongs to the space the map starts in, the lower basis $C$ to the space it lands in: $[T]^B_C$ eats $B$-coordinates and returns $C$-coordinates. So $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}$ eats $B$-coordinates and returns $B'$-coordinates. Read “from top to bottom”: from $B$ to $B'$. Other books write $T^{B'}_{B}$, $M^B_{B'}$, $S_{B'\leftarrow B}$ or put the bases the other way round; the exams follow the script, and the PVK sets sometimes do not, so always check one column.

In the lecture's notation: with $B=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, $B'=(\tilde x_1,\dots,\tilde x_n)$ and $x_j=\sum_i\alpha^i_j\tilde x_i$, the matrix $(\alpha^i_j)$ (row index $i$ up, column index $j$ down) is $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}$.

Proof A: the transition matrix translates coordinates (lecture Lemma 8.0; script Proposition 3.3.8 for $T=\mathrm{Id}_V$)

Claim. For every $v\in V$,

$$[v]_{B'}=[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}\,[v]_B.$$

The idea. This is lesson 11's rule $[T]^B_C[v]_B=[Tv]_C$ with $T=\mathrm{Id}_V$ and $C=B'$. It is worth proving once directly, as the lecture does, because the computation shows why the old basis vectors in new coordinates are the right columns: write $v$ in the old basis, replace each old basis vector by its expression in the new basis, and collect. Coordinates are unique, so what you collect is $[v]_{B'}$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $[v]_B=(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$ and $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}=(a_{ij})$, i.e. $v=\sum_j\lambda_jv_j$ and $v_j=\sum_ia_{ij}v'_i$.”
  • Last line: “So the $i$-th coordinate of $v$ in $B'$ is $\sum_ja_{ij}\lambda_j$, the $i$-th entry of $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}[v]_B$.”
  • The gap: substitute, swap the two finite sums, use uniqueness of coordinates.
The storyWhat you write

A house has an old address: so many steps along each old road.

Let $v\in V$ and $[v]_B=(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)^T$, so $v=\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_jv_j$. Let $(a_{ij})=[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}$; by definition its $j$-th column is $[v_j]_{B'}$, i.e. $v_j=\sum_{i=1}^na_{ij}v'_i$.

Translate each old road into the new map and walk the steps there.

Substituting: $v=\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_j\sum_{i=1}^na_{ij}v'_i$ $=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big(\sum_{j=1}^na_{ij}\lambda_j\Big)v'_i$ (finite sums can be swapped).

A house has only one new address, so this is it.

Coordinates with respect to $B'$ are unique, so the $i$-th entry of $[v]_{B'}$ is $\sum_ja_{ij}\lambda_j$, which is the $i$-th entry of $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}[v]_B$. As $i$ was arbitrary, $[v]_{B'}=[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}[v]_B$. ∎

Moves used: compute one element in two ways the matrix column by column

The proof in one piece

Let $[v]_B=(\lambda_j)$ and $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}=(a_{ij})$, so $v=\sum_j\lambda_jv_j$ and $v_j=\sum_ia_{ij}v'_i$. Then $v=\sum_j\lambda_j\sum_ia_{ij}v'_i=\sum_i\big(\sum_ja_{ij}\lambda_j\big)v'_i$. By uniqueness of coordinates, $[v]_{B'}$ has the entries $\sum_ja_{ij}\lambda_j$, i.e. $[v]_{B'}=[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}[v]_B$. (Alternatively: lesson 11, Proof E, with $T=\mathrm{Id}_V$.) ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Name the coordinates of $v$ in $B$ and the columns of the transition matrix by their defining equations.
  2. Substitute and swap the sums.
  3. Compare coefficients of $v'_i$ (uniqueness of coordinates).

In short: old basis vectors in new coordinates are the columns; then $[v]_{B'}=[\mathrm{Id}]^B_{B'}[v]_B$ is $[T][v]=[Tv]$ for $T=\mathrm{Id}$.

  1. Rung 1. In $V=K^n$ with the standard basis $E=(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ and another basis $B=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$: what is $[\mathrm{Id}]^B_E$?
    Answer

    Its columns are $[v_j]_E=v_j$ (a column vector is its own coordinate vector in the standard basis). So $[\mathrm{Id}]^B_E=(v_1|\cdots|v_n)$, the matrix whose columns are the basis vectors. This is the one transition matrix you can write down without any computation.

  2. Rung 2. Check Proof A on $V=K^2$, $B=(e_2,e_1)$, $B'=E=(e_1,e_2)$ and $v=(5,7)$.
    Answer

    $[v]_B=(7,5)$, since $v=7e_2+5e_1$. $[\mathrm{Id}]^B_E=(e_2|e_1)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, and $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}7\\5\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}5\\7\end{pmatrix}=[v]_E$ ✓. Reordering a basis permutes the coordinates.

3. Translating back: transition matrices are invertible

The clerks' two tables undo each other. In matrices: going from $B$ to $B'$ and back to $B$ is the identity, and lesson 12's product rule turns this into a matrix equation.

Proof B: transition matrices are invertible and inverse to each other (script Corollary 3.3.31, p. 92; lecture p. 47)

Claim. For two bases $B,B'$ of $V$ with $\dim V=n$,

$$[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}\in\mathrm{GL}_n(K),$$

and

$$\big([\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}\big)^{-1}=[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B.$$

The idea. $\mathrm{Id}_V\circ\mathrm{Id}_V=\mathrm{Id}_V$. Read the left side through the bases $B\to B'\to B$ and take matrices with $[S\circ T]=[S][T]$ (lesson 12, Proof A): the product of the two transition matrices is $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_B=I_n$. The same in the other order. A matrix with a two-sided inverse is invertible by definition.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “By Lemma 3.3.29, $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}=[\mathrm{Id}_V\circ\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_B$.”
  • Last line: “Both products are $I_n$, so $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}$ is invertible with inverse $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B$.”
  • The gap: $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^C_C=I_n$ for every basis $C$.
The storyWhat you write

Translating into the new map and back is a relay of two “do nothing” couriers.

By Lemma 3.3.29 (lesson 12, Proof A), applied to $\mathrm{Id}_V:(V,B)\to(V,B')$ followed by $\mathrm{Id}_V:(V,B')\to(V,B)$: $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B\,[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}=[\mathrm{Id}_V\circ\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_B=[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_B$.

Doing nothing on one map has the plain table.

For any basis $C=(c_1,\dots,c_n)$, $\mathrm{Id}_V(c_j)=c_j$ has $[c_j]_C=e_j$, so $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^C_C=I_n$. Hence $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}=I_n$.

The same relay the other way round.

Exchanging the roles of $B$ and $B'$: $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B=[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_{B'}=I_n$. So $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}\in\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$ with inverse $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B$. ∎

Moves used: turn matrices into maps and back show it is the inverse: compose both ways

The proof in one piece

By Lemma 3.3.29, $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}=[\mathrm{Id}_V\circ\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_B=[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_B=I_n$ and $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B=[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_{B'}=I_n$, because $\mathrm{Id}_V$ sends each basis vector to itself. Hence $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}$ is invertible with inverse $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Write $\mathrm{Id}_V=\mathrm{Id}_V\circ\mathrm{Id}_V$ and choose the bases $B\to B'\to B$.
  2. Apply the product rule and $[\mathrm{Id}]^C_C=I_n$.
  3. Do both orders: “invertible” needs a two-sided inverse at this point of the course (one side suffices for square matrices, but that is lesson 15).

In short: $[\mathrm{Id}]^{B'}_B[\mathrm{Id}]^B_{B'}=[\mathrm{Id}]^B_B=I_n$, and the same the other way round.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. In $K^n$ with $P=(v_1|\cdots|v_n)=[\mathrm{Id}]^B_E$: what is $[\mathrm{Id}]^E_B$, and how do you get $[v]_B$ from $v$?
    Answer

    $[\mathrm{Id}]^E_B=P^{-1}$ by Proof B, and $[v]_B=P^{-1}v$ by Proof A. Finding coordinates in a basis of $K^n$ means inverting the matrix of basis vectors, or solving the system $Px=v$.

  2. Rung 2. Three bases $B,B',B''$ of $V$. Express $[\mathrm{Id}]^B_{B''}$ through the other transition matrices.
    Answer

    $\mathrm{Id}=\mathrm{Id}\circ\mathrm{Id}$ through $B\to B'\to B''$: $[\mathrm{Id}]^B_{B''}=[\mathrm{Id}]^{B'}_{B''}[\mathrm{Id}]^B_{B'}$. The indices “cancel” like fractions: the lower $B'$ of the right factor meets the upper $B'$ of the left factor. This is how you translate between two non-standard bases of $K^n$: through $E$, $[\mathrm{Id}]^B_{B'}=[\mathrm{Id}]^E_{B'}[\mathrm{Id}]^B_E=P'^{-1}P$.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof B from the frame alone.

Is every invertible matrix a transition matrix, or only some? HS17 asks exactly this in its true/false block. The answer is yes, and the proof is a good use of lesson 12: an invertible matrix belongs to an isomorphism, and isomorphisms map bases to bases.

Proof C: every invertible matrix is a transition matrix (HS17, (xv); script Exercise 3.3.36 needs it)

Claim. Let $B'=(v'_1,\dots,v'_n)$ be a basis of $V$ and $P=(p_{ij})\in\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$. Put $v_j:=\sum_{i=1}^np_{ij}v'_i$ for $j=1,\dots,n$. Then $B=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ is a basis of $V$, and $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}=P$.

The idea. The column $j$ of $P$ is meant to be $[v_j]_{B'}$, so $v_j$ is forced: it is the vector with those coordinates. The only question is whether these $v_j$ form a basis. Let $T$ be the endomorphism with $T(v'_j)=v_j$; its matrix is $P$. Since $P$ is invertible, $T$ is an isomorphism, and an isomorphism maps the basis $B'$ to a basis (lesson 12, Proof D).

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $T\in\mathrm{End}(V)$ be the linear map with $T(v'_j)=v_j$ (lesson 11, Proof C). Then $[T]_{B'}=P$.”
  • Last line: “$B=T(B')$ is a basis, and the $j$-th column of $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}$ is $[v_j]_{B'}=(p_{1j},\dots,p_{nj})$.”
  • The gap: $T$ is an isomorphism, because the map with matrix $P^{-1}$ is its inverse.

A courier who carries each new road to the place the table names.

By lesson 11, Proof C, there is a unique linear $T:V\to V$ with $T(v'_j)=v_j$ for all $j$. The $j$-th column of $[T]_{B'}=[T]^{B'}_{B'}$ is $[T(v'_j)]_{B'}=[v_j]_{B'}=(p_{1j},\dots,p_{nj})^T$, so $[T]_{B'}=P$.

The inverse table gives a courier who walks every route back.

Let $S\in\mathrm{End}(V)$ with $[S]_{B'}=P^{-1}$ (it exists, since $T\mapsto[T]_{B'}$ is bijective, lesson 11, Proof F). By lesson 12, Proof A, $[S\circ T]_{B'}=P^{-1}P=I_n=[\mathrm{Id}_V]_{B'}$ and $[T\circ S]_{B'}=PP^{-1}=I_n$. As $T\mapsto[T]_{B'}$ is injective, $S\circ T=T\circ S=\mathrm{Id}_V$: $T$ is an isomorphism.

A twin map sends a grid to a grid, and the table reads off as wanted.

An isomorphism maps a basis to a basis (lesson 12, Proof D), so $B=(T(v'_1),\dots,T(v'_n))=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ is a basis of $V$. Finally the $j$-th column of $[\mathrm{Id}_V]^B_{B'}$ is $[v_j]_{B'}=(p_{1j},\dots,p_{nj})^T$, the $j$-th column of $P$. ∎

The proof in one piece

Proof D: the change-of-basis formula (script Corollaries 3.3.30 and 3.3.32, pp. 91–92)

$$[T]^{B'}_{C'}=[\mathrm{Id}_W]^C_{C'}\,[T]^B_C\,[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B.$$

In particular, for an endomorphism $T\in\mathrm{End}(V)$ and $Q:=[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B$:

$$[T]_{B'}=Q^{-1}\,[T]_B\,Q.$$

The idea. $T=\mathrm{Id}_W\circ T\circ\mathrm{Id}_V$: a map does not change when you do nothing before and after it. But you may read each of the three factors with different bases: the first $\mathrm{Id}_V$ from $B'$ to $B$, then $T$ from $B$ to $C$, then $\mathrm{Id}_W$ from $C$ to $C'$. 5. Similar and equivalent matrices

Proof D says that the matrices of one endomorphism in different bases have the form $Q^{-1}AQ$, and the matrices of one map $V\to W$ have the form $PAQ$ with invertible $P,Q$. The script turns these shapes into definitions.

Definition (equivalent and similar matrices; script Definition 3.3.35, p. 93).

  • $A,B\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ are equivalent if there are $P\in\mathrm{GL}_m(K)$ and $Q\in\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$ with $PAQ=B$.
  • $A,B\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ are similar if there is $P\in\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$ with $P^{-1}AP=B$.

Similar matrices are equivalent (take $P^{-1}$ and $P$), but not conversely: $I_2$ and $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ are equivalent (both are invertible: $I_2\cdot I_2\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$) but not similar (below).

Proof E: similarity is an equivalence relation, and similar means “the same endomorphism” (script Exercise 3.3.36, p. 93)

Claim. (a) Similarity is an equivalence relation on $M_{n\times n}(K)$. (b) $A,A'\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ are similar if and only if there are an $n$-dimensional vector space $V$, an endomorphism $T\in\mathrm{End}(V)$ and bases $B,B'$ of $V$ with $A=[T]_B$ and $A'=[T]_{B'}$.

The idea. (a) Each of the three properties is one line with $\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$ being a group (lesson 12): $I_n$ for reflexivity, the inverse for symmetry, the product for transitivity. (b) One direction is Proof D. For the other, $A'=P^{-1}AP$ must be read as a change of basis: take $T=m_A$ on $K^n$, start in the standard basis, and let the new basis be the columns of $P$, which is a basis by Proof C.

Invariants: how to show that two matrices are not similar

To show that $A$ and $A'$ are similar, you name a $P$ (or a basis). To show that they are not, you cannot try all $P$. Instead, find a quantity that is the same for similar matrices and differs for $A$ and $A'$: an invariant. So far we have: “is $0$”, “is $\lambda I_n$”, “is invertible” (Rungs 1–2 above). The most useful one for quick checks is the trace.

Definition and Lemma (trace; script Definition 4.7.6 and Lemma 4.7.7, p. 153). The trace of $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ is $\mathrm{Tr}(A):=a_{11}+\dots+a_{nn}$. For $A,B\in M_{n\times n}(K)$, $\mathrm{Tr}(AB)=\mathrm{Tr}(BA)$.

Proof. $\mathrm{Tr}(AB)=\sum_i\sum_ka_{ik}b_{ki}=\sum_k\sum_ib_{ki}a_{ik}=\mathrm{Tr}(BA)$. ∎

Consequence. Similar matrices have the same trace: $\mathrm{Tr}(P^{-1}AP)=\mathrm{Tr}\big((AP)P^{-1}\big)=\mathrm{Tr}(A)$, using the Lemma with $P^{-1}$ and $AP$. So the trace of an endomorphism, $\mathrm{Tr}(T):=\mathrm{Tr}([T]_B)$, does not depend on the basis.

Careful: an invariant can only rule similarity out. $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $I_2$ have the same trace $2$, and both are invertible, but they are not similar: only $I_2$ is similar to $I_2$. Rank (lessons 15–16), determinant (lessons 18–20) and eigenvalues (lesson 21) are further invariants; even all of them together do not decide similarity in general, which is why Linear Algebra II needs normal forms. Equivalence, in contrast, is decided by one number: two $m\times n$ matrices are equivalent if and only if they have the same rank (script Proposition 3.3.37 and Corollary 3.3.40, pp. 93–94; lesson 16).

6. Changing the dual basis

Every basis $B$ of $V$ has its dual basis $B^*$ of $V^*$ (lesson 10). If we change $B$ to $C$, the dual basis changes from $B^*$ to $C^*$. How are the two transition matrices related? The answer explains lesson 10's recipe “the dual basis is in the rows of the inverse matrix”.

Proof F: dual bases change with the transpose (script, Lemma on p. 318, proof on p. 319)

Claim. Let $B=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ and $C=(w_1,\dots,w_n)$ be bases of $V$ with dual bases $B^*=(v_1^*,\dots,v_n^*)$ and $C^*=(w_1^*,\dots,w_n^*)$. Then

$$[\mathrm{id}_{V^*}]^{C^*}_{B^*}=\big([\mathrm{id}_V]^B_C\big)^T=\Big(\big([\mathrm{id}_V]^C_B\big)^T\Big)^{-1}.$$

The idea. The columns we need are $[w_j^*]_{B^*}$, the new dual vectors in the old dual basis. Lesson 10's Lemma 10.2.10 says that the coordinates of a functional in a dual basis are its values on the basis: $[w_j^*]_{B^*}=(w_j^*(v_1),\dots,w_j^*(v_n))$. And $w_j^*(v_i)$ is the $j$-th coordinate of $v_i$ in $C$, an entry of $[\mathrm{id}_V]^B_C$, but read along a row. Hence the transpose.

By Lemma 10.2.10, $[w_j^*]_{B^*}=\big(w_j^*(v_1),\dots,w_j^*(v_n)\big)^T=(a_{j1},\dots,a_{jn})^T$. This is the $j$-th column of $[\mathrm{id}_{V^*}]^{C^*}_{B^*}$, and it is the $j$-th row of $A$. Hence $[\mathrm{id}_{V^*}]^{C^*}_{B^*}=A^T$.

The second form: undo the other translation, then transpose.

By Proof B, $A=\big([\mathrm{id}_V]^C_B\big)^{-1}$, and $(M^{-1})^T=(M^T)^{-1}$ for invertible $M$ (lesson 12, Exercise 5 (c); from $(XY)^T=Y^TX^T$). So $A^T=\big(([\mathrm{id}_V]^C_B)^T\big)^{-1}$. ∎

Moves used: apply to a basis vector: the delta picks one term the matrix column by column

The proof in one piece

Let $A=(a_{ki})=[\mathrm{id}_V]^B_C$, so $v_i=\sum_ka_{ki}w_k$. Then $w_j^*(v_i)=\sum_ka_{ki}\delta_{jk}=a_{ji}$. By Lemma 10.2.10, $[w_j^*]_{B^*}=(w_j^*(v_i))_i=(a_{j1},\dots,a_{jn})^T$, the $j$-th row of $A$ written as a column. So $[\mathrm{id}_{V^*}]^{C^*}_{B^*}=A^T$, and since $A=([\mathrm{id}_V]^C_B)^{-1}$ and $(M^{-1})^T=(M^T)^{-1}$, also $A^T=\big(([\mathrm{id}_V]^C_B)^T\big)^{-1}$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Name $A=[\mathrm{id}]^B_C$ by its columns: $v_i=\sum_ka_{ki}w_k$.
  2. Evaluate $w_j^*$ on $v_i$: the delta leaves $a_{ji}$.
  3. Coordinates in a dual basis are values (Lemma 10.2.10): column $j$ is row $j$ of $A$.

In short: functionals change with the transposed matrix of the other direction; in $K^n$ with $C=E$ and $P=(v_1|\cdots|v_n)$, the coordinates of $v_j^*$ in $E^*$ are the rows of $P^{-1}$ (lesson 10, Proposition 10.2.7).

Why it's cool

A matrix is a picture of a map taken from one viewpoint, and change of basis is moving the camera. Some viewpoints make the map look simple: the reflection of Worked example 4 is $\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$ seen from the mirror, the symmetric map of the explorer is $\mathrm{diag}(3,1)$. Linear Algebra II is largely the search for the best viewpoint. In Physics I you meet the same idea as principal axes: the moment of inertia of a rigid body is a $3\times3$ matrix that becomes diagonal when you choose axes along the body's symmetry axes, and that is why a spinning top or a thrown book is analysed in those axes. For the Fibonacci map of Serie 8, exercise 35, the best basis gives a diagonal matrix with the entries $\frac{1\pm\sqrt5}2$, and the closed formula for the Fibonacci numbers falls out (lesson 21 and Linear Algebra II).

For wonder: why physicists put indices up and down (optional, 5 min)

Prof. Halbeisen writes the transition matrix as $(\alpha^i_j)$ with one index up and one down, and you will see the same in physics as “contravariant” and “covariant”. Rung 2 of Proof F shows why. If every basis vector is doubled, the coordinates of a vector are halved (they change against the basis: contravariant, upper index, $v=\sum_iv^ie_i$), while the dual basis is halved and the coordinates of a functional are doubled (they change with the basis: covariant, lower index, $\varphi=\sum_j\varphi_je^j$). The value $\varphi(v)=\sum_i\varphi_iv^i$ does not depend on the basis at all: the two changes cancel, which is Proof F in one line ($A^T$ on one side, $A^{-1}$ on the other). Einstein's convention of summing over an index that appears once up and once down is built on exactly this bookkeeping, and it is how general relativity keeps track of coordinate changes.

Worked example 5: quick exam items (HS17, (xv); FS17, (xxx); similarity checks)

True or false? Give a one-line reason or a counterexample.

  1. HS17, (xv): every invertible matrix is a change-of-basis matrix.
    Answer

    True. Proof C: for $P\in\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$ and any basis $B'$, the vectors $v_j=\sum_ip_{ij}v'_i$ form a basis $B$ with $[\mathrm{Id}]^B_{B'}=P$.

  2. FS17, (xxx): let $T:V\to V^*$ be an isomorphism, $B$ an ordered basis of $V$ and $B^*$ the (dual) basis of $V^*$. Then $T(B)=B^*$.
    Answer

    False. $V=K^2$, $B=(e_1,e_2)$, and $T$ the linear map with $T(e_1)=e_2^*$, $T(e_2)=e_1^*$. It maps a basis to a basis, so it is an isomorphism (lesson 12, Proof D), but $T(B)=(e_2^*,e_1^*)\ne(e_1^*,e_2^*)=B^*$ as ordered bases. (Even $T(e_1)=2e_1^*$, $T(e_2)=e_2^*$ works when $2\ne1$, i.e. over every field.) Isomorphisms $V\to V^*$ exist, but none is attached to every basis at once: that is the “not canonical” of lesson 10.

  3. $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ are similar.
    Answer

    True. They are the matrices of one map in the bases $(e_1,e_2)$ and $(e_2,e_1)$. With $P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=P^{-1}$: $P^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}P=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Reordering a basis permutes the diagonal.

  4. $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ are similar.
    Answer

    False. The traces are $5$ and $4$; similar matrices have equal traces.

  5. $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ is similar to the zero matrix.
    Answer

    False. $P^{-1}0P=0$ for all $P$, so only $0$ is similar to $0$. (The trace, $0$, does not see the difference: invariants only rule out.)

Worked example 6: the dual basis in two bases (script Example 10.2.6 and the Lemma on p. 318)
