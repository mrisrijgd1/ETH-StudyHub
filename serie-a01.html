Aufgabe 1 · Sets

Let $A,B,C$ be sets with $A\cup B=A\cup C$ and $A\cap B=A\cap C$. Show that $B=C$. (Drawings are not a proof.)

The idea

Every point is either inside $A$ or outside $A$. Inside $A$, the intersection equation tells you what $B$ and $C$ look like; outside $A$, the union equation does. Together the two equations pin $B$ and $C$ down everywhere, so they must be the same set.

Only for intuition: the proof below does not use the picture.

You need

Set equality by double inclusion: show $B\subseteq C$ and $C\subseteq B$, each starting with “Let $x\in B$ …”. See lesson 1 and the template in lesson 2.

Hint 1

Start with “Let $x\in B$.” You want $x\in C$. You don't know where $x$ is relative to $A$, so split into two cases: $x\in A$ and $x\notin A$.

Hint 2

If $x\in A$: then $x$ is in $A$ and in $B$. Which equation talks about “in $A$ and in $B$”? If $x\notin A$: then $x$ is in $A\cup B$. Which equation lets you swap $B$ for $C$ there?

Hint 3

Case $x\in A$: $x\in A\cap B=A\cap C$, so $x\in C$. Case $x\notin A$: $x\in A\cup B=A\cup C$, and $x\notin A$ leaves only $x\in C$. For $C\subseteq B$, notice that the two assumptions don't change if you swap $B$ and $C$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Two sets are equal when each is contained in the other. Start with one direction.

We show $B\subseteq C$. Let $x\in B$.

Inside $A$, the intersection equation describes $B$ and $C$.

Case $x\in A$. Then $x\in A\cap B=A\cap C$, hence $x\in C$.

Outside $A$, the union equation does. Since $x$ is not in $A$, the only way to be in $A\cup C$ is to be in $C$.

Case $x\notin A$. Then $x\in A\cup B=A\cup C$. Since $x\notin A$, it follows that $x\in C$.

The other direction is the same argument with the letters swapped. Saying so is enough.

Hence $B\subseteq C$. The assumptions are symmetric in $B$ and $C$, so the same argument with $B$ and $C$ exchanged gives $C\subseteq B$. Therefore $B=C$. ∎

Trap

Using only one of the two assumptions. With only $A\cup B=A\cup C$, take $A=\{1\}$, $B=\{1\}$, $C=\emptyset$: the unions agree but $B\ne C$. You really need both equations, one for each case.

Aufgabe 2 · De Morgan's laws

Let $\mathcal A$ be a collection of subsets of a set $X$ (complements are taken in $X$). Show $$\Big(\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal A}A\Big)^c=\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal A}A^c\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Big(\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal A}A\Big)^c=\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal A}A^c.$$

The idea

“Not in any of them” is the same as “outside every one of them”. And “not in all of them” is the same as “outside at least one”. The proof just writes these two sentences with quantifiers. Negating a quantifier flips $\exists$ into $\forall$ and back.

You need

What the symbols mean: $x\in\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal A}A\iff\exists A\in\mathcal A: x\in A$ and $x\in\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal A}A\iff\forall A\in\mathcal A: x\in A$. Negating quantifiers: $\neg\exists A\,P(A)\iff\forall A\,\neg P(A)$ and $\neg\forall A\,P(A)\iff\exists A\,\neg P(A)$ (lesson 1, negation).

Hint 1

Take any $x\in X$ and translate “$x$ is in the left side” into a sentence with a quantifier. Then do the same with the right side and compare.

Hint 2

$x\in\big(\bigcup A\big)^c$ means “not ($\exists A\in\mathcal A$ with $x\in A$)”. Push the “not” inside: what happens to $\exists$?

Hint 3

The whole proof is one chain of $\iff$: complement, then “there is”, then negate to “for all … not”, then “for all … in the complement”, then the intersection. The second law is the same chain with $\forall$ and $\exists$ swapped.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

To show two sets are equal, show an arbitrary element is in one exactly when it is in the other. A chain of $\iff$ does both directions at once.

Let $x\in X$.

Translate the union into “there is”, negate, and the “there is” becomes “for all … not”.

$$\begin{aligned}x\in\Big(\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal A}A\Big)^c&\iff \neg\big(\exists A\in\mathcal A:\ x\in A\big)\\&\iff \forall A\in\mathcal A:\ x\notin A\\&\iff \forall A\in\mathcal A:\ x\in A^c\\&\iff x\in\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal A}A^c.\end{aligned}$$

Same element, same membership: the sets are equal.

Hence $\big(\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal A}A\big)^c=\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal A}A^c$.

Second law: the intersection is a “for all”; negating gives “there is … not”.

$$\begin{aligned}x\in\Big(\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal A}A\Big)^c&\iff \neg\big(\forall A\in\mathcal A:\ x\in A\big)\\&\iff \exists A\in\mathcal A:\ x\notin A\\&\iff x\in\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal A}A^c,\end{aligned}$$ so $\big(\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal A}A\big)^c=\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal A}A^c$. ∎

Note. Nothing here uses that $\mathcal A$ is finite. (For $\mathcal A=\emptyset$, with the conventions $\bigcup_{\emptyset}=\emptyset$ and $\bigcap_{\emptyset}=X$, both laws still hold: they read $X=X$ and $\emptyset=\emptyset$.)

Trap

Proving it for two sets and then doing “induction on the number of sets”. The collection $\mathcal A$ can be infinite (even uncountable), and induction only reaches finite collections. The quantifier proof works for any $\mathcal A$.

Aufgabe 3 · Compositions

Let $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to Z$. Show:

  1. If $g\circ f$ is surjective, then $g$ is surjective. Convince yourself that $f$ need not be.
  2. If $g\circ f$ is surjective and $g$ is injective, then $f$ is surjective.
  3. If $g\circ f$ is injective, then $f$ is injective. Convince yourself that $g$ need not be.
  4. If $g\circ f$ is injective and $f$ is surjective, then $g$ is injective.
The idea

Think of $g\circ f$ as a relay: $f$ runs first, $g$ runs last. If the relay reaches every point of $Z$, the last runner $g$ must reach every point of $Z$. If the relay never sends two starting points to the same place, the first runner $f$ can't have merged them. Parts 2 and 4 say: with one extra assumption, the other runner inherits the property too.

You need

The two templates from lesson 2, compositions.

  • Surjective: “Let $z\in Z$. … So there is $y$ with $g(y)=z$.”
  • Injective: “Let $x_1,x_2$ with $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$. … So $x_1=x_2$.”

Memory aid: the last map inherits surjectivity, the first map inherits injectivity.

Hint 1

Write the first line of each template for the map you want (for 1 and 4 that is $g$, for 2 and 3 it is $f$). Then ask: which assumption can I use on what I have?

Hint 2

1: Surjectivity of $g\circ f$ gives an $x$ with $g(f(x))=z$. Which point of $Y$ is hiding in there? 3: Apply $g$ to both sides of $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$. 2: For $y\in Y$, look at $g(y)$ and use surjectivity of $g\circ f$. 4: Use surjectivity of $f$ to write $y_1=f(x_1)$, $y_2=f(x_2)$.

Hint 3

For the counterexamples in 1 and 3, one example does both: $X=\{0\}$, $Y=\{0,1\}$, $Z=\{0\}$, $f(0)=0$, $g(0)=g(1)=0$. Check that $g\circ f$ is bijective, $f$ is not surjective, and $g$ is not injective.

Solution

1.

Template for “$g$ is surjective”: take any target point.

Let $z\in Z$.

The relay reaches $z$; the point where $g$ takes over is $f(x)$.

Since $g\circ f$ is surjective, there is $x\in X$ with $g(f(x))=z$. Then $y:=f(x)\in Y$ satisfies $g(y)=z$. So $g$ is surjective.

$f$ need not be surjective. Take $X=\{0\}$, $Y=\{0,1\}$, $Z=\{0\}$, $f(0)=0$, $g(0)=g(1)=0$. Then $g\circ f=\mathrm{id}_{\{0\}}$ is surjective, but $1\in Y$ has no preimage under $f$.

One picture, two counterexamples: $f$ misses $1$ (not surjective), and $g$ merges $0$ and $1$ (not injective).

2.

We want a preimage of $y$ under $f$. The relay can reach $g(y)$; injectivity of $g$ then says the relay's middle point must be $y$ itself.

Let $y\in Y$. Since $g\circ f$ is surjective, there is $x\in X$ with $g(f(x))=g(y)$. Since $g$ is injective, $f(x)=y$. So $f$ is surjective.

3.

If $f$ merged two points, $g$ would get them already merged, and the relay would merge them too.

Let $x_1,x_2\in X$ with $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$. Applying $g$ gives $(g\circ f)(x_1)=(g\circ f)(x_2)$, and since $g\circ f$ is injective, $x_1=x_2$. So $f$ is injective.

$g$ need not be injective: in the example from part 1, $g\circ f$ is injective (a map on a one-point set), but $g(0)=g(1)$ with $0\ne1$.

4.

Because $f$ is surjective, every point of $Y$ is “$f$ of something”, so we can pull the question back to $X$, where injectivity of $g\circ f$ works.

Let $y_1,y_2\in Y$ with $g(y_1)=g(y_2)$. Since $f$ is surjective, there are $x_1,x_2\in X$ with $f(x_1)=y_1$ and $f(x_2)=y_2$. Then $(g\circ f)(x_1)=g(y_1)=g(y_2)=(g\circ f)(x_2)$, so $x_1=x_2$ by injectivity of $g\circ f$, and $y_1=f(x_1)=f(x_2)=y_2$. So $g$ is injective. ∎

Trap

In part 2, starting from “let $y\in Y$, there is $x$ with $g(f(x))=y$”. That is wrong: $g\circ f$ lands in $Z$, not in $Y$. You have to feed it $g(y)\in Z$ first.

Aufgabe 4 · An equivalence relation

On $E=\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$ define $$(x_1,y_1)\sim(x_2,y_2)\ :\iff\ \exists\lambda>0:\ (x_2,y_2)=(\lambda x_1,\lambda y_1).$$

  1. Show that $\sim$ is an equivalence relation.
  2. Describe the equivalence classes geometrically.
The idea

Two points are related when one is a stretched or shrunk copy of the other, without flipping direction. So “related” means “pointing in the same direction from the origin”. For each of the three properties you only have to say which stretch factor $\lambda$ works.

You need

The three properties reflexive, symmetric, transitive (lesson 3), and the equivalence class $[u]=\{v\in E: u\sim v\}$.

Hint 1

Write $u=(x_1,y_1)$, $v=(x_2,y_2)$, so $u\sim v$ means $v=\lambda u$ for some $\lambda>0$. For each property, name the $\lambda$.

Hint 2

Reflexive: which $\lambda$ gives $u=\lambda u$? Symmetric: if $v=\lambda u$, solve for $u$; is the new factor still positive? Transitive: if $v=\lambda u$ and $w=\mu v$, write $w$ in terms of $u$.

Hint 3

For part 2: fix $(x,y)$ and let $\lambda$ run through all positive numbers. What shape do the points $(\lambda x,\lambda y)$ trace? Does it include the origin? The other side of the origin?

Solution

1. Write $u\sim v\iff v=\lambda u$ for some $\lambda>0$.

Why this stepWhat you write

Stretching by $1$ changes nothing.

Reflexive: $u=1\cdot u$ and $1>0$, so $u\sim u$.

Undo the stretch by shrinking with $1/\lambda$, which is still positive.

Symmetric: if $v=\lambda u$ with $\lambda>0$, then $u=\lambda^{-1}v$ with $\lambda^{-1}>0$, so $v\sim u$.

Two stretches in a row are one stretch by the product, and a product of positive numbers is positive.

Transitive: if $v=\lambda u$ and $w=\mu v$ with $\lambda,\mu>0$, then $w=(\mu\lambda)u$ with $\mu\lambda>0$, so $u\sim w$.

Hence $\sim$ is an equivalence relation.

2. $[(x,y)]=\{(\lambda x,\lambda y):\lambda>0\}$ is the open ray from the origin through $(x,y)$, without the origin. It is not a whole line, because only positive factors are allowed. Each class meets the unit circle in exactly one point, $\frac{(x,y)}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}$, so the classes correspond to the points of the circle (the directions). ∎

Each ray is one class; the origin (open dot) is excluded.

Trap

Saying the classes are lines through the origin. With $\lambda>0$ only, $(1,0)$ and $(-1,0)$ are not related, so each line splits into two rays. (If $\lambda\ne0$ of any sign were allowed, the classes would be lines.)

Aufgabe 5 · Image and preimage

Let $f:M\to N$, $A,A'\subseteq M$, $B,B'\subseteq N$. Prove or disprove (with a counterexample):

  1. $f^{-1}(B\cup B')=f^{-1}(B)\cup f^{-1}(B')$
  2. $f^{-1}(B\cap B')=f^{-1}(B)\cap f^{-1}(B')$
  3. $f(A\cup A')=f(A)\cup f(A')$
  4. $f(A\cap A')=f(A)\cap f(A')$
  5. $f^{-1}(B^c)=\big(f^{-1}(B)\big)^c$
  6. $f(A^c)=\big(f(A)\big)^c$
The idea

The preimage asks one yes/no question about each point: “does $f(x)$ land in $B$?”. Yes/no questions combine perfectly with “or”, “and” and “not”, so the preimage respects $\cup$, $\cap$ and complements. The image asks “is there some $x$ that lands here?”. “Some” works with “or”, but two different $x$'s can land on the same point, so “and” and “not” break. Expect: 1, 2, 3, 5 true; 4 and 6 false.

You need

Definitions: $x\in f^{-1}(B)\iff f(x)\in B$, and $y\in f(A)\iff\exists x\in A: f(x)=y$. The rule from lesson 2. Note that $f^{-1}(B)$ is the preimage; $f$ need not have an inverse.

Hint 1

Decide true or false first, using the idea above. For the true ones, a chain of “$\iff$” for an arbitrary element is the whole proof.

Hint 2

For the false ones you need a map that sends two different points to the same place. The smallest one: $M=\{1,2\}$, $N=\{0\}$, $f(1)=f(2)=0$.

Hint 3

With that $f$, try $A=\{1\}$, $A'=\{2\}$ for 4: compute $A\cap A'$ first. For 6 take $A=\{1\}$ and compute both $f(A^c)$ and $N\setminus f(A)$.

Solution

1. True. For $x\in M$: $$\begin{aligned}x\in f^{-1}(B\cup B')&\iff f(x)\in B\cup B'\\&\iff f(x)\in B\ \text{or}\ f(x)\in B'\\&\iff x\in f^{-1}(B)\cup f^{-1}(B').\end{aligned}$$

2. True. For $x\in M$: $$\begin{aligned}x\in f^{-1}(B\cap B')&\iff f(x)\in B\ \text{and}\ f(x)\in B'\\&\iff x\in f^{-1}(B)\cap f^{-1}(B').\end{aligned}$$

3. True. If $y\in f(A\cup A')$, then $y=f(x)$ with $x\in A$ or $x\in A'$, so $y\in f(A)\cup f(A')$. Conversely, an element of $f(A)\cup f(A')$ has a preimage in $A$ or in $A'$, hence in $A\cup A'$.

4. False in general. $M=\{1,2\}$, $N=\{0\}$, $f(1)=f(2)=0$, $A=\{1\}$, $A'=\{2\}$. Then $f(A\cap A')=f(\emptyset)=\emptyset$, but $f(A)\cap f(A')=\{0\}\cap\{0\}=\{0\}$.

$A$ and $A'$ share no point, but their images share $0$.

What is always true: $f(A\cap A')\subseteq f(A)\cap f(A')$, because if $y=f(x)$ with $x\in A\cap A'$, then $y$ is in both images. The reverse fails exactly because a common image point can come from different points in $A$ and $A'$.

5. True. For $x\in M$: $$\begin{aligned}x\in f^{-1}(N\setminus B)&\iff f(x)\notin B\\&\iff x\notin f^{-1}(B)\\&\iff x\in M\setminus f^{-1}(B).\end{aligned}$$

6. False in general. Same $f$, $A=\{1\}$: $A^c=\{2\}$, so $f(A^c)=\{0\}$, but $(f(A))^c=N\setminus\{0\}=\emptyset$. ∎

Trap

Writing “$f^{-1}$” as if it were an inverse function and “applying it” to an equation. Here $f^{-1}(B)$ is just the set of points that land in $B$; $f$ need not be invertible. Also: in part 5 the complement on the left is taken in $N$, on the right in $M$.

Aufgabe 6 · Induction

  1. Show that $1^3+2^3+\dots+n^3=\Big(\dfrac{n(n+1)}2\Big)^2$ for all $n\ge1$.
  2. Show that for all $n\ge1$ $$\begin{aligned}&\frac1{n+1}+\dots+\frac1{2n}\\&\quad=1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\dots+\frac1{2n-1}-\frac1{2n}.\end{aligned}$$
  3. Let $n\ge2$ and $0<x_k<1$ for $k=1,\dots,n$. Show that $$(1-x_1)\cdots(1-x_n)>1-(x_1+\dots+x_n).$$
The idea

All three are “dominoes”: check the first case, then show that each case pushes over the next. The skill in every step is the same: write the $(n+1)$ case as the $n$ case plus what is new, use the hypothesis on the $n$ case, and tidy up what is new.

You need

Induction with base case, induction hypothesis, step (lesson 4, the three weapons). For 3: multiplying an inequality by a positive number keeps its direction.

Hint 1

1: $\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}k^3=\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^3+(n+1)^3$. Replace the first sum using the hypothesis. 2: Call the left side $L_n$ and the right side $R_n$ and work out how each changes from $n$ to $n+1$. 3: The base case is $n=2$, not $n=1$.

Hint 2

1: After using the hypothesis, factor out $\frac{(n+1)^2}{4}$; what is left is a perfect square. 2: Going from $n$ to $n+1$, the left side loses $\frac1{n+1}$ (the bottom moves up) and gains $\frac1{2n+1}+\frac1{2n+2}$; the right side gains $\frac1{2n+1}-\frac1{2n+2}$. 3: Multiply the hypothesis by $(1-x_{n+1})$.

Hint 3

2: Use $\frac1{n+1}=\frac2{2n+2}$, so $-\frac1{n+1}+\frac1{2n+2}=-\frac1{2n+2}$, and the two changes are equal. 3: After multiplying, expand $(1-S_n)(1-x_{n+1})$ with $S_n=x_1+\dots+x_n$; one term is positive and can be dropped.

Solution

1.

Why this stepWhat you write

First domino.

Base case $n=1$: $1^3=1=\big(\frac{1\cdot2}2\big)^2$.

Split off the new term, use the hypothesis, factor out $\frac{(n+1)^2}4$, and recognise $(n+2)^2$.

Step: assume the formula for some $n\ge1$. Then $$\begin{aligned}\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}k^3&=\Big(\frac{n(n+1)}2\Big)^2+(n+1)^3=\frac{(n+1)^2}4\big(n^2+4(n+1)\big)\\&=\frac{(n+1)^2(n+2)^2}4=\Big(\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}2\Big)^2,\end{aligned}$$ which is the formula for $n+1$. By induction it holds for all $n\ge1$.

2.

Give both sides names; it keeps the bookkeeping clean.

Let $L_n=\sum_{k=n+1}^{2n}\frac1k$ and $R_n=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\frac{(-1)^{i+1}}i$.

First domino.

Base case: $L_1=\frac12=1-\frac12=R_1$.

The left sum loses its first term and gains two at the end. Rewriting $\frac1{n+1}$ as $\frac2{2n+2}$ makes the change match the right side's change.

Step: assume $L_n=R_n$. Then $$L_{n+1}=\sum_{k=n+2}^{2n+2}\frac1k=L_n-\frac1{n+1}+\frac1{2n+1}+\frac1{2n+2}=L_n+\frac1{2n+1}-\frac1{2n+2},$$ using $\frac1{n+1}=\frac2{2n+2}$. Also $R_{n+1}=R_n+\frac1{2n+1}-\frac1{2n+2}$. So $L_{n+1}=R_{n+1}$, and by induction $L_n=R_n$ for all $n\ge1$.

Another way (from the official solution): a direct proof

Split $R_n$ into all terms minus twice the even ones: $$R_n=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\frac1i-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac1{2i}=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\frac1i-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac1i=\sum_{k=n+1}^{2n}\frac1k=L_n.$$ The alternating signs subtract each even term once; subtracting it twice more from the full sum is the same thing.

3.

Short names for the product and the sum.

Write $P_n=\prod_{k=1}^n(1-x_k)$ and $S_n=\sum_{k=1}^nx_k$.

First domino: expand, and the extra term $x_1x_2$ is positive.

Base case $n=2$: $(1-x_1)(1-x_2)=1-x_1-x_2+x_1x_2>1-(x_1+x_2)$, since $x_1x_2>0$.

Multiply the hypothesis by the new factor. This keeps the direction only because the factor is positive, so say it.

Step: assume $P_n>1-S_n$ for some $n\ge2$, and let $0<x_{n+1}<1$. Since $1-x_{n+1}>0$, $$P_{n+1}=P_n(1-x_{n+1})>(1-S_n)(1-x_{n+1}).$$

Expand and drop the positive term $S_nx_{n+1}$; dropping something positive makes the right side smaller, so “$>$” survives.

$$(1-S_n)(1-x_{n+1})=1-S_n-x_{n+1}+S_nx_{n+1}>1-S_{n+1},$$ because $S_nx_{n+1}>0$. Hence $P_{n+1}>1-S_{n+1}$, and by induction the inequality holds for all $n\ge2$. ∎

Trap

In 3, multiplying the hypothesis by $(1-x_{n+1})$ without saying that it is positive; in the exam that costs points. In 2, forgetting that the left sum also loses its first term $\frac1{n+1}$ when $n$ grows: both ends of the sum move.
