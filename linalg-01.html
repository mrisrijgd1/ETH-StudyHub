StudyHub · Linear Algebra I · Lesson 1

The guilds of Numeria: groups, residue classes, rings, fields

One set, one or two operations, and a short list of rules. By the end you can check in five lines whether something is a group, a ring or a field, and you can compute in $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$. That is what Serie 0 and Serie 1 ask over and over.

🌧
Bad day? Read the story in section 1 and answer the recap questions at the top. That counts. Tomorrow, simply carry on from here: a missed or light day is not a restart.
Covers
Script EN 2023, §1.1.2 “remainder classes modulo $n$” and §1.1.3 “Subgroups” (pp. 8–12: Definition 1.1.11, Definition 1.1.12, Lemma 1.1.13, Theorem 1.1.14, the definition of a group, Definition 1.1.16, Example 1.1.17), and §1.3 “Rings and Fields” (pp. 16–18: Definition 1.3.1, Example 1.3.3, Definition 1.3.7, Definition 1.3.9).
Time
About 70 minutes, with paper for the exercises.
You're done when
you can (1) state the group, ring and field axioms as in the script, (2) check them for a given structure with the group checklist, (3) prove that the identity and inverses are unique and that addition in $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ is well defined, and (4) decide quickly whether a structure is a ring or a field (Serie 0, Serie 1, exam multiple choice).
Script
Main: Script EN 2023 (pages as above). Lecture: Halbeisen's notes W1b, pages 1–3 (§0), which reach the same definitions in a different order; see the side notes.
Why it matters for the exam

The first three Serien are entirely “show that … is an abelian group / a ring / a field”, and the old LinAlg I exams ask it as multiple choice: “Which property does not hold in every group?” (FS19), “For which operation is $(\mathbb R,*,0)$ a group?” (HS18), “Is $M_{2\times2}(\mathbb R)$ a field?” (FS18). All three are exercises below. Computing in $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ comes back in every lesson until vector spaces.

The story: the guilds of Numeria

In the city of Numeria, every guild is a set of members plus a ritual that combines two members into a third. The Group guild has four rules: the ritual never leads outside the guild, it doesn't care about brackets, there is a quiet member who changes nobody, and every member can be undone by a partner. The great houses, Ring and Field, run two rituals at once and force them to cooperate. And on the city clock, the hours wrap around: that is the guild $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$. The city clerk (you) checks each rule with a proof.

1. Groups

A binary operation on a set $G$ is a map $\circ:G\times G\to G$, $(a,b)\mapsto a\circ b$. It takes two elements of $G$ and gives an element of $G$ again.

Definition (group, Script EN 2023, §1.1.3, p. 11). Let $G$ be a set and $\circ:G\times G\to G$. Then $(G,\circ)$ is a group if:

  1. Closure: for all $a,b\in G$, $a\circ b\in G$.
  2. Associativity: for all $a,b,c\in G$, $(a\circ b)\circ c=a\circ(b\circ c)$.
  3. Identity: there is $e\in G$ (the identity element) with $a\circ e=e\circ a=a$ for all $a\in G$.
  4. Inverse: for every $a\in G$ there is $a^{-1}\in G$ (the inverse of $a$) with $a\circ a^{-1}=a^{-1}\circ a=e$.

If moreover $a\circ b=b\circ a$ for all $a,b$, the group is abelian. Abelian groups are usually written additively: $+$, identity $0$, inverse $-a$ (Note 1.1.21, p. 12).

Examples. $(\mathbb Z,+)$; $(\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ with $a^{-1}=\frac1a$. Non-examples. $(\mathbb N,+)$: $1$ has no inverse. $(\mathbb Z,\cdot)$: $2\cdot x=1$ has no solution in $\mathbb Z$.

Closure is the axiom people forget. If $\circ$ is really a map $G\times G\to G$, closure is automatic. But in an exercise you are often handed a formula, and the first thing to check is that it lands in $G$. Example: multiplication on $\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}$ gives $(-1)\cdot(-1)=1$, which is outside.

In the lecture. Halbeisen climbs to groups in steps (W1b, pp. 1–2): a semigroup (associative operation), a monoid (semigroup with a neutral element), then an abelian group (commutative monoid in which every element has exactly one inverse). Same end point. The lecture's words are in the glossary.

The script says “the identity” and “the inverse”. Both are allowed because they are unique, and these two proofs are the first proofs of the course.

Proof A: a left and a right neutral element coincide

Claim. If $e_l$ is left neutral and $e_r$ is right neutral for an operation $\circ$ on a set $M$, then $e_l=e_r$. In particular, there is at most one neutral element. (Script EN 2023 says “the” identity element in the group definition, p. 10; this is why that is allowed.)

The idea. Combine the two candidates with each other, $e_l\circ e_r$, and read it in two ways: once using that $e_l$ changes nothing from the left, once using that $e_r$ changes nothing from the right.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Given: $e_l\circ x=x$ for every $x$, and $x\circ e_r=x$ for every $x$.
  • To show: $e_l=e_r$, an equation between two specific elements.
  • The gap: choose the $x$ in each “for every” cleverly.
The storyWhat you write

Two members both claim to be the quiet one. The clerk makes them perform the ritual with each other.

Consider $e_l\circ e_r\in M$.

The left one is quiet, so the result is simply the right one.

Since $e_l$ is left neutral, with $x=e_r$: $e_l\circ e_r=e_r$.

The right one is quiet too, so the same result is the left one. So they were the same member all along.

Since $e_r$ is right neutral, with $x=e_l$: $e_l\circ e_r=e_l$. Hence $e_l=e_l\circ e_r=e_r$. ∎

Move used: compute one element ($e_l\circ e_r$) in two ways.

The proof in one piece

Let $e_l$ be left neutral and $e_r$ right neutral. Then $e_l=e_l\circ e_r$ because $e_r$ is right neutral, and $e_l\circ e_r=e_r$ because $e_l$ is left neutral. So $e_l=e_r$. If $e,e'$ are both neutral, apply this with $e_l=e$, $e_r=e'$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1. Fill in: “$e_l\circ e_r=\_\_\_$ because $e_l$ is left neutral.”

    Answer

    $e_r$.

  2. Rung 2. Where did we use associativity?

    Answer

    Nowhere. The claim holds for any binary operation, associative or not.

  3. Rung 3. Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Proof B: inverses are unique

Claim. In a group $(G,\circ)$ with identity $e$, an element $x$ has at most one inverse. (The script uses this “uniqueness of the inverse” in Lemma 1.1.20 (b), p. 12, and Lemma 1.3.10 (d), p. 19, without proving it.)

The idea. Take two inverses $x'$ and $x''$. Start from $x'$, sneak in $e=x\circ x''$, rebracket, and the $x'\circ x$ collapses to $e$, leaving $x''$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $x'$ and $x''$ be inverses of $x$.” So four equations: $x'\circ x=e=x\circ x'$ and $x''\circ x=e=x\circ x''$.
  • Last line: “Hence $x'=x''$.”
  • The gap: a chain of equalities from $x'$ to $x''$, each justified by one axiom.
The storyWhat you write

Two partners both claim to undo the same member. The clerk starts with the first one…

$x'=x'\circ e$ ($e$ is neutral)

…and secretly replaces the quiet member by “member followed by the second partner”.

$\phantom{x'}=x'\circ(x\circ x'')$ ($x''$ is an inverse of $x$)

Brackets don't matter in a guild, so the first partner meets the member first, and they cancel.

$\phantom{x'}=(x'\circ x)\circ x''$ (associativity) $=e\circ x''$ ($x'$ is an inverse of $x$) $=x''$ ($e$ is neutral). ∎

Moves used: insert a middle point (here: insert $e=x\circ x''$)

The proof in one piece

Let $x',x''$ be inverses of $x$. Then $x'=x'\circ e=x'\circ(x\circ x'')=(x'\circ x)\circ x''=e\circ x''=x''$, using in turn: $e$ is neutral, $x''$ is a right inverse, associativity, $x'$ is a left inverse, $e$ is neutral. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1. Which two properties of $x'$ and $x''$ did the proof actually use?

    Answer

    Only that $x'$ is a left inverse and $x''$ a right inverse. So as soon as $\circ$ is associative with a neutral element, a left inverse and a right inverse of the same $x$ are always equal.

  2. Rung 2. Write the chain of equalities and name the reason for each step.

  3. Rung 3. Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Name both inverses and write down the four equations.
  2. Start with $x'=x'\circ e$.
  3. Replace $e$ by $x\circ x''$.
  4. Rebracket, then cancel $x'\circ x=e$.

In short: “sneak in $e$, rebracket, collapse.”

The group checklist (use it for every “show that … is an abelian group”)
  1. Closure: $x\circ y\in M$ for all $x,y\in M$.
  2. Associativity.
  3. Neutral element: name $e$ and check $e\circ x=x$ (and $x\circ e=x$).
  4. Inverses: for each $x$ name $x'$ and check $x'\circ x=e$.
  5. Commutativity (for abelian).

In short: close, bracket, neutral, undo, swap.

2. Residue classes: the clock of Numeria

The script calls this example “central to us” (p. 8): it is the first group, the first ring and, for prime $n$, the first finite field. Fix $n\in\mathbb N$, $n\ge1$. For $a,b\in\mathbb Z$ write $a\equiv b\pmod n$ if $n$ divides $b-a$, that is $b-a=ln$ for some $l\in\mathbb Z$. This is an equivalence relation (Exercise 1.1.7, p. 9).

Definition (residue classes, Definition 1.1.11, p. 10). The class of $a\in\mathbb Z$ is $\bar a=a+n\mathbb Z=\{a+nl:l\in\mathbb Z\}$, all integers with the same remainder as $a$ when dividing by $n$. The set of classes is $$\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z=\{\bar0,\bar1,\dots,\overline{n-1}\}.$$

Definition (modular addition, Definition 1.1.12, p. 10). For $\bar a,\bar b\in\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ let $\bar a+\bar b:=\overline{a+b}$.

Example 1.1.10 ($n=3$). $\bar1=\bar4=\overline{-2}=\overline{10}$ and $\bar2+\bar2=\bar4=\bar1$. Each class has many names. For $n=2$: $\bar0$ = even numbers, $\bar1$ = odd numbers, and “odd + odd = even” is $\bar1+\bar1=\bar0$.

The definition picks a name $a$ for the class $\bar a$ and computes with it. If a different name gave a different answer, the definition would be meaningless. So it needs a proof.

Proof C: addition in $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ is well defined (Lemma 1.1.13, p. 10)

Claim. If $\bar a=\overline{a'}$ and $\bar b=\overline{b'}$, then $\overline{a+b}=\overline{a'+b'}$.

The idea. “Same class” means “difference is a multiple of $n$”. Subtract the two sums and regroup: the difference is a sum of two multiples of $n$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Given: $a-a'=ln$ and $b-b'=kn$ for some $l,k\in\mathbb Z$.
  • To show: $(a+b)-(a'+b')$ is a multiple of $n$.
  • The gap: rewrite $(a+b)-(a'+b')$ using the two given differences.
The storyWhat you write

Two travellers name the same hour differently, and so do two others. The clerk writes down what “same hour” means.

Let $\bar a=\overline{a'}$, $\bar b=\overline{b'}$. By definition there are $l,k\in\mathbb Z$ with $a-a'=ln$ and $b-b'=kn$.

Then the clerk compares the two sums by subtracting them.

$(a+b)-(a'+b')=(a-a')+(b-b')=ln+kn=(l+k)n$.

The difference is a whole number of turns of the clock, so both sums name the same hour.

Since $l+k\in\mathbb Z$, $\overline{a+b}=\overline{a'+b'}$. The addition does not depend on the choice of representatives. ∎

Move used: unfold the definition (“same class” = “difference is $ln$”), then regroup.

The proof in one piece

Let $a,a',b,b'\in\mathbb Z$ with $\bar a=\overline{a'}$ and $\bar b=\overline{b'}$. Then there are $l,k\in\mathbb Z$ with $a-a'=ln$ and $b-b'=kn$. It follows that $(a+b)-(a'+b')=(a-a')+(b-b')=(l+k)n$, so $\overline{a+b}=\overline{a'+b'}$. Hence the addition is well defined. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1. Fill in: “$\bar a=\overline{a'}$ means there is $l\in\mathbb Z$ with ___.”

    Answer

    $a-a'=ln$.

  2. Rung 2. Do the same for multiplication: show $\overline{ab}=\overline{a'b'}$ (Example 1.3.3, p. 16). Hint: $ab-a'b'=(a-a')b+a'(b-b')$.

    Answer

    $ab-a'b'=(a-a')b+a'(b-b')=lnb+a'kn=(lb+a'k)n$, so $\overline{ab}=\overline{a'b'}$.

  3. Rung 3. Write the proof of Proof C from the frame alone.

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Take two names for each class and write the two “difference $=$ multiple of $n$” equations.
  2. Subtract the two results.
  3. Regroup into the given differences; factor out $n$.

In short: “two names, subtract, factor $n$.” Every “is it well defined?” question works like this.

Theorem 1.1.14 (p. 10). $(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z,+)$ is an abelian group: associativity and commutativity come from $\mathbb Z$ ($(\bar a+\bar b)+\bar c=\overline{a+b+c}=\bar a+(\bar b+\bar c)$), the identity is $\bar0$, and the inverse of $\bar a$ is $\overline{-a}$.

3. Subgroups

Definition (subgroup, Definition 1.1.16, p. 11). Let $(G,\circ)$ be a group. A subset $H\subseteq G$ is a subgroup if $H\ne\emptyset$, $h_1\circ h_2\in H$ for all $h_1,h_2\in H$, and $h^{-1}\in H$ for all $h\in H$.

Examples 1.1.17. $(\mathbb Z,+)$ in $(\mathbb Q,+)$; $m\mathbb Z=\{ml:l\in\mathbb Z\}$ in $\mathbb Z$; $\{e\}$ and $G$ in any $G$; $\{\bar0,\bar2\}$ in $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$.

A subgroup is a group with the same operation (Note 1.1.18, p. 12): associativity is inherited, and $e=h\circ h^{-1}\in H$ for any $h\in H$. This “three checks, the rest is inherited” pattern comes back for subspaces in lesson 5.

4. Rings: two operations that cooperate

Definition (ring, Definition 1.3.1, p. 16). A set $R$ with two operations $+:R\times R\to R$ and $\cdot:R\times R\to R$ is a ring if

  • (R1) $(R,+)$ is an abelian group;
  • (R2) $\cdot$ is associative;
  • (R3) for all $a,b,c\in R$: $a\cdot(b+c)=a\cdot b+a\cdot c$ and $(a+b)\cdot c=a\cdot c+b\cdot c$ (distributive laws).

$R$ is commutative if $a\cdot b=b\cdot a$ for all $a,b$, and a ring with one if there is $1\in R$ with $1\cdot a=a\cdot1=a$ for all $a$.

Examples. $(\mathbb Z,+,\cdot)$ is a commutative ring with one (Example 1.3.2). $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ with $\bar a\cdot\bar b:=\overline{ab}$ is a finite commutative ring with one $\bar1$ (Example 1.3.3, p. 16; well defined by Rung 2 of Proof C). $(2\mathbb Z,+,\cdot)$ is a commutative ring without one.

$\cdot$ in $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$$\bar0$$\bar1$$\bar2$$\bar3$
$\bar1$$\bar0$$\bar1$$\bar2$$\bar3$
$\bar2$$\bar0$$\bar2$$\bar0$$\bar2$
$\bar3$$\bar0$$\bar3$$\bar2$$\bar1$

Look at $\bar2\cdot\bar2=\bar0$: two nonzero elements whose product is zero. That is why $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$ will not be a field (lesson 3 proves exactly when $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ is one).

In the lecture. Halbeisen's ring (Def. 6, p. 2) always has a $1$: $(R,\cdot)$ must be a monoid. So in the lecture $(2\mathbb Z,+,\cdot)$ is not a ring. If an exam question uses “ring”, check which convention it means; for fields both agree.

5. Fields: everything except zero can be divided by

Definition (field, Definition 1.3.7, p. 18). A set $K$ with two operations $+$ and $\cdot$ is a field if

  1. $(K,+)$ is an abelian group (identity $0$, inverse $-a$);
  2. $K^\times:=K\setminus\{0\}$ with $\cdot$ is an abelian group (identity $1$, inverse $a^{-1}=\frac1a$);
  3. the distributive laws hold.

In other words: a commutative ring with one in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse.

The script also gives the same definition as one flat list (Definition 1.3.9, p. 18), handy as a checklist: associativity, commutativity, $0$ and $-x$ for $+$; associativity, $1$ and $x^{-1}$ for $x\ne0$ for $\cdot$; distributivity; $1\ne0$; commutativity of $\cdot$. Ten axioms in all.

Examples. $\mathbb Q$, $\mathbb R$, $\mathbb C$ (Example 1.3.11). Non-examples. $\mathbb Z$ ($2$ has no inverse); $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$ ($\bar2$ has no inverse: the row of $\bar2$ above never shows $\bar1$).

Why remove $0$? In any ring $0\cdot a=0$ (Lemma 1.3.4, lesson 4), so $0$ can never be inverted unless $0=1$. “Everything except $0$ is invertible” is the most one can ask. And “$K^\times$ is a group” hides two more facts: $1\ne0$ (the group $K^\times$ contains its identity $1$, but not $0$), and a product of nonzero elements is nonzero (closure of $K^\times$). That is Lemma 1.3.10 (a), (c), lesson 4.

In the lecture. Halbeisen's Def. 7 (p. 3) says the same as Definition 1.3.7: a commutative ring $(M,\circ,*)$ such that $M$ without the neutral element of $\circ$ is an abelian group under $*$.

Why it's cool. Vector spaces (lesson 5) are built over a field $K$. Everything you'll prove there works at once for $\mathbb Q$, $\mathbb R$, $\mathbb C$ and the finite fields $\mathbb F_p$ and $\mathbb F_4$, where $1+1=0$. One list of axioms, many worlds.

For wonder (optional, 5 min): a field with four elements

Serie 1, Aufgabe 9 builds a field $K=\{0,1,a,b\}$. Addition: every element is its own inverse ($x+x=0$) and $1+a=b$. Multiplication: $a\cdot a=b$, $a\cdot b=1$, $b\cdot b=a$. So $(K\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ “is” $\mathbb Z_3$ (with $1,a,b$ playing $\bar0,\bar1,\bar2$), and $(K,+)$ “is” $(\mathcal P(\{1,2\}),\triangle)$. It is not $\mathbb Z_4$: there $\bar2\cdot\bar2=\bar0$, which a field forbids.

Worked examples

Try each step in your head before opening the answer.

Worked example 1: $(\mathbb Q_{>0},\cdot)$ is an abelian group (Serie 0, Aufgabe 1(a))

  1. Closure: why is the product of two positive rationals a positive rational?

    Answer

    $\frac mn\cdot\frac kl=\frac{mk}{nl}$ with $mk\in\mathbb Z$, $nl\in\mathbb N_{>0}$, and the product of two positive numbers is positive.

  2. Associativity and commutativity: do you need to prove them from scratch?

    Answer

    No: they hold for $\cdot$ on all of $\mathbb Q$ (or $\mathbb R$), hence in particular on the subset $\mathbb Q_{>0}$. Say this explicitly.

  3. Neutral element and inverses?

    Answer

    $1\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$ with $1\cdot q=q$. For $q=\frac mn>0$ (so $m,n>0$), $q'=\frac nm\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$ and $q'\cdot q=1$. All five checklist items hold, so it is an abelian group.

Worked example 2: $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle)$ is an abelian group (Serie 0, Aufgabe 4)

For a nonempty set $M$ and $x,y\subseteq M$ let $x\triangle y:=(x\setminus y)\cup(y\setminus x)$ (symmetric difference).

  1. Restate $t\in x\triangle y$ in words.

    Answer

    $t$ lies in exactly one of $x$ and $y$. This sentence, call it $(*)$, does all the work below.

  2. Closure, commutativity, neutral element, inverses?

    Answer

    Closure: $x\triangle y\subseteq x\cup y\subseteq M$. Commutative: $(*)$ is symmetric in $x,y$. Neutral: $\emptyset\triangle x=x$. Inverse: $x\triangle x=\emptyset$, so every element is its own inverse.

  3. Associativity: when is $t\in(x\triangle y)\triangle z$?

    Answer

    Case $t\in z$: then $t\in(x\triangle y)\triangle z$ iff $t\notin x\triangle y$, iff $t$ is in none or both of $x,y$; with $z$ that means in exactly one or all three of $x,y,z$. Case $t\notin z$: iff $t\in x\triangle y$, iff $t$ is in exactly one of $x,y,z$. Both cases: $t$ lies in an odd number of the sets $x,y,z$. That description is symmetric, so it also describes $x\triangle(y\triangle z)$. Hence associativity.

Worked example 3: $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle,\cap)$ is a ring, but no field if $|M|>1$ (Serie 1, Aufgabe 6)

  1. What is the “1” of $\cap$?

    Answer

    $M$ itself: $M\cap x=x$ for all $x\subseteq M$. Associativity and commutativity of $\cap$ are immediate from “$t\in x$ and $t\in y$”.

  2. Distributivity $x\cap(y\triangle z)=(x\cap y)\triangle(x\cap z)$: which case split?

    Answer

    If $t\notin x$: $t$ is in neither side. If $t\in x$: $t\in x\cap y\iff t\in y$ and $t\in x\cap z\iff t\in z$, so both sides contain $t$ iff $t$ is in exactly one of $y,z$.

  3. Why no field when $M$ has two elements $p\ne q$?

    Answer

    $\{p\}\ne\emptyset$, but $\{p\}\cap y=M$ is impossible, since $\{p\}\cap y\subseteq\{p\}\ne M$ (because $q\notin\{p\}$). So $\{p\}$ has no inverse for $\cap$.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (LinAlg I exam FS19, multiple choice III)

Which property does not hold in every group $(G,\circ,e)$? (a) $a\circ b=b\circ a$ for all $a,b$. (b) $a\circ e=e\circ a=a$ for all $a$. (c) For all $a$ there is $b$ with $a\circ b=b\circ a=e$. (d) $(a\circ b)\circ c=a\circ(b\circ c)$ for all $a,b,c$.

Solution

(a). Commutativity is the extra rule that makes a group abelian; (b), (c), (d) are the group axioms themselves. A non-abelian example: the invertible $2\times2$ matrices under matrix multiplication.

Exercise 2 (LinAlg I exam HS18, multiple choice VII)

For which operation $*:\mathbb R\times\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ is $(\mathbb R,*,0)$ a group? (a) $a*b=ab^2+a^2b$ (b) $a*b=a+ab+b$ (c) $a*b=a+a^2b^2+b$ (d) $a*b=(a^3+b^3)^{1/3}$

Solution

Frame: test the cheapest axiom first: is $0$ neutral? Then inverses.

(a) $0*b=0\ne b$ for $b\ne0$: $0$ isn't neutral. (b) $0$ is neutral, but $-1$ has no inverse: $(-1)*b=-1-b+b=-1\ne0$ for every $b$. (c) $0$ is neutral, but associativity fails: $(1*1)*(-1)=3*(-1)=3+9-1=11$, while $1*(1*(-1))=1*1=3$ (since $1*(-1)=1+1-1=1$). (d) works: with $\varphi(a)=a^3$ (a bijection of $\mathbb R$), $a*b=\varphi^{-1}(\varphi(a)+\varphi(b))$, so $*$ is “addition in disguise”: associative and commutative, $0*b=b$, and the inverse of $a$ is $-a$.

Exercise 3 (Serie 0, Aufgabe 5(b), shortened)

For $m\ge1$ let $\mathbb Z_m$ (the script writes $\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z$) $=\{\bar0,\dots,\overline{m-1}\}$ with $\bar k+_m\bar l:=\bar a$, where $a$ is the remainder of $k+l$ on division by $m$. Show that $(\mathbb Z_m,+_m)$ is an abelian group. (You may use that “remainder of a sum = remainder of the sum of remainders”.)

Solution

Frame: the group checklist.

Closure: the remainder lies in $\{0,\dots,m-1\}$. Commutative: $k+l=l+k$. Associative: $(\bar k+_m\bar l)+_m\bar n$ and $\bar k+_m(\bar l+_m\bar n)$ are both the remainder of $k+l+n$ (by the given rule), so they agree. Neutral: $\bar0+_m\bar k=\bar k$ since $0+k=k$ has remainder $k$ for $0\le k<m$. Inverse: for $\bar k\ne\bar0$ take $\overline{m-k}$: $k+(m-k)=m$ has remainder $0$; and $\bar0$ is its own inverse. So $(\mathbb Z_m,+_m)$ is an abelian group.

Exercise 4 (Serie 1, Aufgabe 7(b), and exam FS18 MC iv)

(a) Show that the polynomial ring $\mathbb Q[X]$ is not a field. (b) True or false: the real $2\times2$ matrices $M_{2\times2}(\mathbb R)$ with matrix addition and multiplication form a field.

Solution

(a) $X\ne0$, but there is no polynomial $p$ with $X\cdot p=1$: the product $X\cdot p$ has no constant term (its constant coefficient is $0$), while $1$ has constant coefficient $1$. So $X$ has no inverse and $(\mathbb Q[X]\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is not a group.

(b) False. Two independent reasons: multiplication is not commutative ($\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, but the other order gives $\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$), and the nonzero matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ has no inverse.

Exercise 5 (Serie 0, Aufgabe 3, the Fibonacci sequences)

Let $S$ be the set of real sequences $(a_0,a_1,\dots)$ with termwise addition, and $\mathrm{Fib}\subseteq S$ those with $f_{i+2}=f_{i+1}+f_i$ for all $i$. Given that $(S,+_S)$ is an abelian group, show that $(\mathrm{Fib},+_S)$ is one too.

Solution

Frame: associativity and commutativity are inherited from $S$. Check closure, that the neutral element lies in $\mathrm{Fib}$, and that inverses stay in $\mathrm{Fib}$.

Closure: if $f,g\in\mathrm{Fib}$, then $(f+g)_{i+2}=f_{i+2}+g_{i+2}=(f_{i+1}+f_i)+(g_{i+1}+g_i)=(f+g)_{i+1}+(f+g)_i$. Neutral: the zero sequence satisfies $0=0+0$. Inverse: $-f=(-f_0,-f_1,\dots)$ satisfies $-f_{i+2}=-f_{i+1}-f_i$. So $\mathrm{Fib}$ is an abelian group.
