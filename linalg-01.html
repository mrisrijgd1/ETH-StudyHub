Why it matters for the exam

Halbeisen's first two Serien are nothing but “show that … is an abelian group” and “show that … is a ring but not a field”, and his exam will speak the same language. The old Linear Algebra I exams ask it as quick multiple choice: “Which property does not hold in every group?” (FS19, question III), “For which operation $\ast$ is $(\mathbb R,\ast,0)$ a group?” (HS18, question VII), “In the field $\mathbb F_{23}$, $8\cdot9$ equals …” (FS19, question IV). All three are worked below. More importantly, every later definition (vector space, subspace, linear map) is built from the vocabulary of this lesson, so ten minutes saved here are an hour saved later.

1. The story: the guilds of the Free City

The Free City has no king. It is run by its guilds, and a guild is a strange thing: a set of members together with a ritual that takes any two members and produces a third. The Weavers' ritual: two threads are twisted into a cord. The Bell-ringers' ritual: two chimes are rung one after the other. The city charter does not care what the rituals mean. It only asks which rules a ritual obeys, and gives the guild a rank accordingly.

A guild whose ritual never cares about brackets (twisting $a$ with $b$ and then with $c$ gives the same cord as twisting $a$ with the already twisted $b$-and-$c$) gets the lowest rank. A guild that also has a silent member, one who changes nobody in the ritual, rises one rank. A guild in which every member has a partner who undoes them gets a seat on the council. And the two great houses, Ring and Field, run two rituals at once and are forced by the charter to make them cooperate.

On the clock tower, the hours wrap around: after hour $11$ comes hour $0$ again. The clockmakers' guild computes with hours, and they discovered something unsettling. Some hours, multiplied together, give hour $0$, although neither of them is $0$. The council keeps them out of the house of Field. You are the new city clerk. Your job: check the charter's rules, one at a time, with a proof.

In mathematics: a guild is an algebraic structure, a set $M$ with one or more operations. The ranks are semigroup, monoid, group, and with two operations ring and field. The clock is $\mathbb Z/n$, the integers modulo $n$, and the troublesome hours are zero divisors. Linear Algebra needs all of this for one reason: a vector space (lesson 5) is an abelian group of vectors together with a field of numbers that can stretch them. So the first two lessons build the field and the group, and from lesson 5 on we put them together.

2. Operations: what the ritual must do

A binary operation on a set $M$ is a map

$$\circ:\ M\times M\to M,\qquad (a,b)\mapsto a\circ b.$$

It takes an ordered pair of elements of $M$ and returns an element of $M$ again. That last part is the trap of every first exercise. When an exercise hands you a formula, the first thing to check is that the result lands in $M$. The script lists this as the closure axiom (p. 11); Halbeisen says “$\circ$ is an operation on $M$”. Same thing.

Examples and non-examples (from the lecture)

  • $+$ on $\mathbb N_{>0}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}$: the sum of two positive integers is a positive integer. An operation.
  • $+$ on $2\mathbb N_{>0}=\{2,4,6,\dots\}$: even plus even is even. An operation.
  • $\cdot$ on $-\mathbb N=\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}$: not an operation, because $(-1)\cdot(-1)=1\notin-\mathbb N$. Halbeisen's first “Unbeispiel”.
  • $-$ on $\mathbb N$: not an operation, since $2-5=-3\notin\mathbb N$. On $\mathbb Z$ it is an operation (but a badly behaved one, as you'll see).

Two properties an operation may or may not have:

  • associative: $(a\circ b)\circ c=a\circ(b\circ c)$ for all $a,b,c\in M$. Brackets don't matter, so we may write $a\circ b\circ c$.
  • commutative: $a\circ b=b\circ a$ for all $a,b\in M$. Order doesn't matter.

Subtraction on $\mathbb Z$ is neither: $(5-3)-1=1$ but $5-(3-1)=3$, and $5-3\ne3-5$. Composition of maps (script p. 14) is associative but in general not commutative: “mirror, then shift” is not “shift, then mirror”.

In the lecture. A set with an associative operation is a semigroup (Halbeisen's Definition 1); if the operation is also commutative, a commutative semigroup. Examples: $(\mathbb N_{>0},+)$, $(2\mathbb N_{>0},+)$, $(\mathbb R,\cdot)$. The script never uses the word, but it is a handy name for the first rung.

3. Neutral elements, inverses, groups

Look at $(\mathbb N,+)$ with $0\in\mathbb N$. (Halbeisen counts $0$ as a natural number and remarks that this is not done uniformly; the script writes $\mathbb N_0=\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ when it wants the $0$.) The number $0$ is special: $x+0=x$ and $0+x=x$ for every $x$.

Definition (neutral element). Let $\circ$ be an operation on $M$ and $e\in M$.

  • $e$ is left neutral if $e\circ x=x$ for all $x\in M$;
  • $e$ is right neutral if $x\circ e=x$ for all $x\in M$;
  • $e$ is neutral (the script says identity element) if it is both.

The script's group axiom says “there exists an element $e$ … called the identity element” (p. 11), and Halbeisen asks for “exactly one” neutral element. Both are justified by the first proof of the course.

Proof A: a left neutral and a right neutral element coincide

Claim. Let $\circ$ be any operation on $M$. If $e_l$ is left neutral and $e_r$ is right neutral, then $e_l=e_r$. In particular, an operation has at most one neutral element.

The idea. We know nothing about $e_l$ and $e_r$ except how they behave next to other elements. So put them next to each other: $e_l\circ e_r$. The left one is silent, so the result is $e_r$; the right one is silent, so the result is $e_l$. One expression, two values, so the values are equal.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Given: $e_l\circ x=x$ for every $x\in M$, and $x\circ e_r=x$ for every $x\in M$.
  • To show: $e_l=e_r$, an equation between two specific elements.
  • The gap: choose the $x$ in each “for every” cleverly.
The storyWhat you write

Two members both claim to be the guild's silent member. The clerk makes them perform the ritual with each other.

Consider the element $e_l\circ e_r\in M$.

The first one is silent from the left, so what comes out is simply the second.

Since $e_l$ is left neutral, applied to $x=e_r$: $\;e_l\circ e_r=e_r$.

The second is silent from the right, so what comes out is simply the first. They were the same member all along.

Since $e_r$ is right neutral, applied to $x=e_l$: $\;e_l\circ e_r=e_l$. Hence $e_l=e_l\circ e_r=e_r$. If $e$ and $e'$ are both neutral, apply this with $e_l=e$, $e_r=e'$: so $e=e'$. ∎

Moves used: compute one element in two ways unfold the definition

The proof in one piece

Let $e_l$ be left neutral and $e_r$ right neutral for $\circ$. Since $e_r$ is right neutral, $e_l\circ e_r=e_l$. Since $e_l$ is left neutral, $e_l\circ e_r=e_r$. Hence $e_l=e_r$. In particular, if $e$ and $e'$ are neutral elements, then $e$ is left neutral and $e'$ is right neutral, so $e=e'$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Fill in: “$e_l\circ e_r=\underline{\quad}$, because $e_l$ is left neutral.”
    Answer

    $e_r$. (Left neutral means $e_l\circ x=x$; here $x=e_r$.)

  2. Rung 2. Where did the proof use associativity?
    Answer

    Nowhere. The claim holds for every operation, associative or not. Good to know: in an exam, don't cite axioms you didn't use.

  3. Rung 3. Cover everything above and write the proof from the frame alone.
    Check

    You need: the element $e_l\circ e_r$, two evaluations each with its reason, and the conclusion. Three lines.

Next $(\mathbb Z,+)$: now every $x$ has a partner $-x$ with $x+(-x)=0=(-x)+x$.

Definition (inverse). Let $\circ$ be an operation on $M$ with neutral element $e$, and $x,x'\in M$. If $x'\circ x=e$, then $x'$ is a left inverse of $x$; if $x\circ x'=e$, a right inverse; if both, an inverse of $x$.

Proof B: inverses are unique

Claim. Let $\circ$ be an associative operation on $M$ with neutral element $e$. If $x'$ is a left inverse of $x$ and $x''$ is a right inverse of $x$, then $x'=x''$. In particular, every $x$ has at most one inverse. (The script uses this “uniqueness of the inverse” in Lemma 1.1.20 and Lemma 1.3.10, p. 12 and 19, without proving it; the lecture proves it in §2.)

The idea. We want to walk from $x'$ to $x''$ using only equations. The only thing linking them is $x$: $x'\circ x=e$ and $x\circ x''=e$. So start at $x'$, sneak in a neutral element, replace it by $x\circ x''$, and let associativity bring $x'$ and $x$ together so they collapse to $e$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $x'$ be a left inverse and $x''$ a right inverse of $x$, so $x'\circ x=e$ and $x\circ x''=e$.”
  • Last line: “Hence $x'=x''$.”
  • The gap: a chain of equalities from $x'$ to $x''$, each step justified by one axiom or one given equation.
The storyWhat you write

Two partners both claim to undo the same member. The clerk starts with the first one, and adds the silent member at his side, which changes nothing.

$x'=x'\circ e\quad$ ($e$ is neutral)

But the silent member is the same as “the member followed by the second partner”.

$\phantom{x'}=x'\circ(x\circ x'')\quad$ ($x''$ is a right inverse of $x$)

Brackets don't matter in this guild, so the first partner meets the member first, and the two cancel to silence.

$\phantom{x'}=(x'\circ x)\circ x''\quad$ (associativity)
$\phantom{x'}=e\circ x''\quad$ ($x'$ is a left inverse of $x$)

Silence next to the second partner leaves the second partner. They were the same.

$\phantom{x'}=x''\quad$ ($e$ is neutral). ∎

Moves used: sneak in the neutral element

The proof in one piece

Let $x'$ be a left inverse and $x''$ a right inverse of $x$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}x'&=x'\circ e=x'\circ(x\circ x'')\\&=(x'\circ x)\circ x''=e\circ x''=x'',\end{aligned}$$

using in turn: $e$ is neutral, $x''$ is a right inverse, associativity, $x'$ is a left inverse, $e$ is neutral. If $x'$ and $x''$ are both inverses of $x$, they are in particular a left and a right inverse, so $x'=x''$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Which axiom justifies $x'\circ(x\circ x'')=(x'\circ x)\circ x''$?
    Answer

    Associativity. This is the one step where the proof needs it, and why the claim fails for non-associative operations.

  2. Rung 2. Write the chain from $x''$ to $x'$ instead (start with $x''=e\circ x''$).
    Answer
    $$\begin{aligned}x''&=e\circ x''=(x'\circ x)\circ x''\\&=x'\circ(x\circ x'')=x'\circ e=x'.\end{aligned}$$

    Same steps, read backwards.

  3. Rung 3. Write the whole proof from the frame alone, with a reason after every equals sign.
How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Name both inverses and write the two equations $x'\circ x=e$, $x\circ x''=e$.
  2. Start with $x'=x'\circ e$.
  3. Replace $e$ by $x\circ x''$.
  4. Rebracket, collapse $x'\circ x=e$, finish with $e\circ x''=x''$.

In short: sneak in $e$, rebracket, collapse.

The definition of a group, two ways

Script EN 2023, p. 11

$(G,\circ)$ with $\circ:G\times G\to G$ is a group if:

  1. closure: $a\circ b\in G$ for all $a,b\in G$;
  2. associativity: $(a\circ b)\circ c=a\circ(b\circ c)$;
  3. identity: there is $e\in G$ with $a\circ e=e\circ a=a$ for all $a$;
  4. inverse: for every $a$ there is $a^{-1}\in G$ with $a\circ a^{-1}=a^{-1}\circ a=e$.

If moreover $a\circ b=b\circ a$ for all $a,b$, the group is abelian.

Lecture (Halbeisen), Definitions 1–5
  1. semigroup: associative operation;
  2. (commutative) monoid: a (commutative) semigroup with exactly one neutral element;
  3. abelian group: a commutative monoid in which every element has exactly one inverse.

Examples: $(\mathbb N_{>0},+)$ semigroup; $(\mathbb N,+)$ and $(\mathbb Z,\cdot)$ monoids; $(\mathbb Z,+)$ and $(\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ abelian groups.

They describe exactly the same objects. The words “exactly one” in the lecture are free: Proofs A and B show that a neutral element and inverses, once they exist, are automatically unique. So in an exam you check existence and may quote uniqueness. Abelian groups are usually written additively: $+$, neutral element $0$, inverse $-a$, and $a-b$ means $a+(-b)$ (script p. 11–12).

The structure checklist (use it for every “show that … is an abelian group”)

  1. Operation: $x\circ y\in M$ for all $x,y\in M$.
  2. Associativity.
  3. Neutral element: name $e$ and check $e\circ x=x$ (and $x\circ e=x$, or quote commutativity).
  4. Inverses: for each $x$, name $x'$, check that $x'\in M$, and check $x'\circ x=e$.
  5. Commutativity (for “abelian”).

In short: close, bracket, neutral, undo, swap. To show something is not a group, one failing element is enough.

Moves: the structure checklist inherit from the bigger structure one counterexample is enough

Worked example 1: $(\mathbb Q_{>0},\cdot)$ is an abelian group (Serie 0, exercise 1 (a))

Here $\mathbb Q_{>0}=\{q\in\mathbb Q:q>0\}$. Try each step in your head before opening the answer.

  1. Operation: why is the product of two elements again in $\mathbb Q_{>0}$?

    Answer

    For $p,q\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$, $p\cdot q\in\mathbb Q$, and the product of two positive numbers is positive. So $p\cdot q\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$.

  2. Associativity and commutativity: do you have to prove them from scratch?

    Answer

    No. They hold for all rationals, so in particular for all elements of $\mathbb Q_{>0}\subseteq\mathbb Q$. Say this sentence explicitly; it is the whole proof.

  3. Name the neutral element and the inverse of $q$, and check both lie in $\mathbb Q_{>0}$.

    Answer

    $1>0$, so $1\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$, and $1\cdot q=q$. For $q=\frac ab$ with positive integers $a,b$, put $q^{-1}:=\frac ba$. It is positive, so $q^{-1}\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$, and $q\cdot q^{-1}=\frac ab\cdot\frac ba=1$. All five items hold: an abelian group.

Worked example 2: Which property does not hold in every group? (FS19, question III)

The options for a group $(G,\circ,e)$: (a) $a\circ b=b\circ a$ for all $a,b$; (b) $a\circ e=e\circ a=a$ for all $a$; (c) for all $a$ there is $b$ with $a\circ b=b\circ a=e$; (d) $(a\circ b)\circ c=a\circ(b\circ c)$.

  1. Which three options are axioms?

    Answer

    (b) is the identity axiom, (c) the inverse axiom, (d) associativity. They hold in every group by definition.

  2. So the answer is (a). Give a group where it fails.

    Answer

    The bijections of $\{1,2,3\}$ to itself under composition. Let $f$ swap $1$ and $2$, and $g$ swap $2$ and $3$. Then $(g\circ f)(1)=g(2)=3$, but $(f\circ g)(1)=f(1)=2$. So $g\circ f\ne f\circ g$. Commutativity is what makes a group abelian; it is not part of “group”. (Halbeisen only meets abelian groups in §0, but the exam question uses the general definition.)

4. The clock: residue classes $\mathbb Z/n$

The script calls this example “central to us” (p. 8): it is our first finite group, our first finite ring, and for prime $n$ our first finite field. Fix $n\in\mathbb N$, $n\ge1$.

Definition (congruence modulo $n$, p. 8). For $a,b\in\mathbb Z$:

$$\begin{aligned}a\equiv b \pmod n\ &:\iff\ n \text{ divides } b-a\\&\iff\ \exists\,l\in\mathbb Z:\ b-a=ln.\end{aligned}$$

This is an equivalence relation (Exercise 1.1.7; lesson 3 proves it). The residue class of $a$ is

$$\bar a:=a+n\mathbb Z=\{a+nl:\ l\in\mathbb Z\},$$

all integers with the same remainder as $a$ when divided by $n$. The set of classes is (Definition 1.1.11, p. 10)

$$\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z=\{\bar0,\bar1,\dots,\overline{n-1}\}.$$

We'll write $\mathbb Z/n$ for short. Each class has many names: for $n=3$, $\bar1=\bar4=\overline{-2}=\overline{10}$ (Example 1.1.10). The names $0,1,\dots,n-1$ are the standard ones. For $n=2$ the two classes are “the even numbers” and “the odd numbers”, and the rule “odd plus odd is even” becomes $\bar1+\bar1=\bar0$.

Definition (modular addition, Definition 1.1.12, p. 10). For $\bar a,\bar b\in\mathbb Z/n$:

$$\bar a+\bar b:=\overline{a+b}.$$

This definition takes a name $a$ of the first class and a name $b$ of the second, and computes with the names. If another choice of names gave a different class, the “definition” would not define anything. That is what well defined means, and it needs a proof.

Proof C: addition on $\mathbb Z/n$ is well defined (Lemma 1.1.13, p. 10)

Claim. Let $a,a',b,b'\in\mathbb Z$ with $\bar a=\overline{a'}$ and $\bar b=\overline{b'}$. Then $\overline{a+b}=\overline{a'+b'}$.

The idea. “Same class” means “the difference is a multiple of $n$”. So subtract the two sums and regroup: the difference of the sums is the sum of the differences, which is a sum of two multiples of $n$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Given (unfolded): $a-a'=ln$ and $b-b'=kn$ for some $l,k\in\mathbb Z$.
  • To show (unfolded): $(a+b)-(a'+b')=mn$ for some $m\in\mathbb Z$.
  • The gap: rewrite $(a+b)-(a'+b')$ in terms of the two given differences.
The storyWhat you write

Two travellers call the same hour by different numbers, and so do two others. The clerk writes down what “same hour” means.

Let $\bar a=\overline{a'}$ and $\bar b=\overline{b'}$. By definition there are $l,k\in\mathbb Z$ with $a-a'=ln$ and $b-b'=kn$.

Then she compares the two sums by subtracting them.

$(a+b)-(a'+b')=(a-a')+(b-b')$ $=ln+kn=(l+k)n$.

The difference is a whole number of turns of the clock, so both sums name the same hour.

Since $l+k\in\mathbb Z$, we get $\overline{a+b}=\overline{a'+b'}$: the sum does not depend on the choice of representatives. ∎

Moves used: two names, subtract unfold the definition

The proof in one piece

Let $a,a',b,b'\in\mathbb Z$ with $\bar a=\overline{a'}$ and $\bar b=\overline{b'}$. Then there are $l,k\in\mathbb Z$ with $a-a'=ln$ and $b-b'=kn$. It follows that $(a+b)-(a'+b')$ $=(a-a')+(b-b')$ $=(l+k)n$ with $l+k\in\mathbb Z$, so $\overline{a+b}=\overline{a'+b'}$. Hence the addition on $\mathbb Z/n$ is well defined. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Fill in: “$\bar a=\overline{a'}$ means that there is $l\in\mathbb Z$ with $\underline{\qquad}$.”
    Answer

    $a-a'=ln$ (or $a'-a=ln$; both are fine, $l$ just changes sign).

  2. Rung 2. Do the same for multiplication: show $\overline{ab}=\overline{a'b'}$ (script p. 17). Hint: $ab-a'b'=(a-a')b+a'(b-b')$.
    Answer

    $ab-a'b'=(a-a')b+a'(b-b')$ $=lnb+a'kn=(lb+a'k)n$ with $lb+a'k\in\mathbb Z$, so $\overline{ab}=\overline{a'b'}$. The trick “subtract and add $a'b$” is the same as in the product rule for limits in Analysis.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof C from the frame alone.
How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Take two names for each class; write the two “difference $=$ multiple of $n$” equations.
  2. Subtract the two results.
  3. Regroup into the given differences and factor out $n$.

In short: two names, subtract, factor $n$. Every “show that this is well defined” in this course works like this, including quotient spaces in lesson 9.

5. Subgroups and structure-preserving maps

Inside $(\mathbb Z,+)$ sit the even numbers $2\mathbb Z$: adding two even numbers gives an even number, and $-x$ is even when $x$ is. They form a group of their own, with the same operation.

Definition (subgroup, Definition 1.1.16, p. 11). Let $(G,\circ)$ be a group. A subset $H\subseteq G$ is a subgroup if

  1. $H\ne\emptyset$;
  2. $h_1\circ h_2\in H$ for all $h_1,h_2\in H$;
  3. $h^{-1}\in H$ for all $h\in H$.

Examples (Example 1.1.17). $\mathbb Z\subseteq\mathbb Q$ under $+$; $\{0\}\subseteq\mathbb Z$; $m\mathbb Z=\{ml:l\in\mathbb Z\}\subseteq\mathbb Z$ for every $m$; $\{e\}$ and $G$ in any group $G$; $\{\bar0,\bar2\}\subseteq\mathbb Z/4$. Non-examples: $\mathbb N\subseteq\mathbb Z$ (no inverses: $-1\notin\mathbb N$); $\{\bar0,\bar1\}\subseteq\mathbb Z/4$ ($\bar1+\bar1=\bar2\notin H$).

A subgroup is a group with the restricted operation (Note 1.1.18, p. 12). Closure is condition 2. Associativity holds for all elements of $G$, so in particular in $H$. Inverses are condition 3. And the neutral element is in $H$: pick any $h\in H$ (possible by condition 1), then $h^{-1}\in H$ by 3, and $e=h\circ h^{-1}\in H$ by 2. That is why the definition needs “$H\ne\emptyset$” instead of “$e\in H$”: the neutral element comes for free.

Why you'll meet this again. In lesson 8 a subspace is checked in exactly this way: nonempty, closed under the operations, and the rest is inherited. Learn the pattern once.

Next, maps that respect the operation. The map $\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/n$, $a\mapsto\bar a$, turns sums into sums: $\overline{a+b}=\bar a+\bar b$. Doubling $x\mapsto2x$ on $\mathbb Z$ does the same: $2(a+b)=2a+2b$.

Definition (homomorphism, isomorphism, Definition 1.1.19, p. 12). Let $(G,\circ)$ and $(H,\star)$ be groups. A map $\varphi:G\to H$ is a homomorphism if

$$\varphi(g_1\circ g_2)=\varphi(g_1)\star\varphi(g_2)\qquad\text{for all }g_1,g_2\in G.$$

A bijective homomorphism is an isomorphism.

Careful: on the left the operation is $G$'s, on the right it is $H$'s. Examples (Example 1.1.22): $\varphi_m:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z$, $a\mapsto ma$ (an isomorphism exactly when $m=\pm1$; the script's “iff $m=1$” forgets $-1$), and $\pi_n:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/n$, $a\mapsto\bar a$. The exponential $\exp:(\mathbb R,+)\to(\mathbb R_{>0},\cdot)$ is an isomorphism: $e^{x+y}=e^x\cdot e^y$, and it is bijective. It turns addition into multiplication, which is exactly what slide rules and logarithm tables were built on.

Proof E: a homomorphism maps neutral to neutral and inverse to inverse (Lemma 1.1.20, p. 12)

Claim. Let $\varphi:(G,\circ)\to(H,\star)$ be a homomorphism, with neutral elements $e_G$, $e_H$. Then (a) $\varphi(e_G)=e_H$, and (b) $\varphi(a^{-1})=\varphi(a)^{-1}$ for all $a\in G$.

The idea. For (a): $e_G=e_G\circ e_G$, so applying $\varphi$ gives $\varphi(e_G)=\varphi(e_G)\star\varphi(e_G)$. An element that equals its own square in a group must be $e_H$: cancel one copy with its inverse. For (b): $\varphi(a^{-1})$ does what an inverse of $\varphi(a)$ does, and inverses are unique (Proof B).

  • (b) To show: $\varphi(a^{-1})\star\varphi(a)=e_H=\varphi(a)\star\varphi(a^{-1})$; then uniqueness of inverses.

6. Rings: two operations that must cooperate

$\mathbb Z$ has two operations. $(\mathbb Z,+)$ is an abelian group, $(\mathbb Z,\cdot)$ is a commutative monoid, and the two are tied together by the distributive laws: $x\cdot(y+z)=x\cdot y+x\cdot z$.

Definition (ring, Definition 1.3.1, p. 16). A set $R$ with two operations $+:R\times R\to R$ and $\cdot:R\times R\to R$ is a ring if

  • (R1) $(R,+)$ is an abelian group;
  • (R2) $\cdot$ is associative;
  • (R3) for all $a,b,c\in R$: $a\cdot(b+c)=a\cdot b+a\cdot c$ and $(a+b)\cdot c=a\cdot c+b\cdot c$.

$R$ is commutative if $a\cdot b=b\cdot a$ for all $a,b$, and a ring with one if there is $1\in R$ with $1\cdot a=a\cdot1=a$ for all $a$.

In the lecture: a real difference

Halbeisen's ring (Definition 6) asks that $(M,\ast)$ be a monoid: his rings always have a one. So $(2\mathbb Z,+,\cdot)$ is a ring in the script (commutative, without one) but not a ring in the lecture. In Halbeisen's exam, “Ring” means “ring with one”; if you want to be safe, write “ring with one” and check the $1$. For fields both definitions agree, so nothing changes from section 7 on.

7. Fields: everything except zero can be divided by

Definition (field, Definition 1.3.7, p. 18). A set $K$ with two operations $+$ and $\cdot$ is a field if

  1. $(K,+)$ is an abelian group (neutral element $0$, inverse $-a$);
  2. $K^\times:=K\setminus\{0\}$ with $\cdot$ is an abelian group (neutral element $1$, inverse $a^{-1}=\frac1a$);
  3. the distributive laws hold.

In other words: a commutative ring with one in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse.

The script also gives the same definition as one flat list of ten axioms (Definition 1.3.9, p. 18), which makes a handy checklist: for $+$ associativity, commutativity, $0$, $-x$; for $\cdot$ associativity, $1$, $x^{-1}$ for $x\ne0$; distributivity; $1\ne0$; commutativity of $\cdot$.

In the lecture. Halbeisen's Definition 7 says exactly this: $(M,\circ,\ast)$ is a commutative ring and $(M\setminus\{e_\circ\},\ast)$ is an abelian group, where $e_\circ$ is the neutral element of $\circ$. His example is $(\mathbb Q,+,\cdot)$, checked in three bullet points.

Why remove $0$? Because $0\cdot a=0$ in every ring, $0$ could only have an inverse if $0=1$. So “every element except $0$ is invertible” is the most one can ask. And “$K^\times$ is a group” quietly contains two facts: $1\ne0$ (the group $K^\times$ contains its neutral element $1$, but not $0$), and a field has no zero divisors (closure of $K^\times$: nonzero times nonzero is nonzero). That is Lemma 1.3.10 (a) and (c), p. 19, proved in lesson 4.

Examples. $\mathbb Q$, $\mathbb R$, $\mathbb C$ (Example 1.3.11). $\mathbb Z/5$: the table above shows an inverse for each nonzero class ($\bar2^{-1}=\bar3$, $\bar4^{-1}=\bar4$). In general $\mathbb Z/p$ is a field for every prime $p$, written $\mathbb F_p$ (Corollary 1.3.15; lesson 4 proves it with the pigeonhole principle from lesson 3).

Non-examples. $\mathbb Z$: $2$ has no inverse in $\mathbb Z$, since $2x=1$ has no integer solution. $\mathbb Z/4$ and $\mathbb Z/6$: they have zero divisors, and a field has none. More directly, $\bar2$ has no inverse in $\mathbb Z/4$: its row $0,2,0,2$ never shows a $1$.

Why it's cool

From lesson 5 on, everything is done “over a field $K$”. Every theorem you prove there holds at once for $\mathbb Q$, $\mathbb R$, $\mathbb C$, and for the finite fields $\mathbb F_p$, where $\bar1+\bar1=\bar0$ in $\mathbb F_2$. That last world is not exotic: error-correcting codes in every phone, QR code and hard disk are linear algebra over $\mathbb F_2$ and its relatives. One list of axioms, many worlds, and the proofs only have to be written once.

For wonder (optional, 5 min): the clock that checks your bank account

Every IBAN (the long Swiss account number, CH… ) contains two check digits chosen so that a certain number built from the IBAN is $\equiv1\pmod{97}$. When you mistype one digit, the residue modulo $97$ changes, and the bank's computer rejects the number before any money moves. Why $97$? It is a prime, so $\mathbb Z/97$ is a field, and in a field multiplying by a nonzero number never sends a nonzero error to $0$: no zero divisors means no undetected single-digit errors and no undetected swaps of two neighbouring digits. The zero divisors of $\mathbb Z/4$ that the council kept out of the house of Field are exactly the reason a check modulo $100$ would be worse.

Worked example 5: $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle)$ is an abelian group (Serie 0, exercise 4)

For a nonempty set $M$, $\mathcal P(M)$ is the set of all subsets of $M$, and for $x,y\subseteq M$ the symmetric difference is $x\triangle y:=(x\setminus y)\cup(y\setminus x)$.

  1. Say in words when $t\in x\triangle y$.

    Answer

    Exactly when $t$ lies in exactly one of $x$ and $y$. (Call this $(\ast)$; the official solution builds everything on it.)

  2. Operation, commutativity, neutral element, inverses?

    Answer

    Operation: $x\triangle y\subseteq x\cup y\subseteq M$, so $x\triangle y\in\mathcal P(M)$. Commutative: $(\ast)$ is symmetric in $x$ and $y$.     Answer

This condition doesn't depend on the order of $x,y,z$, so (with commutativity) $t\in x\triangle(y\triangle z)=(y\triangle z)\triangle x$ iff the same condition holds. Equal elements, equal sets.

Exercise 1 (Serie 0, exercise 0)

Exercise 2 (Serie 0, exercise 1 (b))

Show that $(\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is an abelian group.

Solution

Frame: as in Worked example 1; the only new point is closure. Moves: the structure checklist, inherit from the bigger structure.

Operation: let $p,q\in\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}$. If $pq=0$, then since $p\ne0$, $q=\frac1p\cdot(pq)=\frac1p\cdot0=0$, contradicting $q\ne0$. So $pq\ne0$, i.e. $pq\in\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}$. Associativity and commutativity hold in $\mathbb Q$, so in particular on the subset. Neutral element: $1\ne0$ and $1\cdot q=q$. Inverses: $q=\frac ab$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}$; then $q^{-1}:=\frac ba\in\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}$ and $q\cdot q^{-1}=1$. So it is an abelian group.

Associativity and commutativity hold in every term because they hold in $\mathbb R$. Neutral: the zero sequence $(0,0,\dots)$. Inverse of $(a_n)$: $(-a_n)$, since $(a_n+(-a_n))=(0)$.

(b) Associativity and commutativity hold for all elements of $S$, in particular for those of $\mathrm{Fib}$. Closure: if $(f_n),(g_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$ and $h_n:=f_n+g_n$, then for every $i$

$$\begin{aligned}h_{i+2}&=f_{i+2}+g_{i+2}\\&=(f_{i+1}+f_i)+(g_{i+1}+g_i)\\&=(f_{i+1}+g_{i+1})+(f_i+g_i)\\&=h_{i+1}+h_i,\end{aligned}$$

using associativity and commutativity in $\mathbb R$. So $(h_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$. The zero sequence is in $\mathrm{Fib}$ ($0=0+0$) and is neutral in all of $S$. If $(f_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$, then $-f_{i+2}=-(f_{i+1}+f_i)=(-f_{i+1})+(-f_i)$, so $(-f_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$, and it is the inverse by (a). Hence $(\mathrm{Fib},+)$ is an abelian group. (In lesson 5 it becomes a vector space, and in Serie 6 its basis gives the closed formula for the Fibonacci numbers.)

Exercise 4 (Serie 0, exercise 2)

Let $V=\mathbb Z^4$, the set of 4-tuples of integers, with componentwise addition $+_V$. Show that $(V,+_V)$ is an abelian group.

Solution

Frame: the checklist, one component at a time. Moves: the structure checklist.

Write $v=(v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4)$; two tuples are equal iff all four components agree, and $(v+_Vw)_i=v_i+w_i$. Operation: $v_i+w_i\in\mathbb Z$, so $v+_Vw\in V$. Associativity: $((u+_Vv)+_Vw)_i=(u_i+v_i)+w_i$ $=u_i+(v_i+w_i)$ $=(u+_V(v+_Vw))_i$ for each $i$. Commutativity likewise. Neutral: $0_V=(0,0,0,0)$. Inverse: $-v=(-v_1,-v_2,-v_3,-v_4)\in V$ and $v+_V(-v)=0_V$. So $(V,+_V)$ is an abelian group. (Every argument works for $K^n$ over any field $K$: the coordinate space of lesson 5.)

Exercise 5 (Serie 1, exercise 6)

(a) Show that $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle,\cap)$ is a commutative ring with one for nonempty $M$. (b) Show it is not a field once $|M|>1$.

Hint

(a) The additive group is Worked example 5. What is the “one” for $\cap$? For distributivity, split into $t\in x$ and $t\notin x$. (b) Take a one-element subset.

Solution

Frame: (R1) done; (R2), commutativity, one, (R3) by element-wise membership; (b) one counterexample. Moves: decide membership element by element, one counterexample is enough.

(a) (R1) is Worked example 5. $\cap$ is an operation on $\mathcal P(M)$ ($x\cap y\subseteq M$), associative and commutative because “$t\in x$ and $t\in y$” is. The one is $M$: $M\cap x=x$ for $x\subseteq M$. Distributivity $x\cap(y\triangle z)=(x\cap y)\triangle(x\cap z)$: let $t\in M$. If $t\notin x$, $t$ is in neither side. If $t\in x$, then $t\in x\cap y\iff t\in y$ and $t\in x\cap z\iff t\in z$, so both sides contain $t$ iff $t$ lies in exactly one of $y,z$. The other distributive law follows by commutativity.

(b) Let $p\ne q$ in $M$. Then $\{p\}\ne\emptyset$, the zero of this ring. For every $y$, $\{p\}\cap y\subseteq\{p\}$, which does not contain $q$, so $\{p\}\cap y\ne M$. Hence $\{p\}$ has no inverse for $\cap$, and the ring is not a field. (Also: $\{p\}\cap\{q\}=\emptyset$ shows zero divisors.)

Exercise 6 (Serie 1, exercise 8 (a)–(c))

Show that $\bar a\cdot\bar b:=\overline{ab}$ is well defined on $\mathbb Z/m$, that $(\mathbb Z/m,+,\cdot)$ is a commutative ring with one, and that it is not a field if $m$ is not prime.

Solution

Frame: well-definedness by two names; ring axioms inherited from $\mathbb Z$; a counterexample from a factorisation. Moves: two names, subtract; inherit from the bigger structure; one counterexample is enough.

Well defined: Rung 2 of Proof C. Ring with one: $(\mathbb Z/m,+)$ is an abelian group (Proof D); for multiplication, $(\bar a\bar b)\bar c=\overline{(ab)c}=\overline{a(bc)}=\bar a(\bar b\bar c)$, $\bar a\bar b=\overline{ab}=\overline{ba}=\bar b\bar a$, $\bar1\bar a=\bar a$, and $\bar a(\bar b+\bar c)=\overline{a(b+c)}=\overline{ab+ac}=\bar a\bar b+\bar a\bar c$, all from the rules in $\mathbb Z$. Not a field: if $m$ is not prime and $m>1$, write $m=kl$ with $1<k,l<m$. Then $\bar k\ne\bar0\ne\bar l$ (they are standard names between $1$ and $m-1$), but $\bar k\cdot\bar l=\bar m=\bar0$: zero divisors. If $\bar k$ had an inverse, $\bar l=\bar k^{-1}\bar k\bar l=\bar k^{-1}\cdot\bar0=\bar0$, a contradiction. (For $m=1$, $\mathbb Z/1=\{\bar0\}$ has $\bar1=\bar0$, which no field allows.) Part (d), “$m$ prime ⇒ field”, is in lesson 4.

Exercise 7 (old exam style)

True or false? (a) $(\mathbb N,+)$ is a group. (b) $(\mathbb Z/7\setminus\{\bar0\},\cdot)$ is a group. (c) Every subgroup of $(\mathbb Z,+)$ contains $0$. (d) $\exp:(\mathbb R,+)\to(\mathbb R_{>0},\cdot)$ is a homomorphism. (e) There is a field with exactly one element.

Solution

(a) False: $1$ has no inverse in $\mathbb N$. (b) True: $7$ is prime, so $\mathbb Z/7$ is a field (lesson 4), and $K^\times$ is a group for every field; check one inverse: $\bar3\cdot\bar5=\overline{15}=\bar1$. (c) True: every subgroup contains the neutral element (section 5). (d) True: $e^{x+y}=e^xe^y$. (e) False: a field has $1\ne0$, so at least two elements.
