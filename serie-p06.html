Serie 6, explained

Gradient, curl and conservative force fields, the storm against the Grande Dixence dam, a crate sliding into a wagon, and capturing an asteroid around the Moon. For every task: the idea in plain words, a sketch, up to three hints that each give away a little more, then the solution with every step explained next to what you write, and the trap people fall into.

How to use this page.

  1. Read the task and try it on paper for 10 minutes. Draw a sketch first: in physics it is half the work.
  2. Stuck on where to start? Read The idea and open You need.
  3. Still stuck? Open Hint 1, try again, then Hint 2, then Hint 3. Hint 3 is almost the whole solution.
  4. Only then open the solution. The left column says why each step is there, the right column is what you write.

The solutions follow the official Lösung (Serie 6, Physik I HS26). The task texts are short English summaries: keep the official sheet next to you for the exact wording. With the StudyHub connector, your own AI can check your attempt before you look.

Aufgabe 1 · Vectors and force fields

  1. Compute the gradients of (i) $f_1(x,y)=x^2+y^2$ and (ii) $f_2(x,y)=x^2-y^2$, and sketch the gradient and the level lines.
  2. Which of these force fields are conservative? $$\begin{aligned}\vec F_1&=-mg\,\hat e_z,\\ \vec F_2&=-Cx\,\hat e_x+Dy^2\,\hat e_y,\\ \vec F_3&=d\,(\vec a\times\vec r).\end{aligned}$$
  3. Find the potentials for the fields that have one.
  4. For each non-conservative field from b), compute the work from the start of the curve at $t=0$ to the point $(1,1,1)$, once along $\vec r(t)=(t,t,t)$ and once along $\vec r(t)=(t,t,t^2)$.
The idea

The gradient points uphill, as steeply as possible, and is always perpendicular to the level lines (like contour lines on a map). A force field is conservative if it is “minus the gradient of something”, a potential $U$. Then the work between two points doesn't depend on the path. The test is the curl: if $\nabla\times\vec F=\vec0$, a potential exists; if not, it doesn't. Part d) shows what “not conservative” means in practice: two paths with the same start and end give different work.

Grey: level lines. Coloured: the gradient (direction only). Left, circles with arrows straight out. Right, hyperbolas (dotted: the lines $y=\pm x$ where $f_2=0$), arrows pointing away from the $y$-axis and towards the $x$-axis.

You need

$\nabla f=\big(\partial_xf,\ \partial_yf,\ \partial_zf\big)$. Curl: $$\nabla\times\vec F=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_yF_z-\partial_zF_y\\ \partial_zF_x-\partial_xF_z\\ \partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x\end{pmatrix}.$$ Conservative ⟺ $\nabla\times\vec F=\vec0$ (on all of space) ⟺ $\vec F=-\nabla U$. Work along a path: $W=\int_0^1\vec F(\vec r(t))\cdot\dot{\vec r}(t)\,dt$ (Physics lesson 5, lesson 6).

Hint 1

a) Differentiate with respect to $x$ (treat $y$ as a constant), then with respect to $y$. For the sketch: where is $f$ constant? For $f_1$ these are circles; for $f_2$, $x^2-y^2=c$ are hyperbolas. b) $\vec F_1$ is constant. For $\vec F_2$, check what each component depends on.

Hint 2

b) Write out $\vec a\times\vec r$ first: $\big(a_2z-a_3y,\ a_3x-a_1z,\ a_1y-a_2x\big)$. Then compute each curl component, e.g. $\partial_y F_z-\partial_z F_y=d\,a_1-(-d\,a_1)$. c) Find $U$ with $-\partial_xU=F_x$, $-\partial_yU=F_y$, $-\partial_zU=F_z$ by integrating each component.

Hint 3

d) Only $\vec F_3$ is non-conservative. Path 1: $\vec r=(t,t,t)$, $\dot{\vec r}=(1,1,1)$, so $\vec F_3=d\,t\,(a_2-a_3,\ a_3-a_1,\ a_1-a_2)$. Its components add to $0$. Path 2: $\vec r=(t,t,t^2)$, $\dot{\vec r}=(1,1,2t)$.

Solution

a) Gradients.

Why this stepWhat you write

Partial derivatives, one variable at a time.

$\nabla f_1=\begin{pmatrix}2x\\2y\end{pmatrix}$, $\quad\nabla f_2=\begin{pmatrix}2x\\-2y\end{pmatrix}$.

Describe the sketch: level lines, and the gradient perpendicular to them, pointing uphill.

$f_1$: level lines are circles around the origin; $\nabla f_1$ points radially outwards and gets longer further out.
$f_2$: level lines are hyperbolas $x^2-y^2=c$ (the lines $y=\pm x$ for $c=0$); $\nabla f_2$ points away from the $y$-axis in $x$ and towards the $x$-axis in $y$ (a saddle).

b) Conservative or not?

A constant field has no derivatives at all, so its curl is zero.

$\nabla\times\vec F_1=\vec0$: conservative (as expected for gravity near the ground).

In $\vec F_2$, $F_x$ depends only on $x$ and $F_y$ only on $y$. Every “cross” derivative in the curl is zero.

$$\begin{aligned}&\nabla\times\vec F_2\\&=\begin{pmatrix}0-\partial_z(Dy^2)\\ \partial_z(-Cx)-0\\ \partial_x(Dy^2)-\partial_y(-Cx)\end{pmatrix}\\&=\vec0.\end{aligned}$$ Conservative.

Write out the cross product. Here $F_x$ depends on $y$ and $z$ but not on $x$: a hint that it will fail.

$$\vec F_3=d\begin{pmatrix}a_2z-a_3y\\a_3x-a_1z\\a_1y-a_2x\end{pmatrix}.$$

Compute the curl component by component.

$$\begin{aligned}\nabla\times\vec F_3&=\begin{pmatrix}da_1+da_1\\da_2+da_2\\da_3+da_3\end{pmatrix}\\&=2d\,\vec a\neq\vec0\end{aligned}$$ (for $\vec a\ne\vec 0$). Not conservative.

c) Potentials (from $\vec F=-\nabla U$).

Integrate each component. The first two equations say $U$ doesn't depend on $x$ or $y$; the third gives the $z$-part.

$0=-\partial_xU$, $0=-\partial_yU$, $-mg=-\partial_zU$, so $U_1=mgz+U_0$.

Same procedure; the minus signs flip.

$-\partial_xU=-Cx$, $-\partial_yU=Dy^2$, so $U_2=\frac C2x^2-\frac D3y^3+U_0$.

A field with non-zero curl can't have a potential.

$\vec F_3$ has no potential.

d) Work for $\vec F_3$ from $\vec r(0)=(0,0,0)$ to $(1,1,1)$, with $W=\int_0^1\vec F_3(\vec r(t))\cdot\dot{\vec r}(t)\,dt$.

Path 1: put $x=y=z=t$ into $\vec F_3$, dot with $\dot{\vec r}=(1,1,1)$, i.e. add the components.

$$\begin{aligned}W_1=d\int_0^1\big[&(a_2-a_3)\\+&(a_3-a_1)\\+&(a_1-a_2)\big]\,t\,dt\\=0.&\end{aligned}$$

Path 2: now $z=t^2$ and $\dot{\vec r}=(1,1,2t)$.

$$\vec F_3=d\begin{pmatrix}a_2t^2-a_3t\\a_3t-a_1t^2\\a_1t-a_2t\end{pmatrix}.$$

Dot product: the $a_3$-terms cancel, the rest collect into one $t^2$ term.

$$\begin{aligned}W_2&=d\int_0^1(a_1-a_2)\,t^2\,dt\\&=\tfrac13\,d\,(a_1-a_2).\end{aligned}$$

Compare: same endpoints, different work (whenever $a_1\neq a_2$).

$W_1\neq W_2$: the work depends on the path, which confirms that $\vec F_3$ is not conservative.

Trap

Forgetting the minus sign in $\vec F=-\nabla U$: then $U_1=-mgz$, which would make the potential energy fall as you go up. Check your $U$ by differentiating it back. In d), also don't forget the factor $\dot{\vec r}$: you integrate $\vec F\cdot\frac{d\vec r}{dt}\,dt$, not $\vec F\,dt$.

Aufgabe 2 · A gust of wind against the Grande Dixence

The Grande Dixence dam is $695\,$m long and $285\,$m high. In a storm, air at $100\,$km/h hits the wall at right angles and is deflected sideways (parallel to the wall). Air has density $1.3\,$kg/m³. Set up the equation for the impulse and use it to find the average force of the wind on the dam.

The idea

The wind is a stream of air that the wall stops (in the direction towards the wall). Stopping momentum takes a force: force is momentum stopped per second. Each second, the wall stops a slab of air $v$ metres thick, of mass $\rho Av$, moving at speed $v$. So the momentum stopped per second is $\rho Av\cdot v$. That gives $F=\rho Av^2$: double the wind speed, four times the force.

In a time $\Delta t$, a slab of air of thickness $\Delta x=v\Delta t$ hits the wall and loses all its momentum towards the wall.

You need

Impulse $=$ change of momentum: $J=F\,\Delta t=\Delta p$; Newton's third law (the force of the air on the wall is opposite to the force of the wall on the air). Momentum: Physics lesson 7.

Hint 1

Look at the air in a box of cross-section $A$ (the wall's area) and thickness $\Delta x$ in front of the wall. What is its mass? What is its momentum towards the wall before and after hitting it?

Hint 2

$\Delta p_x=0-mv=-\rho A\Delta x\,v$. The wall gets the opposite impulse, $J=\rho A\Delta x\,v$. This happens in the time the slab needs to arrive: $\Delta t=\frac{\Delta x}v$.

Hint 3

$F=\frac J{\Delta t}=\rho Av^2$. Now put in $A=695\cdot285\,$m² and $v=27.8\,$m/s.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Pick a parcel of air: the slab in front of the wall. $+x$ points towards the wall.

Mass of the slab: $m=\rho A\,\Delta x$.

It arrives with speed $v$ and leaves sideways, so its $x$-momentum drops to $0$.

$\Delta p_x=0-mv_x=-\rho A\Delta x\,v_x$.

The wall receives the opposite impulse (third law), and impulse is average force × time.

$J_x=-\Delta p_x=\rho A\Delta x\,v_x=F\,\Delta t$.

The time is how long the slab takes to arrive: $\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}=v_x$.

$$F=\frac{\rho A\,\Delta x\,v_x}{\Delta t}=\rho A\,v_x^2.$$

Numbers: $v=100\,\text{km/h}=27.8\,$m/s.

$$\begin{aligned}F&=1.3\cdot(695\cdot285)\cdot27.8^2\,\text N\\&\approx2.0\cdot10^8\,\text N.\end{aligned}$$

Sense check. $2\cdot10^8\,$N sounds huge, but spread over $2\cdot10^5\,$m² it is a pressure of only $1000\,$Pa, one hundredth of the air pressure. If the air bounced straight back instead of sliding off sideways, the momentum change (and the force) would double.

Trap

Writing $F=\rho Av$ (mass per second) and forgetting the second $v$: force is momentum per second, and each kilogram brings momentum $v$. A units check catches it: $\rho Av$ has units kg/s, not N.

Aufgabe 3 · Loading station (part of an old exam problem)

A crate of mass $m=200\,$kg, initially at rest, slides down a slope (angle $\alpha=30^\circ$, length $l=10\,$m, kinetic friction $\mu_G=0.4$) into a freight wagon of mass $M=2\,$t, where it comes to rest. The unbraked wagon then rolls horizontally and soon hits an elastic buffer.

  1. With what speed $v_k$ does the crate slide into the wagon? (Neglect the crate's length.)
  2. With what speed $v_W$ does the wagon move after the crate has landed in it?
  3. How soft must the buffer be so that static friction ($\mu_H=0.7$) keeps the crate from sliding during the impact? Find the maximum spring constant $k$.
The idea

Three stages, three tools. Slope: energy (height energy minus friction work becomes kinetic energy). Landing: momentum, but only the horizontal part, because the ground takes up the vertical part; the crate's speed along the slope has horizontal part $v_k\cos\alpha$. Buffer: the spring stops the wagon, and at maximum compression the deceleration is largest. The crate rides along only if static friction can give it that deceleration. A stiffer spring stops the wagon faster, so there is a maximum $k$.

Slope (energy) → landing in the wagon (horizontal momentum) → buffer (spring; maximum deceleration at maximum compression).

You need

Energy with friction: $\Delta E_{\text{kin}}=mgh-W_{\text{friction}}$, with $W=\mu_GF_Nl$ and $F_N=mg\cos\alpha$ (Physics lesson 6). Perfectly inelastic collision: momentum is conserved (Physics lesson 8). Spring energy $\frac12kx^2$, spring force $kx$.

Hint 1

a) Height of the slope: $h=l\sin\alpha$. Friction work along the slope: $l\mu_Gmg\cos\alpha$. Set $\frac12mv_k^2=mgh-W$. b) Only horizontal momentum is conserved. What is the horizontal part of $mv_k$?

Hint 2

c) At maximum compression all of the kinetic energy $\frac12(M+m)v_W^2$ is in the spring, $\frac12kx^2$. That gives $x$. The force is then $kx$, so the deceleration is $a=\frac{kx}{M+m}$.

Hint 3

Combining: $a=v_W\sqrt{\frac{k}{M+m}}$. The crate doesn't slide if static friction can supply $ma$: $ma\le\mu_Hmg$. Set $v_W\sqrt{\frac k{M+m}}=\mu_Hg$ and solve for $k$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Energy at the top, minus the work friction does over the whole slope, is the kinetic energy at the bottom.

$$\begin{aligned}\tfrac12mv_k^2&=mgl\sin\alpha\\&\quad-l\,\mu_Gmg\cos\alpha.\end{aligned}$$

Mass cancels; solve.

$$\begin{aligned}v_k&=\sqrt{2lg(\sin\alpha-\mu_G\cos\alpha)}\\&=\sqrt{196.2\cdot0.154}\,\tfrac{\text m}{\text s}\approx5.5\,\tfrac{\text m}{\text s}.\end{aligned}$$ (The official solution rounds to $5\,$m/s.)

b) The rails and ground take up the vertical push, so only horizontal momentum is conserved. The crate ends at rest in the wagon: they move together.

$$mv_k\cos\alpha=(M+m)\,v_W.$$

Solve.

$v_W=\dfrac{mv_k\cos\alpha}{M+m}=\dfrac{200\cdot5.5\cdot0.866}{2200}\,\dfrac{\text m}{\text s}\approx0.4\,\dfrac{\text m}{\text s}$.

c) The largest force comes at the largest compression, when all the kinetic energy is stored in the spring.

$\tfrac12kx^2=\tfrac12(M+m)v_W^2\ \Rightarrow\ x=v_W\sqrt{\dfrac{M+m}{k}}$.

Newton for the wagon (with the crate) at that moment.

$(M+m)\,a=kx\ \Rightarrow\ a=v_W\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{M+m}}$.

The crate must decelerate with the wagon. Only static friction can do that, at most $\mu_Hmg$.

No sliding if $ma\le\mu_Hmg$. Limit: $v_W\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{M+m}}=\mu_Hg$.

Solve for $k$. A softer spring is fine; a stiffer one makes the crate slide.

$$\begin{aligned}k_{\max}&=\frac{(M+m)\,g^2\mu_H^2}{v_W^2}\\&\approx6\cdot10^5\,\frac{\text{kg}}{\text s^2}.\end{aligned}$$

About the rounding. The official solution uses the rounded $v_W=0.4\,$m/s and gets $k\approx6\cdot10^5\,$kg/s². With the unrounded $v_W=0.43\,$m/s you get $5.6\cdot10^5\,$kg/s². Since $k\propto\frac1{v_W^2}$, small rounding in $v_W$ shows up doubled in $k$. Either answer, with the working shown, is fine.

Trap

Using the full $mv_k$ in b). The crate comes in at $30^\circ$ downwards; the vertical part of its momentum goes into the ground, not into the wagon's motion. Second trap: using kinetic energy conservation for the landing. The crate comes to rest in the wagon, which is a perfectly inelastic collision: energy is lost, momentum is not.

Aufgabe 4 · Capturing an asteroid

A platinum-rich asteroid ($m_a=10^6\,$kg) is to be put into a stable circular orbit around the Moon ($R_m=1737\,$km, $M_m=7.5\cdot10^{22}\,$kg). At time $t_a$ it passes the Moon at $v_a=3\,$km/s, parallel to the surface, at its closest distance $d=13\,$km above the surface. Neglect the Earth; the Moon's potential vanishes at infinity.

  1. Show that the asteroid's kinetic energy is enough to escape the Moon.
  2. Find the speed $v_{\text{orbit}}$ for a circular orbit at height $d$.
  3. A rocket ($m_r=10^5\,$kg), flying exactly against the asteroid, hits it at $t_a$ and sticks to it, slowing it to $v_{\text{orbit}}$. What speed $v_r$ must the rocket have?
  4. (i) What happens if the rocket is faster (less energy and angular momentum than in c)? (ii) What is the largest $v_r$ for which the asteroid doesn't crash into the Moon? (iii) What is the smallest $v_r$ that still gives a bound (stable) orbit?
The idea

Energy decides whether something is bound: total energy $E=\frac12mv^2-\frac{GMm}r$ negative means it can never escape. The asteroid has $E>0$, so it would fly away. The rocket hit is a perfectly inelastic collision: momentum is conserved, and you choose $v_r$ so the combined speed is exactly what you want. That speed sets the orbit: exactly $v_{\text{orbit}}$ gives a circle; a bit slower gives an ellipse that dips towards the Moon (too slow and it hits the surface); faster up to $\sqrt2\,v_{\text{orbit}}$ gives an ellipse that swings out; beyond that it escapes.

Schematic (heights exaggerated). The collision point is the farthest point of the “slower” ellipse and the closest point of the “faster” one.

You need

$E=\frac12mv^2-\frac{GMm}r$; bound orbit ⟺ $E<0$. Circular orbit: $\frac{mv^2}r=\frac{GMm}{r^2}$. Inelastic collision: momentum conservation. Ellipse: angular momentum $r_Av_A=r_Pv_P$ at the far and near points, plus energy conservation (Physics lesson 8, lesson 9).

Hint 1

a) Compute $E=\frac12m_av_a^2-\frac{GM_mm_a}{R_m+d}$ and check its sign. b) Gravity is the centripetal force at $r=R_m+d$.

Hint 2

c) Momentum along the asteroid's direction: $m_av_a-m_rv_r=(m_a+m_r)v_{\text{orbit}}$. Solve for $v_r$. d) For (ii): the new orbit has its farthest point at $r_A=R_m+d$ and its closest at $r_P=R_m$ (just touching).

Hint 3

(ii) Combine $r_Av_A=r_Pv_P$ and $\frac12v_A^2-\frac{GM}{r_A}=\frac12v_P^2-\frac{GM}{r_P}$; eliminate $v_P$ to get $v_A$, then use the momentum equation from c). (iii) The bound limit is $E=0$: $v_{\text{crit}}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R_m+d}}=\sqrt2\,v_{\text{orbit}}$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Escape is possible when the total energy (with $U\to0$ at infinity) is positive.

$$\begin{aligned}E&=\tfrac12m_av_a^2-\frac{GM_mm_a}{R_m+d}\\&=4.5\cdot10^{12}\,\text J-2.86\cdot10^{12}\,\text J\\&=1.6\cdot10^{12}\,\text J>0.\end{aligned}$$ So it can escape.

b) Circle: gravity provides the centripetal force.

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{m_av^2}{R_m+d}&=\frac{GM_mm_a}{(R_m+d)^2}\\ \Rightarrow v_{\text{orbit}}&=\sqrt{\frac{GM_m}{R_m+d}}\\&\approx1691\,\tfrac{\text m}{\text s}.\end{aligned}$$

c) Perfectly inelastic collision, head-on: momentum conservation, with the rocket's momentum negative.

$m_av_a-m_rv_r=(m_a+m_r)\,v_{\text{orbit}}$.

Solve for $v_r$.

$$\begin{aligned}v_r&=\frac{m_av_a-(m_a+m_r)v_{\text{orbit}}}{m_r}\\&\approx11.40\,\frac{\text{km}}{\text s}.\end{aligned}$$

d)(i) A faster rocket brakes the asteroid more. The collision point becomes the farthest point of an ellipse, and the opposite side of the orbit comes closer to the Moon.

The orbit becomes an ellipse (as long as $E<0$) whose far point is the collision point; on the other side of the Moon it comes closer than $d$.

(ii) Limit: the near point just touches the surface. Use angular momentum and energy conservation between the far point A and the near point P.

$r_Av_A=r_Pv_P$ with $r_A=R_m+d$, $r_P=R_m$, and $$\tfrac12v_A^2-\frac{GM_m}{r_A}=\tfrac12v_P^2-\frac{GM_m}{r_P}.$$

Put $v_P=\frac{r_A}{r_P}v_A$ into the energy equation and solve for $v_A$.

$$\begin{aligned}v_A&=\sqrt{\frac{2GM_m\big(\frac1{r_A}-\frac1{r_P}\big)}{1-\big(\frac{r_A}{r_P}\big)^2}}\\&\approx1688\,\frac{\text m}{\text s}.\end{aligned}$$

Same momentum equation as in c).

$v_{r,\max}=\dfrac{m_av_a-(m_a+m_r)v_A}{m_r}\approx11.43\,$km/s (official: $11.44$). Only $0.3\,\%$ above the value for the circle: the rocket must be controlled very precisely.

(iii) Bound means $E<0$. The limit $E=0$ gives the escape speed at that height.

$$\begin{aligned}v_{\text{crit}}&=\sqrt{\frac{2GM_m}{R_m+d}}=\sqrt2\,v_{\text{orbit}}\\&\approx2.4\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text s}.\end{aligned}$$

Momentum equation again.

$v_{r,\min}=\dfrac{m_av_a-(m_a+m_r)v_{\text{crit}}}{m_r}\approx3.7\,$km/s (the official solution, with $v_{\text{crit}}$ rounded to $2.4\,$km/s, gets $3.6\,$km/s).

Trap

Using kinetic-energy conservation for the rocket hit: it is perfectly inelastic, so only momentum is conserved. And in a), comparing $\frac12mv^2$ with $mgh$: near the Moon $g$ is not constant over the distances involved, so use $-\frac{GMm}r$, with $r$ measured from the Moon's centre ($R_m+d$, not $d$).
