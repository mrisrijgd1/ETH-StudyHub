The Rails of the Plain

Differential equations: equations for an unknown function that involve its derivative. Three types are on every exam: separable equations $y'=f(x)g(y)$, linear equations of first order $y'+f(x)y=g(x)$, and linear equations of second order with constant coefficients $y''+a_1y'+a_0y=g(x)$. For each, a recipe, why it gives all solutions, and how to fit the initial values. Script section 8.5.

1. The story: the rails of the plain

On the great plain, the rail-spirit has laid a tiny piece of rail at every single point, each one tilted at its own angle. Nobody laid whole tracks; there are only these short pieces, infinitely many, side by side. Put a cart down anywhere, and it rolls along the pieces, always in the direction of the rail under its wheels, and so it traces out a long, curved journey.

The travellers ask two questions. Given where I start, where will I be later? And: could two carts starting from the same point ever part ways? The plain's surveyors can't answer for every plain. But on three kinds of plain, the ones the rail-spirit likes best, they have exact recipes, and these are the plains in every exam.

In maths: the rails are a direction field: at each point $(x,y)$ the equation $y'=f(x,y)$ prescribes a slope. A solution is a function whose graph has the prescribed slope at each of its points; the starting point is the initial value $y(x_0)=y_0$. The three friendly kinds are separable, linear first-order, and linear second-order with constant coefficients.

2. What a differential equation is

Definitions. An (ordinary, first-order) differential equation is an equation $y'=f(x,y)$ for an unknown differentiable function $y:I\to\mathbb R$ on an interval $I$: a solution satisfies $y'(x)=f(x,y(x))$ for all $x\in I$. Together with a condition $y(x_0)=y_0$ it is an initial value problem. A second-order equation involves $y''$ as well, and then one usually prescribes $y(x_0)$ and $y'(x_0)$.

Finding solutions can be hard, but checking one is always easy: differentiate and plug in. Make that your habit at the end of every exercise.

Worked example 1: solutions, and how many

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

  1. Check that $y=x-1+Ce^{-x}$ solves $y'=x-y$ for every $C\in\mathbb R$. Which $C$ gives $y(0)=1$?

    Answer

    $y'=1-Ce^{-x}$ and $$\begin{aligned}x-y&=x-x+1-Ce^{-x}\\&=1-Ce^{-x}\end{aligned}.$$Equal. $y(0)=-1+C=1$ gives $C=2$, the curve through the dot in the figure.

  2. Find two different solutions of $y'=2\sqrt{|y|}$ with $y(0)=0$. (2019 winter exam, 10c, 1 point: “give an initial value problem without a unique solution.”)

    Answer

    $y=0$, and $y(x)=x^2$ for $x\ge0$, $y(x)=0$ for $x<0$ (check: $y'=2x=2\sqrt{x^2}$ for $x\ge0$, and $0$ for $x<0$). Two carts from the same point can part ways! That's why “find all solutions” needs a proof, which our three recipes come with. (Uniqueness in general is a second-semester theorem.)

  3. Which equation has the solutions $y=Ce^{3x}$, and how many solutions does $y'=3y$, $y(0)=5$ have?

    Answer

    $y'=3y$. Exactly one: $y=5e^{3x}$. If $y$ is any solution, then $(ye^{-3x})'=(y'-3y)e^{-3x}=0$, so $ye^{-3x}$ is constant, equal to its value $5$ at $0$. This trick is Proof B below.

3. Separable equations

An equation $y'=f(x)\,g(y)$ is separable: the right side splits into a factor depending only on $x$ and one depending only on $y$. Examples: $y'=x(y^2+1)$, $y'=e^{x-y}=e^xe^{-y}$, $y'=\frac{y^2}{x^2}$. The cookbook writes $\frac{dy}{g(y)}=f(x)\,dx$ and integrates both sides. Here is why that's correct.

Proof A: separation of variables

Theorem. Let $f:I\to\mathbb R$ and $g:J\to\mathbb R$ be continuous on open intervals $I,J$, with $g(y)\neq0$ for all $y\in J$. Let $F$ be an antiderivative of $f$ and $H$ one of $\frac1g$. A differentiable function $y:I'\to J$ on an interval $I'\subseteq I$ solves $y'=f(x)g(y)$ if and only if there is a constant $C$ with

$$H\bigl(y(x)\bigr)=F(x)+C\qquad\text{for all }x\in I'.$$

$H$ is strictly monotone, so this can be solved for $y$: $y(x)=H^{-1}\bigl(F(x)+C\bigr)$. Separately, every zero $c$ of $g$ gives the constant solution $y\equiv c$.

The idea. $\frac{dy}{g(y)}=f(x)\,dx$ is a shorthand for a chain-rule statement: $\frac d{dx}H(y(x))=\frac{y'(x)}{g(y(x))}$, and the equation says this equals $f(x)=F'(x)$. Two functions with the same derivative on an interval differ by a constant.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • “⇒”: from $y'=f(x)g(y)$, show that $H(y(x))-F(x)$ is constant: its derivative is $0$ on an interval.
  • “⇐”: differentiate $H(y(x))=F(x)+C$.
  • Monotone: $H'=\frac1g$ never changes sign.
The storyWhat you write

Measure the cart's height with a bent ruler $H$; the bend exactly cancels the $y$-part of the rails.

“⇒”. Let $y$ be a solution. By the chain rule, for $x\in I'$,

$$\frac d{dx}H\bigl(y(x)\bigr)=\frac{y'(x)}{g\bigl(y(x)\bigr)}=f(x)=F'(x).$$

Same speed of change everywhere: the difference never changes.

So $\bigl(H\circ y-F\bigr)'=0$ on the interval $I'$, and by the mean value theorem $H\circ y-F$ is a constant $C$.

Backwards, the same computation.

“⇐”. If $H(y(x))=F(x)+C$, differentiate: $\frac{y'(x)}{g(y(x))}=f(x)$, so $y'(x)=f(x)g(y(x))$.

The ruler never folds back, so a reading gives back one height.

$g$ is continuous without zeros on the interval $J$, so by the intermediate value theorem it has one sign, and so does $H'=\frac1g$. Hence $H$ is strictly monotone and injective, and $y(x)=H^{-1}(F(x)+C)$. Finally, if $g(c)=0$, then $y\equiv c$ has $y'=0=f(x)g(c)$. ∎

Moves used: name the constant, then plug in a point check by differentiating

The proof in one piece

Let $y:I'\to J$ be differentiable. If $y'=f(x)g(y)$, then by the chain rule $(H\circ y)'(x)=\frac{y'(x)}{g(y(x))}=f(x)=F'(x)$ for all $x\in I'$; so $(H\circ y-F)'=0$ on the interval $I'$, and $H\circ y-F$ is constant by the mean value theorem. Conversely, differentiating $H(y(x))=F(x)+C$ gives $\frac{y'(x)}{g(y(x))}=f(x)$, i.e. $y'=f(x)g(y)$. Since $g$ is continuous and has no zeros on the interval $J$, it has constant sign by the intermediate value theorem; so $H'=\frac1g$ has constant sign, $H$ is strictly monotone, and $y=H^{-1}(F+C)$. If $g(c)=0$, the constant function $c$ is a solution since both sides vanish. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Write $y'=y^2$ in this form. What are $f$, $g$, $F$, $H$, and the non-constant solutions?

    Answer

    $f=1$, $g(y)=y^2$ on $J=(0,\infty)$ or $(-\infty,0)$, $F=x$, $H(y)=-\frac1y$. So $-\frac1y=x+C$, $y=-\frac1{x+C}$, on an interval not containing $-C$. Plus the constant solution $y\equiv0$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Why does the theorem need $J$ to avoid the zeros of $g$, and why is that harmless in exam questions?

    Answer

    Dividing by $g(y)$ needs $g(y)\neq0$. In exam questions the initial value usually has $g(y_0)\neq0$; then $y$ stays in an interval $J$ without zeros of $g$, at least near $x_0$, and the formula holds there. (Example 2 of Worked example 1 shows what can happen when $g(y_0)=0$ and $g$ is not differentiable: several solutions.)

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

The recipe for a separable initial value problem $y'=f(x)g(y)$, $y(x_0)=y_0$

  1. Factor the right side as $f(x)\,g(y)$. (Einsiedler's HS21 grading gave the first point for this alone.)
  2. Separate and integrate: $\int\frac{dy}{g(y)}=\int f(x)\,dx$, giving $H(y)=F(x)+C$.
  3. Constant: put in $x_0,y_0$ to find $C$ (doing it before solving for $y$ is often easier).
  4. Solve for $y$ and give the maximal interval: the largest interval containing $x_0$ on which the formula makes sense and stays in $J$.
  5. Check by differentiating. Don't forget constant solutions if the question asks for all solutions.

Worked example 2: separable equations from the exams

  1. Solve $y'=xy^2+x$, $y(0)=1$. (Einsiedler HS21, question 3, 4 points.)

    Answer

    Factor: $y'=x(y^2+1)$; $g(y)=y^2+1$ has no zeros. Separate: $\int\frac{dy}{1+y^2}=\int x\,dx$, so $\arctan y=\frac{x^2}2+C$. At $x=0$: $\arctan1=\frac\pi4=C$. So

    $$y(x)=\tan\left(\frac{x^2}2+\frac\pi4\right),$$

    valid while $\frac{x^2}2+\frac\pi4<\frac\pi2$, i.e. for $|x|<\sqrt{\pi/2}$. (A common mistake from the grading notes: treating it as linear by dropping the $+x$. It isn't linear because of $y^2$.)

  2. Find all solutions $y:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ of $y'=e^{x-y}$ that are defined on all of $\mathbb R$. (Felder HS22, 1.A8, 4 points.)

    Answer

    $y'=e^xe^{-y}$, and $g(y)=e^{-y}$ has no zeros. Separate: $\int e^y\,dy=\int e^x\,dx$, so $e^y=e^x+C$ and $y=\log(e^x+C)$. This is defined on all of $\mathbb R$ if and only if $e^x+C>0$ for every $x$. As $e^x\to0$ for $x\to-\infty$, that means $C\ge0$. Answer: $y(x)=\log(e^x+C)$ with $C\ge0$.

  3. Solve $y'=y(1-y)$ with $y(0)=\frac12$.

    Answer

    Partial fractions: $\frac1{y(1-y)}=\frac1y+\frac1{1-y}$, so $\log\frac y{1-y}=x+C$ for $0<y<1$. At $0$: $\log1=0=C$. So $\frac y{1-y}=e^x$ and $y=\frac{e^x}{1+e^x}$, defined on all of $\mathbb R$. (The constant solutions $y\equiv0$ and $y\equiv1$ are the zeros of $g$. This is the logistic equation of population growth.)

4. Linear equations of first order

Definition. A linear differential equation of first order is $y'+f(x)\,y=g(x)$ with given continuous $f,g:I\to\mathbb R$ on an interval $I$. It is homogeneous if $g=0$, otherwise inhomogeneous; $g$ is the forcing term (Störfunktion).

“Linear” means: $y$ and $y'$ appear only to the first power, multiplied by functions of $x$. $y'=x^2y+\sin x$ is linear; $y'=y^2$ and $y'=\sin y$ are not. Some equations are both linear and separable, like $y'=\sin(x)\,y$; then either method works.

Proof B: all solutions of a linear first-order equation (Lemma 8.60, p. 398, and variation of constants, p. 398–399)

Theorem. Let $f,g:I\to\mathbb R$ be continuous on an interval $I$, $F$ an antiderivative of $f$, and $G$ an antiderivative of $g\,e^{F}$. Then the solutions $y:I\to\mathbb R$ of $y'+f(x)y=g(x)$ are exactly

$$y(x)=e^{-F(x)}\bigl(G(x)+C\bigr),\qquad C\in\mathbb R.$$

In particular (with $g=0$, $G=0$), the solutions of the homogeneous equation $y'+f(x)y=0$ are exactly the multiples $Ce^{-F(x)}$. For every $x_0\in I$ and $y_0\in\mathbb R$ there is exactly one solution with $y(x_0)=y_0$.

The idea. The script's way, in two steps. First solve the equation without the forcing term: the homogeneous solutions are $Ae^{-F(x)}$. Then vary the constant: try $y=A(x)e^{-F(x)}$ with a function $A$ instead of a number. Plugging in, everything except $A'$ cancels, and the equation turns into $A'=ge^F$, which lesson 19 solves by integrating. Finally, all solutions are one particular solution plus all homogeneous ones.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Two directions: every function of the form $e^{-F}(G+C)$ is a solution, and every solution has this form.
  • Uniqueness for the initial value: exactly one $C$ fits $y(x_0)=y_0$.
The storyWhat you write

First the rails without any push: a cart left alone.

Homogeneous equation. For differentiable $y:I\to\mathbb R$, the product rule and $F'=f$ give $\bigl(ye^{F}\bigr)'=\bigl(y'+fy\bigr)e^{F}$. So $y'+fy=0$ if and only if $\bigl(ye^F\bigr)'=0$, which on the interval $I$ holds if and only if $ye^F$ is a constant $A$, i.e. $y=Ae^{-F}$.

Now the push: let the constant travel along with the cart.

Variation of constants. Put $y_p=A(x)e^{-F}$ with $A$ differentiable. Then $$y_p'+fy_p=A'e^{-F}-Afe^{-F}+fAe^{-F}=A'e^{-F},$$so $y_p$ solves $y'+fy=g$ if $A'=ge^{F}$. Take $A=G$.

Two journeys under the same push differ by a journey without push.

All solutions. If $y$ solves $y'+fy=g$, then $(y-y_p)'+f(y-y_p)=g-g=0$, so $y-y_p=Ce^{-F}$ by the first step. Conversely, $y_p+Ce^{-F}$ is a solution. Hence the solutions are exactly $y=e^{-F}(G+C)$.

The starting point decides the constant.

$y(x_0)=y_0$ means $e^{-F(x_0)}(G(x_0)+C)=y_0$, which has exactly one solution $C=y_0e^{F(x_0)}-G(x_0)$. ∎

Moves used: a helper function name the constant, then plug in a point

The proof in one piece

For differentiable $y:I\to\mathbb R$, the product rule gives $(ye^F)'=(y'+fy)e^F$. Hence $y'+fy=0$ if and only if $ye^F$ is constant on the interval $I$, i.e. the homogeneous solutions are $Ae^{-F}$, $A\in\mathbb R$. For $y_p=Ge^{-F}$ we get $y_p'+fy_p=G'e^{-F}=ge^Fe^{-F}=g$, so $y_p$ is a particular solution (variation of constants). If $y$ is any solution, $y-y_p$ solves the homogeneous equation, so $y-y_p=Ce^{-F}$; conversely $y_p+Ce^{-F}$ is a solution. So the solutions are exactly $e^{-F}(G+C)$, and $y(x_0)=y_0$ determines $C=y_0e^{F(x_0)}-G(x_0)$ uniquely. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Homogeneous: $(ye^F)'=(y'+fy)e^F$, so $y_{\text{hom}}=Ae^{-F}$.
  2. Vary the constant: $y_p=A(x)e^{-F}$ gives $A'=ge^F$.
  3. All solutions: the difference of two solutions is homogeneous, so $y=y_p+Ae^{-F}$; fit $A$.

In short: “solve without $g$, then let the constant vary.” In multiple choice with $f=\frac kx$, the homogeneous solution is $Ax^{-k}$ and the whole question takes two minutes (Exercises 1 and 2).

A shortcut you may meet elsewhere: the integrating factor. Multiply the equation by $e^F$; the left side becomes $(ye^F)'$, so $(ye^F)'=ge^F$. It is the same computation, since for $y=A(x)e^{-F}$ we have $ye^F=A$, but in the exam write the script's steps.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why does the integrating-factor shortcut give the same answer as variation of constants?

    Answer

    With $y=A(x)e^{-F}$ we have $ye^F=A$, so $(ye^F)'=ge^F$ says exactly $A'=ge^F$. The two methods write the same equation in different letters.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Where was it used that $I$ is an interval? Give an example where the conclusion fails on a union of two intervals.

    Answer

    “Derivative $0$ ⇒ constant” needs an interval. On $(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)$, the equation $y'=\frac yx$ has the solutions $y=C_1x$ for $x<0$ and $y=C_2x$ for $x>0$ with independent constants.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

The recipe for $y'+f(x)y=g(x)$, $y(x_0)=y_0$ (the script's “Kochrezept”)

  1. Normal form: bring it to $y'+f(x)y=g(x)$ (watch the sign of $f$: $y'=2y+\sin x$ has $f=-2$).
  2. Homogeneous equation by separating variables: $\frac{dy}{y}=-f(x)\,dx$, so $\log|y|=-F(x)+c$ and $y_{\text{hom}}=Ae^{-F(x)}$. Often this is simple: $f=\frac kx$ gives $y_{\text{hom}}=Ax^{-k}$.
  3. Variation of constants: put $y_p=A(x)e^{-F(x)}$ into the equation. Everything but $A'$ cancels: $A'(x)=g(x)e^{F(x)}$. Integrate (any antiderivative will do).
  4. Constant: $y=y_p+y_{\text{hom}}$; fit $A$ from $y(x_0)=y_0$, then check.

Worked example 3: linear first-order equations

  1. The general solution of $y'=\sin(x)\,y$ (2017 summer exam, 1e, 1 point).

    Answer

    Normal form $y'-\sin(x)y=0$, so $f=-\sin x$, $F=\cos x$, and $y=Ce^{-\cos x}$, $C\in\mathbb R$. (Separable too: $\log|y|=-\cos x+c$.)

  2. Solve $y'-2xy=e^{x^2}$, $y(0)=1$ (the script's example).

    Answer

    $f=-2x$, $F=-x^2$, so $y_{\text{hom}}=Ae^{x^2}$. Vary the constant: $y=A(x)e^{x^2}$ gives $A'e^{x^2}=e^{x^2}$, so $A'=1$ and $A(x)=x$. Hence $y=(x+A)e^{x^2}$, and $y(0)=1$ gives $A=1$:

    $$y(x)=(x+1)e^{x^2}.$$

    Check: $$y'=e^{x^2}+2x(x+1)e^{x^2}=e^{x^2}+2xy.$$✓

  3. $u'=-\frac1xu+x^2$, $u(1)=\frac54$. Find $u(2)$. (Figalli FS24, MC6, 2 points.)

    Answer

    Normal form $u'+\frac1xu=x^2$ on $(0,\infty)$; $F=\log x$, so $u_{\text{hom}}=\frac Ax$. Vary the constant: $u=\frac{A(x)}x$ gives $\frac{A'}x=x^2$, so $A'=x^3$ and $A(x)=\frac{x^4}4$. Hence $u=\frac1x\left(\frac{x^4}4+A\right)$. At $x=1$: $\frac54=\frac14+A$, $A=1$. So $u=\frac{x^3}4+\frac1x$ and $u(2)=2+\frac12=\frac52$.

5. Second order with constant coefficients: the homogeneous equation

Definition. For constants $a_0,a_1$ (real or complex), $y''+a_1y'+a_0y=0$ is a homogeneous linear equation of second order with constant coefficients. Its characteristic polynomial is $p(T)=T^2+a_1T+a_0$. With a forcing term, $y''+a_1y'+a_0y=g(x)$ is inhomogeneous.

The guess: try $y=e^{\alpha x}$. Then $y'=\alpha y$ and $y''=\alpha^2y$, so $y''+a_1y'+a_0y=p(\alpha)\,e^{\alpha x}$. The exponential solves the equation exactly when $\alpha$ is a root of $p$. Everything else is bookkeeping, and two proofs make it watertight.

Proof C: superposition (Wichtige Übung 8.66 (i) and Übung 8.62)

Theorem. Let $L(y)=y''+a_1y'+a_0y$ (or $L(y)=y'+f(x)y$).

  1. If $y_1,y_2$ solve $L(y)=0$, so does $c_1y_1+c_2y_2$ for all constants $c_1,c_2$: the solutions form a vector space.
  2. If $y_p$ is one solution of $L(y)=g$, then the solutions of $L(y)=g$ are exactly $y=y_p+y_h$ with $y_h$ a solution of $L(y)=0$. In words: general = particular + homogeneous.
  3. If $L(y_1)=g_1$ and $L(y_2)=g_2$, then $L(y_1+y_2)=g_1+g_2$.

The idea. $L$ is linear: $L(c_1y_1+c_2y_2)=c_1L(y_1)+c_2L(y_2)$, because differentiation is linear. Everything follows, exactly as in linear algebra: the solutions of $Av=w$ are one particular solution plus the kernel of $A$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • 1 and 3: compute $L$ of the combination.
  • 2: two inclusions: $y_p+y_h$ is a solution; and any solution $y$ has $y-y_p$ homogeneous.
The storyWhat you write

The rule for the rails respects adding and scaling journeys.

By linearity of the derivative, $L(c_1y_1+c_2y_2)=c_1L(y_1)+c_2L(y_2)$ for twice differentiable $y_1,y_2$. This gives 1 (with $L(y_i)=0$) and 3.

One known journey plus a free journey is a journey.

2. If $L(y_h)=0$, then $L(y_p+y_h)=g+0=g$.

Two journeys under the same pull differ by a free journey.

Conversely, if $L(y)=g$, then $L(y-y_p)=g-g=0$, so $y_h:=y-y_p$ is homogeneous and $y=y_p+y_h$. ∎

Moves used: set equality by two inclusions

The proof in one piece

Differentiation is linear, so $L(c_1y_1+c_2y_2)=c_1L(y_1)+c_2L(y_2)$; this proves 1 and 3. For 2: if $L(y_h)=0$ then $L(y_p+y_h)=L(y_p)+L(y_h)=g$; and if $L(y)=g$ then $L(y-y_p)=0$, so $y=y_p+(y-y_p)$ has the claimed form. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why is the solution set of $y''+a_1y'+a_0y=g$ not a vector space when $g\neq0$?

    Answer

    $0$ is not a solution ($L(0)=0\neq g$). It is an affine space: a vector space shifted by $y_p$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Does superposition work for $y'=y^2$?

    Answer

    No: $y'=y^2$ is not linear. $y=-\frac1x$ is a solution on $(0,\infty)$, but $2y=-\frac2x$ has $(2y)'=\frac2{x^2}\neq(2y)^2=\frac4{x^2}$.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Proof D: all solutions of $y''+a_1y'+a_0y=0$ (Section 8.5.4, p. 401–402, and Wichtige Übung 8.66)

Theorem. Let $$p(T)=T^2+a_1T+a_0=(T-\alpha_1)(T-\alpha_2)$$with $\alpha_1,\alpha_2\in\mathbb C$. The solutions $y:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C$ of $y''+a_1y'+a_0y=0$ are exactly

  • $y=C_1e^{\alpha_1x}+C_2e^{\alpha_2x}$ if $\alpha_1\neq\alpha_2$,
  • $y=(C_1+C_2x)e^{\alpha x}$ if $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha$ (double root),

with constants $C_1,C_2\in\mathbb C$. If $a_0,a_1$ are real and $\alpha_{1,2}=\beta\pm i\gamma$ with $\gamma\neq0$, the real solutions are exactly $y=e^{\beta x}\bigl(C_1\cos(\gamma x)+C_2\sin(\gamma x)\bigr)$, $C_1,C_2\in\mathbb R$.

You'll need this
  • Proof B with complex constants: for $\alpha\in\mathbb C$, the solutions of $z'=\alpha z+h(x)$ are found the same way, with homogeneous solutions $Ae^{\alpha x}$ and then variation of constants: $z=A(x)e^{\alpha x}$ gives $A'(x)=h(x)e^{-\alpha x}$. (A complex function with derivative $0$ on an interval is constant: apply the real result to real and imaginary parts.)

The idea. Factor the equation like the polynomial. With $a_1=-(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)$ and $a_0=\alpha_1\alpha_2$, set $z=y'-\alpha_2y$. Then $z'-\alpha_1z=y''+a_1y'+a_0y$. So a second-order equation is two first-order equations in a row, and Proof B solves each: first $z$, then $y$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Two inclusions: the given functions are solutions; every solution is of that form.
  • Cases: $\alpha_1\neq\alpha_2$ and $\alpha_1=\alpha_2$; then the real form.
The storyWhat you write

Split the second-order rails into two first-order rails.

Let $y$ be a solution and $z=y'-\alpha_2y$. Using $a_1=-(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)$, $a_0=\alpha_1\alpha_2$:

$$\begin{aligned}z'-\alpha_1z&=y''-\alpha_2y'-\alpha_1y'+\alpha_1\alpha_2y\\&=y''+a_1y'+a_0y=0.\end{aligned}$$

The first rail is easy.

By Proof B (with $f=-\alpha_1$), $z=Ae^{\alpha_1x}$ for a constant $A$. So $y'-\alpha_2y=Ae^{\alpha_1x}$, i.e. $\bigl(ye^{-\alpha_2x}\bigr)'=Ae^{(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)x}$.

The second rail depends on whether the two roots differ.

If $\alpha_1\neq\alpha_2$: $ye^{-\alpha_2x}=\frac A{\alpha_1-\alpha_2}e^{(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)x}+C_2$, so $y=C_1e^{\alpha_1x}+C_2e^{\alpha_2x}$ with $C_1=\frac A{\alpha_1-\alpha_2}$. If $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha$: $(ye^{-\alpha x})'=A$, so $ye^{-\alpha x}=Ax+C_1$ and $y=(C_1+C_2x)e^{\alpha x}$ with $C_2=A$.

And every such journey really follows the rails.

Conversely, $e^{\alpha_ix}$ solves the equation since $p(\alpha_i)=0$. For a double root, $y=xe^{\alpha x}$ has $$y''+a_1y'+a_0y=\bigl(p(\alpha)x+p'(\alpha)\bigr)e^{\alpha x}=0,$$as $p(\alpha)=p'(\alpha)=0$. By Proof C, all the combinations solve it.

For real rails, take real and imaginary parts.

Real form: $e^{(\beta\pm i\gamma)x}=e^{\beta x}(\cos\gamma x\pm i\sin\gamma x)$, so the span of $e^{\alpha_1x},e^{\alpha_2x}$ equals the span of $e^{\beta x}\cos\gamma x$ and $e^{\beta x}\sin\gamma x$. If $a_0,a_1$ are real and $y$ is a real solution, it is a complex combination of these two, and taking real parts of $y=C_1e^{\beta x}\cos\gamma x+C_2e^{\beta x}\sin\gamma x$ shows that real $C_1,C_2$ suffice. ∎

Moves used: split into cases set equality by two inclusions

The proof in one piece

Write $a_1=-(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)$, $a_0=\alpha_1\alpha_2$. Let $y$ be a solution and $z=y'-\alpha_2y$; then $z'-\alpha_1z=y''+a_1y'+a_0y=0$, so $z=Ae^{\alpha_1x}$ by Proof B. Hence $(ye^{-\alpha_2x})'=(y'-\alpha_2y)e^{-\alpha_2x}=Ae^{(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)x}$. If $\alpha_1\neq\alpha_2$, integrating gives $ye^{-\alpha_2x}=\frac A{\alpha_1-\alpha_2}e^{(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)x}+C_2$, so $y=C_1e^{\alpha_1x}+C_2e^{\alpha_2x}$. If $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha$, then $ye^{-\alpha x}=Ax+C_1$, so $y=(C_1+C_2x)e^{\alpha x}$. Conversely, $L(e^{\alpha x})=p(\alpha)e^{\alpha x}$ and $L(xe^{\alpha x})=(p(\alpha)x+p'(\alpha))e^{\alpha x}$ show that these functions are solutions when $\alpha$ is a root (a double root, for $xe^{\alpha x}$), and by superposition so are their combinations. For real coefficients and $\alpha_{1,2}=\beta\pm i\gamma$, Euler's formula shows that $e^{\alpha_1x},e^{\alpha_2x}$ and $e^{\beta x}\cos\gamma x,e^{\beta x}\sin\gamma x$ span the same space; a real solution equals its own real part, so it is a real combination of the latter. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Check $L(xe^{\alpha x})=\bigl(p(\alpha)x+p'(\alpha)\bigr)e^{\alpha x}$.

    Answer

    $y=xe^{\alpha x}$: $y'=(1+\alpha x)e^{\alpha x}$, $y''=(2\alpha+\alpha^2x)e^{\alpha x}$. Sum: $$\bigl((\alpha^2+a_1\alpha+a_0)x+(2\alpha+a_1)\bigr)e^{\alpha x},$$and $p'(\alpha)=2\alpha+a_1$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Deduce: an initial value problem $y(x_0)=y_0$, $y'(x_0)=y_1$ has exactly one solution.

    Answer

    For distinct roots, the conditions give the linear system $C_1e^{\alpha_1x_0}+C_2e^{\alpha_2x_0}=y_0$, $\alpha_1C_1e^{\alpha_1x_0}+\alpha_2C_2e^{\alpha_2x_0}=y_1$, with determinant $(\alpha_2-\alpha_1)e^{(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)x_0}\neq0$; similarly for a double root. So exactly one $(C_1,C_2)$.

Worked example 4: homogeneous second-order equations

  1. $y''-y'=0$ and $y''=0$.

    Answer

    $p(T)=T^2-T=T(T-1)$, roots $0,1$: $y=C_1+C_2e^x$. And $p(T)=T^2$, double root $0$: $y=C_1+C_2x$ (as expected).

  2. $y''+y=0$ with $y(0)=0$, $y'(0)=1$.

    Answer

    Roots $\pm i$ ($\beta=0$, $\gamma=1$): $y=C_1\cos x+C_2\sin x$. $y(0)=C_1=0$, $y'(0)=C_2=1$: $y=\sin x$.

  3. $y''-2y'+y=0$.

    Answer

    $p(T)=(T-1)^2$, double root $1$: $y=(C_1+C_2x)e^x$.

  4. $y''+2y'+5y=0$.

    Answer

    Roots $-1\pm2i$ (the recap): $y=e^{-x}\bigl(C_1\cos2x+C_2\sin2x\bigr)$. A damped oscillation.

  5. The spring $\ddot y+d\dot y+ky=0$ ($d\ge0$ friction, $k>0$ stiffness): what do the three cases mean?

    Answer

    Roots $-\frac d2\pm\sqrt{\frac{d^2}4-k}$. Weak friction ($\frac{d^2}4<k$): complex roots, $e^{-dt/2}(C_1\cos\gamma t+C_2\sin\gamma t)$, a dying oscillation. Strong friction ($\frac{d^2}4>k$): two negative real roots, the spring creeps back without swinging, like a door closer. The borderline is the double root $-\frac d2$: $(C_1+C_2t)e^{-dt/2}$.

Why it's cool. The same equation $\ddot y+d\dot y+ky=0$ describes a weight on a spring, a swinging pendulum (for small swings), the current in an electric circuit with a coil, a resistor and a capacitor, and the swaying of a tall building in the wind. Whether the motion oscillates, dies down slowly or creeps back is decided by one thing: whether a quadratic polynomial has real or complex roots. The complex numbers, which looked like an algebra trick, are exactly the oscillations of the real world.

6. Second order: finding a particular solution

By Proof C, the inhomogeneous equation $y''+a_1y'+a_0y=g$ needs one solution $y_p$; then add the homogeneous solutions from Proof D. For the forcing terms in exams (polynomials, exponentials, $\sin$, $\cos$ and products), you guess the shape and fit the coefficients.

Resonance rule: if the exponent ($0$ for a polynomial, $\alpha$, or $i\gamma$) is a root of $p$ of multiplicity $\ell$, multiply the Ansatz by $x^\ell$. Then put the Ansatz into the equation and compare coefficients.

Worked example 5: particular solutions and initial values

  1. The general solution of $y''-y=e^x$ (Kobel-Keller HS24, question 13, 3 points).

    Answer

    Homogeneous: roots $\pm1$, $C_1e^x+C_2e^{-x}$. The forcing $e^x$ has $\alpha=1$, a simple root: resonance, Ansatz $y_p=Axe^x$. Then $y_p'=A(1+x)e^x$, $y_p''=A(2+x)e^x$, and $y_p''-y_p=2Ae^x=e^x$, so $A=\frac12$:

    $$y=C_1e^x+C_2e^{-x}+\frac x2e^x.$$

    (With $y_p=Ae^x$ you'd get $0=e^x$: that's the sign you forgot the resonance rule.)

  2. Solve $y''-y=-x$, $y(0)=y'(0)=0$ (2019 winter exam, question 2, 3 points).

    Answer

    $0$ is not a root, so the Ansatz is $y_p=ax+b$: $-(ax+b)=-x$ gives $a=1$, $b=0$. General: $y=x+C_1e^x+C_2e^{-x}$. Conditions: $C_1+C_2=0$ and $1+C_1-C_2=0$, so $C_1=-\frac12$, $C_2=\frac12$, and $y=x-\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}2=x-\sinh x$.

  3. $u''+4u=1+4x$, $u(0)=0$, $u'(0)=1$. Find $u(\pi)$. (Figalli FS24, MC7, 2 points.)

    Answer

    Homogeneous: roots $\pm2i$, $A\sin2x+B\cos2x$. Particular: $u_p=ax+b$, $4(ax+b)=1+4x$, so $u_p=x+\frac14$. Then $u(0)=B+\frac14=0$ and $u'(0)=2A+1=1$, so $A=0$, $B=-\frac14$:

    $$u=x+\frac14-\frac14\cos2x,\qquad u(\pi)=\pi.$$

  4. Which Ansatz for $y''+y=x\sin x$? (Script's example.)

    Answer

    $i$ is a simple root of $T^2+1$, and the polynomial factor has degree 1: $$\begin{aligned}y_p&=x\bigl((ax+b)\cos x+(cx+d)\sin x\bigr)\end{aligned},$$four coefficients to fit.

7. Worked examples: which type?

A mixed set to revise the whole lesson before the exercises. In the exam, the first and most important step is to recognise the type.

The decision list for “solve this differential equation”

  1. Second order with constants in front of $y'',y',y$? Characteristic polynomial (Proof D), Ansatz for the forcing term (section 6), then initial values.
  2. First order, linear ($y$ and $y'$ only to the first power)? Normal form $y'+f(x)y=g(x)$, solve the homogeneous equation, then vary the constant (Proof B).
  3. First order, right side factors as $f(x)g(y)$? Separate (Proof A); find the maximal interval.
  4. Neither? The question will give a hint, usually a substitution like $u=\frac yx$ that turns it into one of these types.
  5. Multiple choice? Often you can save time: check each option against the equation and the initial values.

Worked example 6: recognise, then solve

  1. The general solution of $(x^2+1)y'=y$ (2019 winter exam, 1d, 1 point).

    Answer

    Linear (and separable): $y'-\frac1{1+x^2}y=0$, $f=-\frac1{1+x^2}$, $F=-\arctan x$. So $y=Ce^{\arctan x}$.

  2. Solve $y'=2y+\sin x$, $y(0)=\frac12$ (2018 winter exam, question 3, 3 points).

    Answer

    Linear with constant coefficient: homogeneous $Ce^{2x}$. Particular: Ansatz $a\sin x+b\cos x$ (the method of section 6 works for first order too): $$\begin{aligned}&a\cos x-b\sin x\\&\quad=2a\sin x+2b\cos x+\sin x,\end{aligned}$$so $a=2b$ and $-b=2a+1$, giving $b=-\frac15$, $a=-\frac25$. Then $y(0)=C-\frac15=\frac12$, $C=\frac7{10}$:

    $$y=\frac7{10}e^{2x}-\frac{2\sin x+\cos x}5.$$

  3. Solve $y''+y'-6y=1$, $y(0)=y'(0)=0$ (2017 summer exam, question 3, 3 points).

    Answer

    $$p(T)=T^2+T-6=(T+3)(T-2).$$Particular: constant $y_p=-\frac16$. So $y=C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{-3x}-\frac16$, with $C_1+C_2=\frac16$ and $2C_1-3C_2=0$: $C_1=\frac1{10}$, $C_2=\frac1{15}$.

    $$y=\frac{e^{2x}}{10}+\frac{e^{-3x}}{15}-\frac16.$$

  4. Find the general solution of $y'=\frac{y+\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}x$ for $x>0$, using $u=\frac yx$ (Einsiedler FS22, question 3, 4 points).

    Answer

    $y=xu$, $y'=u+xu'$, and the right side is $u+\sqrt{1+u^2}$ (as $x>0$). So $xu'=\sqrt{1+u^2}$: separable. $\int\frac{du}{\sqrt{1+u^2}}=\int\frac{dx}x$ gives $\operatorname{arsinh}u=\log x+c$, so $$u=\sinh(\log x+c)=\frac12\left(Ax-\frac1{Ax}\right)$$with $A=e^c>0$. Hence

    $$y=xu=\frac12\left(Ax^2-\frac1A\right),\qquad A>0.$$

  5. $y''+4y'+4y=0$, $y(0)=1$, $y'(0)=0$.

    Answer

    Double root $-2$: $y=(C_1+C_2x)e^{-2x}$. $C_1=1$, and $y'(0)=C_2-2C_1=0$ gives $C_2=2$: $y=(1+2x)e^{-2x}$.

Exercise 1 (PVK mock HS25, MC6, 2 points)

$u'(x)=\frac2xu(x)+x$, $u(1)=0$. Find $u(2)$. (A) $4\log2$ (B) $2\log2$ (C) $2$ (D) $\log2$

Solution

(A). Normal form $u'-\frac2xu=x$ on $(0,\infty)$: $F=-2\log x$, so $u_{\text{hom}}=Ax^2$. Vary the constant: $u=A(x)x^2$ gives $A'x^2=x$, so $A'=\frac1x$ and $A(x)=\log x$. Hence $u=x^2(\log x+A)$, and $u(1)=0$ gives $A=0$. So $u=x^2\log x$ and $u(2)=4\log2$.

Exercise 2 (Figalli FS26, MC12, 4 points)

Open the original: FS26 exam

$u'=-\frac2xu+x$, $u(1)=\frac14$. Find $u(2)$. (A) $1$ (B) $\frac12$ (C) $\frac14$ (D) $2$

Solution

(A). $u'+\frac2xu=x$; $F=2\log x$, so $u_{\text{hom}}=\frac A{x^2}$. Vary the constant: $u=\frac{A(x)}{x^2}$ gives $\frac{A'}{x^2}=x$, so $A'=x^3$ and $A(x)=\frac{x^4}4$. Hence $u=\frac1{x^2}\left(\frac{x^4}4+A\right)$, and $u(1)=\frac14$ gives $A=0$, $u=\frac{x^2}4$, $u(2)=1$.

Exercise 3 (PVK mock HS25, MC7, 2 points)

Open the original: PVK mock exam · solutions

$u''-u=e^x$, $u(0)=0$, $u'(0)=1$. Find $u(1)$. (A) $\frac{3e^2-1}{4e}$ (B) $\frac{3e}4$ (C) $\frac{4e}3$ (D) $4e$

(A). By Worked example 5, $u=C_1e^x+C_2e^{-x}+\frac x2e^x$. $u(0)=C_1+C_2=0$, $u'(0)=C_1-C_2+\frac12=1$. So $C_1=\frac14$, $C_2=-\frac14$, and

Exercise 4 (Felder FS23, 1.A7, 4 points)

Open the original: FS23 exam with solutions

Find the solution $y:I\to\mathbb R$ of the initial value problem $x^2y'=y^2$, $y(1)=2$ (2 points), and determine the maximal interval $I$ where it is defined (2 points).

Solution

Near $x=1$, $x\neq0$ and $y\neq0$, so separate: $\int\frac{dy}{y^2}=\int\frac{dx}{x^2}$, i.e. $-\frac1y=-\frac1x+C$. At $x=1$: $-\frac12=-1+C$, so $C=\frac12$ and

$$\begin{aligned}&\frac1y=\frac1x-\frac12=\frac{2-x}{2x}\\&y(x)=\frac{2x}{2-x}\end{aligned}.$$

Check: $y'=\frac{2(2-x)+2x}{(2-x)^2}=\frac4{(2-x)^2}$, so $x^2y'=\frac{4x^2}{(2-x)^2}=y^2$ for every $x\neq2$, including $x=0$. The formula is undefined at $x=2$ (the solution is unbounded near it), so the maximal interval containing $1$ is $I=(-\infty,2)$ (the official answer).

(a) $u'=-\sin(x)u+\sin(x)$, $u(0)=0$. Find $u(\pi)$. (A) $1-e$ (B) $1+e^{-1}$ (C) $1-e^{-2}$ (D) $e^{-1}$

Exercise 7 (Felder HS22, 3.MC6, 2 points)

Open the original: HS22 exam

The differential equation $y'=y\cos(x^2)+x$ for $y:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ has (A) exactly one solution (B) uncountably many solutions (C) exactly two solutions (D) countably infinitely many solutions.

Solution

(B). It is linear with continuous coefficients: $y'-\cos(x^2)y=x$. By Proof B, the solutions are $e^{-F}(G+C)$ with one solution for each $C\in\mathbb R$ (equivalently, one for each initial value $y(0)\in\mathbb R$), and different $C$ give different functions. Bonus: resonance (the script's example)

Solve $\ddot y+y=\sin t$, $y(0)=\dot y(0)=0$: a spring without friction, pushed exactly in its own rhythm. Is the solution bounded?

Solution

Homogeneous: $A\sin t+B\cos t$. The forcing has $i$, a simple root of $T^2+1$: resonance, Ansatz $y_p=t(a\cos t+b\sin t)$. Then $\ddot y_p+y_p=-2a\sin t+2b\cos t$, so $a=-\frac12$, $b=0$: $y_p=-\frac t2\cos t$. With the initial values ($B=0$, $A-\frac12=0$):

$$y(t)=\frac12\sin t-\frac t2\cos t.$$

Unbounded: at $t=2\pi n$ it is $-\pi n$. A tiny push in the right rhythm makes the swing grow without limit. This is resonance: why soldiers break step on bridges, and why one guitar string can make another one sing.

9. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Euler's rails. How does a computer follow the rails when there's no formula? It takes small steps: from $(x,y)$, walk a distance $h$ along the current rail, $y_{\text{new}}=y+h\,f(x,y)$. That turns the differential equation into a difference equation, the discrete version in the script's Section 8.5.1. For $y'=y$, $y(0)=1$, $n$ steps of size $\frac1n$ give $\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n$ at $x=1$: the sequence from lesson 16, which converges to $e$. So the oldest formula for $e$ is Euler's method on the simplest differential equation.

Can carts part ways? Worked example 1 showed they can, for $y'=2\sqrt{|y|}$. The culprit is the square root's infinitely steep start at $0$. In the second semester, the Picard–Lindelöf theorem shows that if $f(x,y)$ is a little nicer (Lipschitz in $y$), the cart's journey is uniquely decided by its start. Its proof is a fixed-point argument: the 2017 summer exam already asks about it.
