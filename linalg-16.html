1. The story: the two censuses

The Free City keeps its tax records in a great ledger: one line for every household, one column for every kind of goods. Two clerks are sent to count how much the ledger really knows. The first clerk reads it line by line and asks how many households are genuinely different, not just mixtures of others. The second reads it column by column and asks how many kinds of goods are genuinely different.

The ledger can be tall and narrow or short and wide; the lines live in one space and the columns in another. Nobody expects the two clerks to agree. Yet they always do. This lesson explains why, three times: once with the clerks' everyday tool, the tidying of the ledger by row operations (Gaussian elimination); once with the mapmakers' normal form from lesson 15; and once with the surveyors of surveyors from lesson 14.

The tidying also has a practical gift. One elimination tells the clerks at the same time which households are independent, which columns are independent, and which combinations of columns cancel out completely.

In mathematics: for $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ the rows span a subspace of $K^n$ and the columns span a subspace of $K^m$. Their dimensions, the row rank and the column rank, are equal.

2. Row space and column space

Definition (row and column space, row and column rank; script Definitions 2.3.22 and 2.3.25, pp. 59–60). Let $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ with rows $u_1,\dots,u_m\in K^n$ and columns $v_1,\dots,v_n\in K^m$.

  • $\mathrm{RowS}(A):=\mathrm{Sp}(u_1,\dots,u_m)\subseteq K^n$ is the row space, and $\mathrm{row\,rank}(A):=\dim\mathrm{RowS}(A)$.
  • $\mathrm{ColS}(A):=\mathrm{Sp}(v_1,\dots,v_n)\subseteq K^m$ is the column space, and $\mathrm{col\,rank}(A):=\dim\mathrm{ColS}(A)$.

By lesson 15, Proof A, $\mathrm{ColS}(A)=\mathrm{Im}(m_A)$ (lesson 15), so $\mathrm{col\,rank}(A)=\mathrm{rank}(A)$. The transpose $A^T$ (script Definition 2.3.30, p. 61) swaps the two: $\mathrm{RowS}(A)=\mathrm{ColS}(A^T)$ and $\mathrm{ColS}(A)=\mathrm{RowS}(A^T)$.

Proof A: row operations do not change the row space (script Lemma 2.3.23, p. 59)

Claim. If $B\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ comes from $A$ by row operations, then $\mathrm{RowS}(A)=\mathrm{RowS}(B)$.

The idea. It suffices to check one operation at a time. Each new row is a combination of the old rows, so the new span lies in the old span; and each operation can be undone by another row operation, so the old span lies in the new one.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “It suffices to treat one row operation; let $u_1,\dots,u_m$ be the rows of $A$.”
  • Last line: “so both lists span the same subspace of $K^n$.”
  • The gap: each list consists of linear combinations of the other list (lesson 6: then the spans are equal).
The storyWhat you write

Reordering the lines of the ledger changes nothing.

$L_i\leftrightarrow L_j$: the list of rows is the same list in another order, so the span is the same.

A line written in another currency still says the same.

$\lambda L_i\to L_i$ with $\lambda\ne0$: $\lambda u_i\in\mathrm{Sp}(u_1,\dots,u_m)$, and $u_i=\lambda^{-1}(\lambda u_i)\in\mathrm{Sp}(u_1,\dots,\lambda u_i,\dots,u_m)$.

Adding one household's record to another's can be undone by subtracting it again.

$L_i+\lambda L_j\to L_i$ ($i\ne j$): $u_i+\lambda u_j$ is a combination of $u_1,\dots,u_m$; conversely $u_i=(u_i+\lambda u_j)-\lambda u_j$ is a combination of the new rows, since $u_j$ is still one of them.

So no tidying step changes what the ledger knows.

In each case every new row lies in the old span and every old row in the new span, so the spans are equal (lesson 6). By induction on the number of operations, $\mathrm{RowS}(A)=\mathrm{RowS}(B)$. ∎

Moves used: to show $x\in S$: bring $x$ to the form that defines $S$ undo the row operation

The proof in one piece

It suffices to treat one operation. A swap only reorders the rows. For $\lambda L_i\to L_i$ ($\lambda\ne0$): $\lambda u_i$ is a combination of the old rows and $u_i=\lambda^{-1}(\lambda u_i)$ of the new ones. For $L_i+\lambda L_j\to L_i$ ($i\ne j$): $u_i+\lambda u_j$ is a combination of the old rows and $u_i=(u_i+\lambda u_j)-\lambda u_j$ of the new ones. So each list lies in the span of the other, and the spans agree. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Reduce to one operation.
  2. New rows are combinations of old rows: “⊆”.
  3. Undo the operation: “⊇”.

In short: row operations are reversible, and each step only mixes rows, so the row space stays. Caution: the column space usually changes. For $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, the operation $L_2-L_1\to L_2$ gives $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, and $\mathrm{ColS}$ moves from $\mathrm{Sp}((1,1)^T)$ to $\mathrm{Sp}((1,0)^T)$. Only its dimension survives (Proof C).

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Why must $\lambda\ne0$ in the second operation?
    Answer

    With $\lambda=0$ the row $u_i$ becomes $0$ and cannot be recovered: the row space can shrink. For $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}$, “$0\cdot L_1$” gives the zero matrix with row space $\{0\}$.

  2. Rung 2. Show: $Ax=0$ and $Bx=0$ have the same solutions when $B$ comes from $A$ by row operations.
    Answer

    $x$ solves $Ax=0$ iff every row $u$ of $A$ satisfies $u\cdot x=0$. If this holds for all rows of $A$, it holds for every combination of them, hence for all rows of $B$ (they lie in $\mathrm{RowS}(A)$). By symmetry (the rows of $A$ lie in $\mathrm{RowS}(B)$) the converse holds. This is the fact Proof C builds on.

3. Row echelon form: where both ranks can be read off

Definition (row echelon form; pivots). A matrix $B\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ is in row echelon form if its zero rows are at the bottom and the first nonzero entry of each nonzero row, its pivot, stands strictly to the right of the pivot of the row above. If the nonzero rows are $u_1,\dots,u_r$, their pivots stand in columns $j_1<j_2<\dots<j_r$, the pivot columns. If moreover every pivot is $1$ and every pivot column has zeros apart from its pivot, $B$ is in reduced row echelon form. Gaussian elimination brings every matrix into (reduced) row echelon form by row operations.

Example: $B=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&0&-1\\0&0&1&2\\0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$ is reduced, with $r=2$ and pivot columns $j_1=1$, $j_2=3$.

Proof B: in a row echelon matrix, row rank = number of pivots = column rank (script Lemma 2.3.27, p. 60)

Claim. Let $B\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ be in row echelon form with nonzero rows $u_1,\dots,u_r$ and pivot columns $j_1<\dots<j_r$. Then (1) $u_1,\dots,u_r$ is a basis of $\mathrm{RowS}(B)$; (2) the pivot columns $c_{j_1},\dots,c_{j_r}$ of $B$ are a basis of $\mathrm{ColS}(B)$. In particular $\mathrm{row\,rank}(B)=\mathrm{col\,rank}(B)=r$.

The idea. The staircase shape makes independence visible. For the rows, look at the pivot positions from left to right: at column $j_1$ only $u_1$ is nonzero, so its coefficient must vanish, then at $j_2$, and so on. For the columns, read from the bottom up: in row $r$ only the last pivot column is nonzero. Spanning is free for the rows (the other rows are zero); for the columns we count dimensions.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (1) First line: “Let $\alpha_1u_1+\dots+\alpha_ru_r=0$.” Last line: “so all $\alpha_i=0$; and the rows below $u_r$ are zero, so $u_1,\dots,u_r$ span $\mathrm{RowS}(B)$.”
  • (2) First line: “Every column has zeros below row $r$, so $\mathrm{ColS}(B)\subseteq K^r\times\{0\}^{m-r}$.” Last line: “$r$ independent vectors in this $r$-dimensional space are a basis of it, so it equals $\mathrm{ColS}(B)$.”
  • The gap: the staircase: entry $(i,j)$ of $B$ is $0$ whenever $j<j_i$.
The storyWhat you write

At the first pivot, only the first household has goods of that kind.

(1) Let $\sum_{i=1}^r\alpha_iu_i=0$, and write $b_{ij}$ for the entries. For $i\ge2$ we have $j_1<j_i$, so $b_{ij_1}=0$. The $j_1$-th entry of the sum is therefore $\alpha_1b_{1j_1}=0$, and $b_{1j_1}\ne0$ gives $\alpha_1=0$.

Then the second pivot, and so on down the stairs.

Induction: if $\alpha_1=\dots=\alpha_k=0$ ($k<r$), the $j_{k+1}$-th entry of the sum is $\alpha_{k+1}b_{k+1,j_{k+1}}$ (rows $i>k+1$ vanish there because $j_{k+1}<j_i$), so $\alpha_{k+1}=0$. Hence $u_1,\dots,u_r$ are independent; the other rows are $0$, so they span $\mathrm{RowS}(B)$.

For the columns, start at the bottom step.

(2) Let $\sum_{k=1}^r\beta_kc_{j_k}=0$. In row $r$, the columns $j_1,\dots,j_{r-1}$ have entry $0$ (they lie left of the pivot $j_r$), so row $r$ of the sum is $\beta_rb_{rj_r}=0$ and $\beta_r=0$. Going up row by row in the same way, $\beta_{r-1}=\dots=\beta_1=0$.

Count the room: there is space for exactly $r$ kinds of goods, and we have found $r$.

Rows $r+1,\dots,m$ of $B$ are zero, so $\mathrm{ColS}(B)\subseteq K^r\times\{0\}^{m-r}$, a space of dimension $r$. The $r$ pivot columns are independent and lie in it, so they are a basis of it (lesson 7, equilibrium). Hence $\mathrm{ColS}(B)=K^r\times\{0\}^{m-r}$ with basis $c_{j_1},\dots,c_{j_r}$. ∎

Moves used: induction: base, hypothesis, step count first: $n$ vectors in an $n$-dimensional space

The proof in one piece

(1) Let $\sum_i\alpha_iu_i=0$. Since $b_{ij}=0$ for $j<j_i$, the $j_1$-th entry of the sum is $\alpha_1b_{1j_1}$, so $\alpha_1=0$; inductively, once $\alpha_1=\dots=\alpha_k=0$, the $j_{k+1}$-th entry is $\alpha_{k+1}b_{k+1,j_{k+1}}$, so $\alpha_{k+1}=0$. The remaining rows are zero, so $u_1,\dots,u_r$ is a basis of $\mathrm{RowS}(B)$. (2) Let $\sum_k\beta_kc_{j_k}=0$. Row $r$ gives $\beta_rb_{rj_r}=0$, so $\beta_r=0$, and upwards $\beta_{r-1}=\dots=\beta_1=0$. The pivot columns are $r$ independent vectors in $K^r\times\{0\}^{m-r}\supseteq\mathrm{ColS}(B)$, which has dimension $r$; so they are a basis of it, and $\mathrm{ColS}(B)$ equals it. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Rows: compare entries at the pivot columns, left to right.
  2. Columns: compare entries row by row, bottom to top.
  3. Columns span: count, $\mathrm{ColS}(B)\subseteq K^r\times\{0\}$.

In short: for a staircase both ranks are the number of steps. The script then defines $\mathrm{rank}(A)$ as the number of pivots of any row echelon form of $A$, and this number does not depend on how you eliminate: by Proof A and (1), the number of pivots is $\dim\mathrm{RowS}(A)$.

4. Row operations keep the column relations: the Gauss proof

Row operations change the column space (Proof A, caution), so Proof B does not yet say anything about $\mathrm{ColS}(A)$. The script's “magic” (Example 2.3.33) is that they keep something better: every linear relation between the columns.

Proof C: column rank = row rank, by Gaussian elimination (script Satz 2.3.32 and Corollary 2.3.34, pp. 62–63)

Claim. Let $B$ come from $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ by row operations, with columns $a_1,\dots,a_n$ and $b_1,\dots,b_n$.

  1. For all $x\in K^n$: $\;x_1a_1+\dots+x_na_n=0\iff x_1b_1+\dots+x_nb_n=0$.
  2. If $B$ is in row echelon form with pivot columns $j_1,\dots,j_r$, then $a_{j_1},\dots,a_{j_r}$ (the columns of the original $A$) are a basis of $\mathrm{ColS}(A)$.
  3. Hence $\mathrm{col\,rank}(A)=r=\mathrm{row\,rank}(A)$.

The idea. A relation between the columns is a solution of $Ax=0$, because $Ax=\sum_jx_ja_j$; and row operations do not change the solutions (Rung 2 of Proof A). So $A$ and $B$ have exactly the same column relations. “Independent” and “is a combination of” are statements about relations, so they transfer from $B$, where Proof B decides them, back to $A$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (1) First line: “$\sum_jx_ja_j=Ax$ and $\sum_jx_jb_j=Bx$.” Last line: “so $Ax=0\iff Bx=0$.”
  • (2) First line: “Let $\sum_k\beta_ka_{j_k}=0$.” Then: “Let $a_l$ be any column; we write it as a combination of $a_{j_1},\dots,a_{j_r}$.”
  • The gap: translate each statement into a vector $x$ with $Ax=0$, move it to $B$ by (1), use Proof B there, move back.
The storyWhat you write

A recipe that cancels the columns of the ledger cancels them after tidying too, and the other way round.

(1) $\sum_jx_ja_j=Ax$ and $\sum_jx_jb_j=Bx$. Each row of $B$ is a combination of rows of $A$, so $Ax=0$ implies $Bx=0$; each row of $A$ is a combination of rows of $B$ (Proof A: the row spaces agree), so $Bx=0$ implies $Ax=0$.

The pivot columns of the tidy ledger are independent, so the same columns of the original ledger are.

(2) Let $\sum_k\beta_ka_{j_k}=0$. This is $Ax=0$ for the $x$ with $x_{j_k}=\beta_k$ and all other entries $0$. By (1), $Bx=0$, i.e. $\sum_k\beta_kb_{j_k}=0$, and by Proof B (2) all $\beta_k=0$.

Every other column of the tidy ledger is a mixture of the pivot columns, with the same recipe in the original.

Let $l\in\{1,\dots,n\}$. By Proof B (2), $b_l=\sum_k\gamma_kb_{j_k}$ for some $\gamma_k\in K$. So $Bx=0$ for $x=e_l-\sum_k\gamma_ke_{j_k}$, hence $Ax=0$ by (1), i.e. $a_l=\sum_k\gamma_ka_{j_k}$. So $a_{j_1},\dots,a_{j_r}$ span $\mathrm{ColS}(A)$, and they are a basis.

Both clerks count $r$.

(3) $\mathrm{col\,rank}(A)=r$ by (2), and $\mathrm{row\,rank}(A)=\mathrm{row\,rank}(B)=r$ by Proof A and Proof B (1). ∎

Moves used: row operations keep the column relations undo the row operation

The proof in one piece

(1) $\sum_jx_ja_j=Ax$, $\sum_jx_jb_j=Bx$, and the rows of $A$ and of $B$ span the same space (Proof A), so $Ax=0\iff Bx=0$. (2) If $\sum_k\beta_ka_{j_k}=0$, then by (1) $\sum_k\beta_kb_{j_k}=0$, so all $\beta_k=0$ by Proof B. For any $l$, Proof B gives $b_l=\sum_k\gamma_kb_{j_k}$; applying (1) to $x=e_l-\sum_k\gamma_ke_{j_k}$ gives $a_l=\sum_k\gamma_ka_{j_k}$. So $a_{j_1},\dots,a_{j_r}$ is a basis of $\mathrm{ColS}(A)$. (3) Hence $\mathrm{col\,rank}(A)=r=\mathrm{row\,rank}(B)=\mathrm{row\,rank}(A)$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Column relation $=$ solution of $Ax=0$; same solutions after row operations.
  2. Independence and “is a combination of” are relations: transfer them from $B$ to $A$.
  3. Count: $r$ pivot columns of $A$, $r$ nonzero rows of $B$.

In short: the pivot columns of the original matrix are a basis of its column space; the nonzero rows of the echelon form are a basis of the row space. Never mix them up: the pivot columns of $B$ are usually not in $\mathrm{ColS}(A)$ (Worked example 1, step 3).

Worked example 1: four spaces with one elimination (script Example 2.3.33)

Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}-3&6&-1&1\\1&-2&2&3\\2&-4&5&8\end{pmatrix}\in M_{3\times4}(\mathbb R)$. Find bases of $\mathrm{RowS}(A)$, $\mathrm{ColS}(A)$ and $\mathrm{Ker}(m_A)$, and the rank.

  1. Bring $A$ to reduced row echelon form.
    Answer

    $L_1\leftrightarrow L_2$; then $L_2+3L_1\to L_2$ and $L_3-2L_1\to L_3$ give rows $(1,-2,2,3)$, $(0,0,5,10)$, $(0,0,1,2)$. Then $L_2\leftrightarrow L_3$, $L_3-5L_2\to L_3$, $L_1-2L_2\to L_1$:

    $$B=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&0&-1\\0&0&1&2\\0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

    Two pivots, in columns $1$ and $3$: $\mathrm{rank}(A)=2$.

Proof D: column rank = row rank, by the normal form (script Lemma 3.4.4 and Satz 3.4.1, pp. 95–97)

Claim. (1) Let $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$, $P\in\mathrm{GL}_m(K)$ and $Q\in\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$. Then $\mathrm{col\,rank}(PAQ)=\mathrm{col\,rank}(A)$ and $\mathrm{row\,rank}(PAQ)=\mathrm{row\,rank}(A)$. (2) Hence $\mathrm{col\,rank}(A)=\mathrm{row\,rank}(A)$ for every $A$.

The idea. Both ranks survive multiplication by invertible matrices: the column rank because invertible maps keep the dimension of an image, the row rank because transposing turns it into a column rank. And by lesson 15, every $A$ can be multiplied into $D_r$, where both ranks are visibly $r$.

Why it's cool

A matrix with a thousand rows and three columns has row rank at most three: a thousand equations about three unknowns can contain at most three independent pieces of information. Nobody would guess this from the rows alone; it is the column clerk who knows. Turned around, every big table of data that is “really” described by a few hidden factors has small rank, and the rank is the number of those factors. Recommendation systems and image compression start exactly there: they approximate a huge table by one of small rank, a sum of a few matrices $uv^T$ of rank $1$ (Exercise 4).

For wonder: the rank does not depend on the field (optional, 5 min)

A matrix with rational entries is also a real and a complex matrix. Could it have rank $2$ over $\mathbb Q$ and rank $3$ over $\mathbb C$, because complex coefficients find more relations? No: Gaussian elimination only ever adds, subtracts, multiplies and divides the entries, so it runs identically over $\mathbb Q$, $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb C$ and finds the same pivots. The rank is decided inside the smallest field that contains the entries. (Over a field of a different characteristic, such as $\mathbb F_2$, it can change: $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$ has rank $2$ over $\mathbb Q$ but rank $1$ over $\mathbb F_2$, where $-1=1$. The entries are then read differently, so it is really a different matrix.)

(b) “⇐”: column $j$ of $uv^T$ is $v_ju$. Since $v\ne0$, some column is a nonzero multiple of $u$, so $\mathrm{ColS}(uv^T)=\mathrm{Sp}(u)$ has dimension $1$. “⇒”: $\mathrm{ColS}(A)=\mathrm{Sp}(u)$ for some $u\ne0$, so every column is $a_j=v_ju$ for some $v_j\in K$; with $v=(v_1,\dots,v_n)^T$ this says $A=uv^T$, and $v\ne0$ because $A\ne0$.

(c) Write $PAQ=D_r$ (Proof D). Then $A=P^{-1}D_rQ^{-1}=\sum_{i=1}^rP^{-1}E_{ii}Q^{-1}$, and $P^{-1}E_{ii}Q^{-1}$ has rank $\mathrm{rank}(E_{ii})=1$ (Proof D (1)). By (a), $r$ is also the minimum number of rank-$1$ summands.

Exercise 5 (solvability of $Ax=b$ by ranks)

Let $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ and $b\in K^m$, and write $(A\,|\,b)$ for $A$ with the extra column $b$. Show: (a) $Ax=b$ has a solution if and only if $\mathrm{rank}(A\,|\,b)=\mathrm{rank}(A)$; (b) $Ax=b$ has a solution for every $b$ if and only if $\mathrm{rank}(A)=m$, if and only if the rows of $A$ are independent; (c) $Ax=b$ has at most one solution for every $b$ if and only if $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$, if and only if the columns of $A$ are independent.

Hint

$Ax=b$ is solvable iff $b\in\mathrm{ColS}(A)$. For (b) use row rank = column rank; for (c), the solutions form $x_0+\mathrm{Ker}(m_A)$ when nonempty.

Solution

Frame: translate each statement into column spaces and kernels; count. (Checked twice.)

(a) $Ax=\sum_jx_ja_j$, so $Ax=b$ is solvable iff $b\in\mathrm{ColS}(A)$ iff $\mathrm{ColS}(A\,|\,b)=\mathrm{ColS}(A)$. Since always $\mathrm{ColS}(A)\subseteq\mathrm{ColS}(A\,|\,b)$, equality holds iff the dimensions agree (lesson 8).

(b) Solvable for every $b$ iff $\mathrm{ColS}(A)=K^m$ iff $\mathrm{rank}(A)=m$. By row rank $=$ column rank, this means the $m$ rows span an $m$-dimensional space, i.e. they are independent.

(c) If $x_0$ is a solution, all solutions are $x_0+\mathrm{Ker}(m_A)$ (lesson 11). So “at most one” for every $b$ iff $\mathrm{Ker}(m_A)=\{0\}$ iff (rank–nullity) $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$ iff the $n$ columns span an $n$-dimensional space, i.e. they are independent.

Together: a square matrix has independent rows iff it has independent columns iff it is invertible. HS21, (XVII) (Worked example 4) is (c).

Exercise 6 (HS24, problem 4.A1–4.A2, 7 points)

Let $A\in M_{m\times n}(\mathbb R)$ with $m>n>0$ and $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$. (4.A1) Show that $A^TA\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb R)$ is invertible. (For real matrices the exam's adjoint $A^*$ is $A^T$.) (4.A2) Does this still hold over $\mathbb C$?

Hint

$A^TA$ is square, so it suffices that $\mathrm{Ker}(m_{A^TA})=\{0\}$ (lesson 15, Proof C). If $A^TAx=0$, multiply by $x^T$ from the left and recognise $\|Ax\|^2$. For (4.A2), what is $y^Ty$ for $y=(1,i)^T$?

Solution

Frame: square matrix: kernel zero suffices; the sum of squares trick. (Checked twice, no official solution. The scalar product is Linear Algebra II, but this argument uses only $y^Ty=y_1^2+\dots+y_m^2$.)

(4.A1) Let $x\in\mathbb R^n$ with $A^TAx=0$. Then $0=x^TA^TAx=(Ax)^T(Ax)=\sum_iy_i^2$ for $y=Ax$. A sum of real squares is $0$ only if every $y_i=0$, so $Ax=0$. Since $\mathrm{rank}(A)=n$, $m_A$ is injective (rank–nullity), so $x=0$. Hence $m_{A^TA}$ is injective, and as a square matrix $A^TA$ is invertible (lesson 15, Proof C).

(4.A2) With the adjoint $A^*=\bar A^T$ (conjugate transpose), yes: $x^*A^*Ax=\sum_i|y_i|^2$, and the same proof works. With the plain transpose, no: $A=\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix}$ has rank $1=n$, but $A^TA=1+i^2=0$.

Exercise 7 (script Corollary 10.5.9 at work)

Let $V,W$ be finite-dimensional and $T:V\to W$ linear. Using only $\dim\mathrm{Im}\,T^*=\dim\mathrm{Im}\,T$ (Proof E (1)) and rank–nullity, show: $T$ is injective iff $T^*$ is surjective, and $T$ is surjective iff $T^*$ is injective.

Hint

$T^*:W^*\to V^*$, and $\dim V^*=\dim V$, $\dim W^*=\dim W$. Surjective means rank $=$ dimension of the target; injective means rank $=$ dimension of the source.

Solution

Frame: translate both properties into ranks; compare. (Checked twice.)

$T^*$ surjective $\iff\dim\mathrm{Im}\,T^*=\dim V^*=\dim V\iff\dim\mathrm{Im}\,T=\dim V\iff\dim\mathrm{Ker}\,T=0$ (rank–nullity) $\iff T$ injective.

$T^*$ injective $\iff\dim\mathrm{Im}\,T^*=\dim W^*=\dim W$ (rank–nullity for $T^*$) $\iff\dim\mathrm{Im}\,T=\dim W\iff T$ surjective.

Lesson 14, Proof F (3), proved the same with annihilators; here it is a pure count. In matrices: $A$ has independent columns iff $A^T$ has spanning columns.
