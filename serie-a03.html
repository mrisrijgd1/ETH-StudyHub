Aufgabe 1 · Infimum, supremum, minimum and maximum

With $\mathbb N=\{1,2,3,\dots\}$, find the infimum and the supremum of the following subsets of $\mathbb R$. Do they have a minimum or a maximum?

  1. $A=\{a-b: a,b\in\mathbb R,$ $1<a<2,$ $3<b<4\}$
  2. $B=\left\{\frac n{n+1}: n\in\mathbb N\right\}$
  3. $C=\left\{\frac nm: m,n\in\mathbb N,\ m+n\le10\right\}$
The idea

The supremum is the lowest ceiling, the maximum is a ceiling that is itself in the set. So for each set: first guess the ceiling and the floor, then prove they are the lowest ceiling and highest floor, and finally ask whether they are attained. $A$ is an open interval (ends never reached). $B$ climbs towards $1$ but never gets there. $C$ is a finite set, and finite non-empty sets always have a min and a max.

$B$ starts at its minimum $\frac12$ and crowds towards $1$, which it never reaches (open dot).

You need

Maximum, bounds and supremum (lesson 6; script, page 105). The working test for “$s=\sup X$”: (1) $s$ is an upper bound, and (2) for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $x\in X$ with $x>s-\varepsilon$ (lesson 6, the exam recipe). For $B$ you need the Archimedean principle (script, page 111).

Hint 1

$A$: from $1<a<2$ and $3<b<4$, what range can $a-b$ have? Then show that every number in that range is reached. $B$: write $\frac n{n+1}=1-\frac1{n+1}$. $C$: what values can $m$ and $n$ take?

Hint 2

$A$: given $t\in(-3,-1)$, try $a=\frac{t+5}2$ and $b=\frac{5-t}2$. $B$: to show no number below $1$ is an upper bound, pick $n$ with $\frac1{n+1}<\varepsilon$. $C$: $1\le m,n\le9$.

Hint 3

Answers: $A=(-3,-1)$, so $\inf A=-3$, $\sup A=-1$, no min, no max. $B$: $\min B=\inf B=\frac12$, $\sup B=1$, no max. $C$: $\min C=\frac19$ at $(m,n)=(9,1)$, $\max C=9$ at $(m,n)=(1,9)$.

Solution
Setinfsupminmax
$A$$-3$$-1$nonenone
$B$$\frac12$$1$$\frac12$none
$C$$\frac19$$9$$\frac19$$9$
Why this stepWhat you write

First describe $A$ exactly. One inclusion: subtract the bounds.

1. From $1<a<2$ and $3<b<4$ it follows that $-3<a-b<-1$. So $A\subseteq(-3,-1)$.

Other inclusion: every $t$ in the interval is hit. Choose $a$ and $b$ symmetric around the middle values and check the bounds.

Conversely, let $t\in(-3,-1)$ and set $a:=\frac{t+5}2$, $b:=\frac{5-t}2$. Then $1<a<2$, $3<b<4$ and $a-b=t$. Hence $A=(-3,-1)$.

For an open interval the ends are the inf and sup, and they are not in the set.

So $\inf A=-3$, $\sup A=-1$. Since $-3,-1\notin A$, $A$ has neither a minimum nor a maximum.

Rewrite $B$'s elements so that the distance to $1$ is visible.

2. For $n\ge1$: $\frac n{n+1}=1-\frac1{n+1}$, so $\frac12\le\frac n{n+1}<1$. The value $\frac12$ is attained at $n=1$, hence $\inf B=\min B=\frac12$.

$1$ is a ceiling. To show it is the lowest one, beat every $1-\varepsilon$ with Archimedes.

$1$ is an upper bound. Let $\varepsilon>0$. By the Archimedean principle there is $n\in\mathbb N$ with $n+1>\frac1\varepsilon$; then $\frac n{n+1}=1-\frac1{n+1}>1-\varepsilon$. So no number below $1$ is an upper bound, and $\sup B=1$. Since $1\notin B$, there is no maximum.

$C$ is finite: find the range of $m$ and $n$, bound the fraction, and show both bounds are attained.

3. From $m,n\ge1$ and $m+n\le10$ we get $1\le m,n\le9$, hence $\frac19\le\frac nm\le9$. Both bounds are attained: $\frac19$ at $(m,n)=(9,1)$ and $9$ at $(m,n)=(1,9)$. So $\inf C=\min C=\frac19$ and $\sup C=\max C=9$. ∎

Trap

For $B$, writing “$\frac n{n+1}\to1$, so $\sup B=1$” without the $\varepsilon$-argument. At this point of the course, the clean proof is: upper bound, plus “for every $\varepsilon>0$ some element is above $1-\varepsilon$”. And for $A$, showing only $A\subseteq(-3,-1)$: that gives bounds, but not that they are the best bounds.

Aufgabe 2 · An inequality

For $a,b$ in a field $K$ with $b\ne0$ we write $\frac ab:=ab^{-1}$. Let $x,y,z\in\mathbb R$ with $xyz>0$. Prove that $$\frac x{yz}+\frac y{xz}+\frac z{xy}\ \ge\ \frac1x+\frac1y+\frac1z.$$

The idea

Fractions are annoying, so clear them: multiply everything by $xyz$. Because $xyz>0$, the inequality keeps its direction, and it turns into $x^2+y^2+z^2\ge xy+yz+zx$. That one is a classic: the difference is half a sum of squares, and squares are never negative.

You need

Rules of an ordered field (script, page 77): multiplying an inequality by a positive number keeps its direction; squares are $\ge0$. Lesson 5 works with the same rules.

Hint 1

Why are all the fractions defined? (What does $xyz>0$ say about each of $x,y,z$?) Then multiply both sides by $xyz$. What does each term become?

Hint 2

$\frac x{yz}\cdot xyz=x^2$ and $\frac1x\cdot xyz=yz$. So you need $x^2+y^2+z^2\ge xy+yz+zx$. Try writing $x^2+y^2+z^2-xy-yz-zx$ with squares.

Hint 3

$$\begin{aligned}&(x-y)^2+(y-z)^2+(z-x)^2\\&=2(x^2+y^2+z^2)\\&\quad-2(xy+yz+zx).\end{aligned}$$

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Check first that every fraction makes sense.

Since $xyz>0$, none of $x,y,z$ is $0$, so all fractions are defined.

Clear the denominators. Say why the direction is kept, and that the step can be undone (multiply by $(xyz)^{-1}>0$), so the two inequalities are equivalent.

Multiplying by $xyz>0$ (and back by $(xyz)^{-1}>0$) preserves the inequality, so the claim is equivalent to $$x^2+y^2+z^2\ \ge\ yz+xz+xy.$$

Write the difference as a sum of squares; then it is obviously $\ge0$.

This holds, since $$\begin{aligned}&x^2+y^2+z^2-xy-yz-zx\\&=\tfrac12\big((x-y)^2+(y-z)^2\\&\qquad+(z-x)^2\big)\ \ge\ 0.\end{aligned}$$ ∎

In one line: the difference of the two sides of the original inequality is $\dfrac{(x-y)^2+(y-z)^2+(z-x)^2}{2xyz}\ge0$.

Remarks. The numbers need not be positive one by one: $xyz>0$ is enough (e.g. $x=y=-1$, $z=1$ is allowed). Equality holds exactly when $x=y=z$, which together with $xyz>0$ means $x=y=z>0$.

Trap

Multiplying by $xyz$ without saying “$xyz>0$, so the direction is kept”. With a negative factor the inequality would flip; that sentence is what the grader looks for. Also: starting from the claim and arriving at something true proves nothing unless every step is an equivalence, so say so.

Aufgabe 3 · The supremum of the reciprocals

Let $A\subseteq\mathbb R$ with $\alpha:=\inf A>0$, and let $A^{-1}:=\{\frac1a: a\in A\}$. Show that $$\sup\big(A^{-1}\big)=\frac1\alpha.$$

The idea

Taking reciprocals of positive numbers turns the order around: big becomes small and small becomes big. So the highest floor of $A$ becomes the lowest ceiling of $A^{-1}$. In the proof, every “upper bound of $A^{-1}$” is translated into a “lower bound of $A$” and back.

The floor $\alpha$ of $A$ turns into the ceiling $\frac1\alpha$ of $A^{-1}$.

You need

Supremum = least upper bound, infimum = greatest lower bound (lesson 6; script, page 105). For positive numbers, $0<a\le b\iff\frac1b\le\frac1a$.

Hint 1

Two things to show: (i) $\frac1\alpha$ is an upper bound of $A^{-1}$; (ii) every upper bound $u$ of $A^{-1}$ satisfies $u\ge\frac1\alpha$.

Hint 2

(i): every $a\in A$ satisfies $a\ge\alpha>0$. (ii): first note $u>0$ (why?). Then $\frac1a\le u$ for all $a$ means $a\ge\frac1u$ for all $a$. What kind of bound is $\frac1u$ for $A$?

Hint 3

$\frac1u$ is a lower bound of $A$, and $\alpha$ is the greatest lower bound, so $\frac1u\le\alpha$, i.e. $u\ge\frac1\alpha$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

$\inf A$ being a real number means $A$ is not empty, so $A^{-1}$ isn't either.

Since $\alpha=\inf A$ is a real number, $A\ne\emptyset$.

Ceiling: flip $a\ge\alpha$; both sides are positive, so flipping reverses the inequality.

For every $a\in A$: $a\ge\alpha>0$, hence $\frac1a\le\frac1\alpha$. So $\frac1\alpha$ is an upper bound of $A^{-1}$.

Lowest ceiling: take any ceiling $u$. It is positive, because it lies above some $\frac1{a_0}>0$.

Let $u$ be any upper bound of $A^{-1}$. For some $a_0\in A$ we have $u\ge\frac1{a_0}>0$.

Flip again: a ceiling for the reciprocals is a floor for $A$.

For every $a\in A$, $\frac1a\le u$ gives $a\ge\frac1u$ (both positive). So $\frac1u$ is a lower bound of $A$.

$\alpha$ is the greatest floor, so $\frac1u$ can't be above it. Flip one last time.

Since $\alpha$ is the greatest lower bound, $\frac1u\le\alpha$, i.e. $u\ge\frac1\alpha$. So $\frac1\alpha$ is the least upper bound: $\sup(A^{-1})=\frac1\alpha$. ∎

Trap

Flipping inequalities without checking signs. $a\le b\Rightarrow\frac1b\le\frac1a$ needs both numbers positive. That is why the proof shows $u>0$ before flipping $\frac1a\le u$. Without $\alpha>0$ the statement is false: for $A=(0,1]$ the set $A^{-1}=[1,\infty)$ is unbounded.

Aufgabe 4 · Open and closed subsets of ℝ

Prove:

  1. An open subset of $\mathbb R$ has no maximum.
  2. If $A\subseteq\mathbb R$ is non-empty, bounded and closed, then $\sup A$ is a maximum of $A$: $\sup A\in A$ and $\max A=\sup A$.
The idea

1: In an open set, every point has some room around it, in particular room to the right, so there is always a bigger element. 2: The supremum is approached from inside $A$ as closely as you like. If it were outside $A$, it would sit in the open complement with a whole window around it free of $A$, but the supremum can't have a window free of $A$ just below it.

If $s\notin A$, there would be a gap just below $s$, so a smaller number would already be an upper bound.

You need

Open: for every $x\in U$ there is $\varepsilon>0$ with $(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq U$. Closed: the complement $\mathbb R\setminus A$ is open (script, page 101; lesson 5). Characterisation of the supremum: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $a\in A$ with $a>s-\varepsilon$ (script, page 105). Part 1 is lesson 6, Proof B; both parts together are the script's lemma on page 483.

Hint 1

1: Suppose $x\in U$ were the maximum. Use the open window around $x$. Don't forget $U=\emptyset$. 2: Suppose $s:=\sup A\notin A$. Where does $s$ lie, and what does that give you?

Hint 2

1: $x+\frac\varepsilon2\in U$ and is bigger than $x$. 2: $s\in\mathbb R\setminus A$, which is open, so $(s-\varepsilon,s+\varepsilon)\subseteq\mathbb R\setminus A$ for some $\varepsilon>0$.

Hint 3

2: But $s-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound of $A$, so some $a\in A$ satisfies $s-\varepsilon<a\le s$. That $a$ lies in the window: contradiction.

Solution

1.

Why this stepWhat you write

The empty set is open and has no maximum, since a maximum must be an element.

Let $U\subseteq\mathbb R$ be open. If $U=\emptyset$, it has no maximum, because a maximum must be an element of the set.

Any candidate has room to its right inside $U$; step half-way into that room.

Let $x\in U$. Since $U$ is open, there is $\varepsilon>0$ with $(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq U$. In particular $x+\frac\varepsilon2\in U$ and $x+\frac\varepsilon2>x$. So no element of $U$ is a maximum.

2.

Non-empty and bounded above: the supremum exists by completeness.

Let $A$ be non-empty, bounded and closed. By completeness, $s:=\sup A\in\mathbb R$ exists. We show $s\in A$.

Assume the opposite and use that the complement is open.

Suppose $s\notin A$. Since $A$ is closed, $\mathbb R\setminus A$ is open, so there is $\varepsilon>0$ with $(s-\varepsilon,s+\varepsilon)\subseteq\mathbb R\setminus A$.

But just below the supremum there must be points of $A$.

On the other hand, $s-\varepsilon<s$ is not an upper bound of $A$, since $s$ is the least one. So there is $a\in A$ with $s-\varepsilon<a\le s<s+\varepsilon$, i.e. $a\in(s-\varepsilon,s+\varepsilon)$, contradicting that this interval contains no point of $A$.

An upper bound that belongs to the set is its maximum.

Hence $s\in A$. Since $a\le s$ for all $a\in A$, $s$ is the maximum: $\max A=\sup A$. ∎

Why “non-empty” is needed: $\emptyset$ is bounded and closed, but has no maximum and no real supremum.

Trap

In 1, forgetting the case $U=\emptyset$, or taking $x+\varepsilon$ instead of $x+\frac\varepsilon2$: the window $(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)$ is open, so $x+\varepsilon$ itself need not be in $U$. In 2, using “closed means it contains its boundary” as the definition: in this course, closed means “the complement is open”.

Aufgabe 5 · Fixed points of monotone functions

Let $K$ be an ordered field. A function $f:K\to K$ is called increasing if $x\le y$ implies $f(x)\le f(y)$. Let $a,b\in K$ with $a<b$, and let $f:K\to K$ be increasing with $f(a)>a$ and $f(b)<b$. Decide for

  1. $K=\mathbb R$,
  2. $K=\mathbb Q$,

whether $f$ necessarily has a fixed point, i.e. some $\xi\in K$ with $f(\xi)=\xi$. Give a proof or a counterexample. Hint from the sheet for 1: consider the supremum of $S:=\{x\in[a,b]: x\le f(x)\}$.

The idea

At $a$ the graph of $f$ is above the diagonal, at $b$ below it, and $f$ never goes down. In $\mathbb R$ the graph must meet the diagonal: the last place where it is still above the diagonal is a supremum, and completeness guarantees that this place exists. In $\mathbb Q$ completeness fails, and the meeting point can be a “hole” such as $\sqrt2$. Note: $f$ need not be continuous. Monotonicity plus completeness is enough.

For $x>0$: $f(x)=\frac{2(x+1)}{x+2}$. It crosses the diagonal only at $x=\sqrt2$, which is not rational, so on $\mathbb Q$ there is no fixed point. (Scale: 60 px per unit.)

You need

Completeness: every non-empty set bounded above has a supremum in $\mathbb R$ (script, page 105; lesson 6, Proof A). For the counterexample: $x^2=2$ has no rational solution (lesson 4, Proof B).

Hint 1

1: $a\in S$ and $b$ is an upper bound, so $\xi:=\sup S\in[a,b]$ exists. Show $\xi\le f(\xi)$ and $f(\xi)\le\xi$ separately. 2: Look for an increasing rational function whose fixed points solve $x^2=2$.

Hint 2

1: For $x\in S$: $x\le f(x)\le f(\xi)$. So $f(\xi)$ is an upper bound of $S$; hence $\xi\le f(\xi)$. Then show $f(\xi)\in S$, which gives $f(\xi)\le\xi$. 2: Try $f(x)=\frac{2(x+1)}{x+2}$ for $x>0$, and $f(x)=1$ for $x\le0$, with $a=1$, $b=2$.

Hint 3

1: $f(\xi)\in S$ needs two things: $f(\xi)\in[a,b]$ (use $a\le\xi\le f(\xi)\le f(b)<b$) and $f(\xi)\le f(f(\xi))$ (apply $f$ to $\xi\le f(\xi)$). 2: For $x>0$: $f(x)=x$ $\iff x(x+2)=2x+2$ $\iff x^2=2$.

Solution

1. $K=\mathbb R$: a fixed point always exists.

Why this stepWhat you write

The set of points where $f$ is still on or above the diagonal. It is non-empty and bounded, so completeness gives its supremum.

Let $S:=\{x\in[a,b]: x\le f(x)\}$. Since $f(a)>a$, $a\in S$; and $b$ is an upper bound of $S$. By completeness of $\mathbb R$, $\xi:=\sup S$ exists, and $\xi\in[a,b]$.

First half: $f(\xi)$ is a ceiling for $S$, because $f$ only goes up.

For every $x\in S$: $x\le\xi$, so by monotonicity $x\le f(x)\le f(\xi)$. Thus $f(\xi)$ is an upper bound of $S$, and since $\xi$ is the least one, $$\xi\le f(\xi).$$

Second half: show that $f(\xi)$ itself is in $S$. First it lies in $[a,b]$…

Moreover $a\le\xi\le f(\xi)$ and $f(\xi)\le f(b)<b$, so $f(\xi)\in[a,b]$.

…and it is still on or above the diagonal: apply $f$ to $\xi\le f(\xi)$.

Applying monotonicity to $\xi\le f(\xi)$ gives $f(\xi)\le f\big(f(\xi)\big)$. Hence $f(\xi)\in S$, and therefore $f(\xi)\le\sup S=\xi$.

Both inequalities together.

Together: $f(\xi)=\xi$.

2. $K=\mathbb Q$: a fixed point need not exist.

A function defined on all of $\mathbb Q$ with rational values, whose only possible crossing is at $\sqrt2$.

Define $f:\mathbb Q\to\mathbb Q$ by $f(x):=1$ for $x\le0$ and $f(x):=\dfrac{2(x+1)}{x+2}$ for $x>0$. For $x>0$ the denominator is positive and the value is rational, so $f$ is well defined.

Increasing: check the three cases of where $x\le y$ can lie.

Increasing. For $x\le y\le0$: $f(x)=f(y)=1$. For $0<x\le y$: $$\begin{aligned}f(y)-f(x)&=\frac{2(y-x)}{(y+2)(x+2)}\\&\ge0.\end{aligned}$$ For $x\le0<y$: $$\begin{aligned}f(x)=1&<1+\frac y{y+2}\\&=\frac{2(y+1)}{y+2}=f(y).\end{aligned}$$

The boundary conditions with $a=1$, $b=2$.

With $a=1$, $b=2$: $f(1)=\frac43>1$ and $f(2)=\frac32<2$.

No rational fixed point: for $x\le0$ the value is $1\ne x$; for $x>0$ a fixed point would be a rational square root of $2$.

No fixed point. For $x\le0$: $f(x)=1\ne x$. For $x>0$: $$\begin{aligned}f(x)=x&\iff 2x+2=x^2+2x\\&\iff x^2=2,\end{aligned}$$ which has no rational solution. So $f$ has no fixed point in $\mathbb Q$, although all assumptions hold. ∎

Trap

Using the intermediate value theorem on $g(x)=f(x)-x$. It needs continuity, and $f$ is only monotone here. The supremum argument is what makes the proof work. Second trap: forgetting to check $f(\xi)\in[a,b]$ before concluding $f(\xi)\in S$.

Aufgabe 6 · Accumulation points in ℂ

Let $A\subseteq\mathbb C$ and $z_0\in\mathbb C$. We call $z_0$ an accumulation point of $A$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $a\in A$ with $0<|a-z_0|<\varepsilon$. Let $A$ be infinite and bounded (there is $M>0$ with $A\subseteq B_M(0)$). Show that $A$ has an accumulation point in $\mathbb C$.

The idea

This is the plane version of the theorem you know for $\mathbb R$ (lesson 6, Proof G): there, you sweep a vertical line from left to right and stop where “finitely many points behind me” switches to “infinitely many”. In the plane, do this twice. First sweep in the $x$-direction to find a vertical strip around $x_0$ that always contains infinitely many points. Then, inside thin strips around $x_0$, sweep upwards to find $y_0$. Every small square around $x_0+iy_0$ then contains infinitely many points of $A$.

Step 1 finds the strip around $x_0$, step 2 the height $y_0$; every square around $z_0=x_0+iy_0$ contains infinitely many points of $A$.

You need

The real version and its proof: a bounded infinite set $A\subseteq\mathbb R$ has an accumulation point (script, page 113; lesson 6, Proof G). Completeness: sup exists (script, page 105). $|u+iv|=\sqrt{u^2+v^2}$ (script, page 101). If $E$ is infinite and $F\subseteq E$ is finite, then $E\setminus F$ is infinite.

Hint 1

Identify $\mathbb C$ with $\mathbb R^2$ and choose $D>0$ with $A\subseteq[-D,D]\times[-D,D]$. Copy the real proof for the first coordinate: let $X$ be the set of all $x\in\mathbb R$ such that $A\cap\big((-\infty,x]\times\mathbb R\big)$ is finite, and $x_0:=\sup X$.

Hint 2

As in the real proof, for every $\eta>0$ the strip $[x_0-\eta,x_0+\eta]\times\mathbb R$ contains infinitely many points of $A$. Now do the same upwards inside thin strips: let $Y$ be the set of all $y$ for which some $\varepsilon>0$ makes $A\cap\big([x_0-\varepsilon,x_0+\varepsilon]\times(-\infty,y]\big)$ finite, and $y_0:=\sup Y$.

Hint 3

For $\rho>0$ put $r=\frac\rho2$. Since $y_0-r\in Y$, some thin strip has only finitely many points below height $y_0-r$; since $y_0+r\notin Y$, that strip (made thinner, of width $\delta\le r$) has infinitely many points below $y_0+r$. The difference is infinite and lies in a square of half-side $r$ around $z_0$, so within distance $\sqrt2\,r<\rho$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Work in coordinates, and put $A$ in a box.

Identify $\mathbb C$ with $\mathbb R^2$ via $x+iy\leftrightarrow(x,y)$. Since $A$ is bounded, there is $D>0$ with $A\subseteq[-D,D]\times[-D,D]$.

Step 1, the $x$-sweep. Points with only finitely many points of $A$ to their left. Far left: none at all. Far right: all of $A$.

Let $$\begin{aligned}&X:=\{x\in\mathbb R:\\&\quad A\cap\big((-\infty,x]\times\mathbb R\big)\ \text{finite}\}.\end{aligned}$$ For $x<-D$ the intersection is empty, so $x\in X$; for $x\ge D$ it is all of $A$, which is infinite, so $x\notin X$. Thus $X$ is non-empty and bounded above, and $x_0:=\sup X\in[-D,D]$ exists.

$X$ is closed downwards, so it contains everything left of $x_0$ and nothing right of $x_0$.

If $x\in X$ and $t\le x$, then $t\in X$ (a subset of a finite set is finite). So $t<x_0$ implies $t\in X$ (there is $x\in X$ with $x>t$), and $t>x_0$ implies $t\notin X$.

Every vertical strip around $x_0$ has infinitely many points: infinitely many left of $x_0+\eta$ minus finitely many left of $x_0-\eta$.

Let $\eta>0$. Then $A\cap\big((-\infty,x_0+\eta]\times\mathbb R\big)$ is infinite and $A\cap\big((-\infty,x_0-\eta]\times\mathbb R\big)$ is finite. Removing the second from the first leaves infinitely many points, so $$\begin{aligned}&A\cap\big([x_0-\eta,x_0+\eta]\times\mathbb R\big)\\&\text{is infinite for every }\eta>0.\quad(\ast)\end{aligned}$$

Step 2, the $y$-sweep, inside thin strips around $x_0$.

Let $Y$ be the set of all $y\in\mathbb R$ for which there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that $$A\cap\big([x_0-\varepsilon,x_0+\varepsilon]\times(-\infty,y]\big)$$ is finite.

Below the box: nothing, so in $Y$. Above the box: the whole strip, infinite by $(\ast)$, so not in $Y$.

For $y<-D$ the intersection is empty for every $\varepsilon$, so $y\in Y$. For $y\ge D$ it equals $A\cap\big([x_0-\varepsilon,x_0+\varepsilon]\times\mathbb R\big)$, infinite for every $\varepsilon>0$ by $(\ast)$, so $y\notin Y$. Hence $y_0:=\sup Y\in[-D,D]$ exists.

Same argument as for $X$: everything below $y_0$ is in $Y$, everything above is not.

$Y$ is closed downwards (for $t\le y\in Y$ the same $\varepsilon$ works), so $y<y_0\Rightarrow y\in Y$ and $y>y_0\Rightarrow y\notin Y$.

Step 3. Fix a radius $\rho$. Below $y_0-r$: finitely many points in some strip. Make the strip thin enough ($\delta\le r$) to fit in the square; it stays finite.

Let $z_0:=x_0+iy_0$, let $\rho>0$ and $r:=\frac\rho2$. Since $y_0-r\in Y$, there is $\varepsilon_0>0$ such that $$\begin{aligned}A\cap\big(&[x_0-\varepsilon_0,x_0+\varepsilon_0]\\&\times(-\infty,y_0-r]\big)\end{aligned}$$ is finite. With $\delta:=\min\{\varepsilon_0,r\}$, also $$\begin{aligned}F:=A\cap\big(&[x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta]\\&\times(-\infty,y_0-r]\big)\end{aligned}$$ is finite.

Below $y_0+r$: infinitely many points in every strip, in particular in this one.

Since $y_0+r\notin Y$, for every width, in particular $\delta$, the set $$\begin{aligned}E:=A\cap\big(&[x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta]\\&\times(-\infty,y_0+r]\big)\end{aligned}$$ is infinite.

Infinite minus finite is infinite, and what's left lies in a small square around $z_0$. Pick a point different from $z_0$.

So $$\begin{aligned}E\setminus F=A\cap\big(&[x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta]\\&\times(y_0-r,y_0+r]\big)\end{aligned}$$ is infinite, and we can choose $a=u+iv$ in it with $a\ne z_0$. Then $|u-x_0|\le\delta\le r$ and $|v-y_0|\le r$, so $$\begin{aligned}|a-z_0|^2&=(u-x_0)^2+(v-y_0)^2\\&\le2r^2,\\ |a-z_0|&\le\sqrt2\,r=\frac\rho{\sqrt2}<\rho,\end{aligned}$$ and $|a-z_0|>0$ since $a\ne z_0$.

The radius was arbitrary.

Since $\rho>0$ was arbitrary, $z_0$ is an accumulation point of $A$. ∎

Another way: halving squares

Start with the square $Q_0=[-D,D]^2$, which contains infinitely many points of $A$. Cut it into four equal closed squares; at least one of them contains infinitely many points of $A$ (otherwise $A$ would be a union of four finite sets). Call it $Q_1$ and repeat. The squares $Q_0\supseteq Q_1\supseteq\cdots$ have side $2D/2^n$. Their $x$-projections are nested closed intervals, and so are the $y$-projections, so by the nested interval principle (script, page 114) there is a point $z_0$ in all $Q_n$. Every disc around $z_0$ of radius $\rho$ contains $Q_n$ once $\sqrt2\cdot 2D/2^n<\rho$, hence infinitely many points of $A$, in particular one $\ne z_0$.

Trap

Applying the real theorem to the real parts and to the imaginary parts separately. If all points of $A$ lie on a vertical line (say $A=\{i/n\}$), the set of real parts is $\{0\}$, which is finite and has no accumulation point. You must keep the two coordinates linked, as in step 2 (thin strips around $x_0$) or with squares.
