1. The story: ropes over the valley

Below the glassmaker's mountain lies a long valley, and the valley people are rope-makers. They have a test for a good valley: pick any two points on the ground, stretch a rope between them, and look. In a bowl-shaped valley the rope never touches the ground between its ends; the ground always sags below it. Valleys like that are easy to live in. Water runs to one lowest place, and from any point the ground ahead climbs at least as steeply as the ground behind.

The rope-makers also settle arguments between runners. Two runners start from the same finish line and run backwards in time towards a common starting moment, both arriving at height zero. Who was ahead, and by how much, just before the start? Looking at their positions tells nothing: both are near zero. But comparing their speeds tells everything. That trick has a name in the valley: the rule of the marquis.

In maths: a bowl-shaped valley is a convex function: its graph lies below every chord, and its slope $f'$ is increasing. The runners are two functions $f,g$ with $f(x),g(x)\to0$, and the rule of the marquis (Guillaume de l'Hôpital) says $\lim\frac fg=\lim\frac{f'}{g'}$. Both rest on the mean value theorem.

2. Deciding the candidates: tests for extrema

Lesson 17 gave the candidates: endpoints, non-differentiable points, and critical points ($f'=0$). On a compact interval, comparing the values at the candidates finds the global extrema. To decide whether a single candidate is a local maximum or minimum, or to work on open or unbounded intervals, we need tests.

Proof A: the sign-change test and the second derivative test (Korollar 8.37, p. 374–375)

Theorem. Let $I$ be an interval, $x_0$ an interior point of $I$, and $f:I\to\mathbb R$ continuous and differentiable on $I\setminus\{x_0\}$.

  1. Sign change. If for some $\delta>0$: $f'>0$ on $(x_0-\delta,x_0)$ and $f'<0$ on $(x_0,x_0+\delta)$, then $f$ has an isolated local maximum at $x_0$. (Signs reversed: isolated local minimum. Same sign on both sides: no extremum.)
  2. Second derivative. If $f$ is twice continuously differentiable on $I$, $f'(x_0)=0$ and $f''(x_0)<0$, then $f$ has an isolated local maximum at $x_0$. ($f''(x_0)>0$: isolated local minimum.)

The idea. Uphill on the left, downhill on the right: you're at a peak. The mean value theorem turns “$f'>0$ on the left” into “$f$ rises towards $x_0$”. For the second test, $f''<0$ near $x_0$ means $f'$ is decreasing there; since $f'(x_0)=0$, $f'$ is positive just before and negative just after, so test 1 applies.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Goal of 1: “$f(x)<f(x_0)$ for all $x\in(x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta)$, $x\neq x_0$.” Two cases: $x<x_0$ and $x>x_0$.
  • Goal of 2: reduce to 1: find $\delta$ with $f'>0$ on the left and $f'<0$ on the right.
The storyWhat you write

Walk up to the point from the left: the ground rises all the way.

1. Let $x\in(x_0-\delta,x_0)$. $f$ is continuous on $[x,x_0]$ and differentiable on $(x,x_0)$, so by the mean value theorem $f(x_0)-f(x)=f'(\xi)(x_0-x)$ for some $\xi\in(x,x_0)$. Both factors are $>0$, so $f(x)<f(x_0)$.

Walk away to the right: the ground falls all the way. So the point is the top.

Let $x\in(x_0,x_0+\delta)$. Likewise $f(x)-f(x_0)=f'(\eta)(x-x_0)$ with $\eta\in(x_0,x)$, which is $<0$. So $f(x)<f(x_0)$ for all $x\neq x_0$ in $(x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta)$: an isolated local maximum. (Differentiability at $x_0$ itself was never used.)

A road that bends downwards at a level point: just before, it still climbs; just after, it already falls.

2. $f''$ is continuous and $f''(x_0)<0$, so $f''<0$ on some $(x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta)\subseteq I$. There $f'$ is strictly decreasing (lesson 17, Proof H). With $f'(x_0)=0$: $f'(x)>0$ for $x_0-\delta<x<x_0$ and $f'(x)<0$ for $x_0<x<x_0+\delta$. By 1, $x_0$ is an isolated local maximum. ∎

Moves used: mean value theorem between two points

The proof in one piece

1. For $x\in(x_0-\delta,x_0)$, the mean value theorem on $[x,x_0]$ gives $f(x_0)-f(x)=f'(\xi)(x_0-x)>0$ with $\xi\in(x,x_0)$. For $x\in(x_0,x_0+\delta)$, it gives $f(x)-f(x_0)=f'(\eta)(x-x_0)<0$ with $\eta\in(x_0,x)$. So $f(x)<f(x_0)$ for $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$.

2. By continuity, $f''<0$ on some $(x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta)$, so $f'$ is strictly decreasing there; as $f'(x_0)=0$, $f'>0$ to the left of $x_0$ and $f'<0$ to the right. Apply 1. ∎

    Yes: $f$ is continuous, $f'=-1$ on the left and $+1$ on the right, so $0$ is an isolated local minimum. Test 1 never needs $f'(x_0)$.

The recipe for “find all local and global extrema”

  1. Candidates: endpoints in $I$, points where $f$ is not differentiable, zeros of $f'$.
  2. Sign table of $f'$ between consecutive candidates (factor $f'$!). A sign change $+\,|\,-$ is a local maximum, $-\,|\,+$ a local minimum, no change is no extremum. At a left endpoint, $f'>0$ just after it means a local minimum; at a right endpoint, $f'>0$ just before it means a local maximum (the script's endpoint rules).
  3. Global: on $[a,b]$, compare all candidate values. On an open or unbounded interval, also compute the limits at the ends: a global maximum exists only if some candidate value beats them.
  4. Answer in words: “local maximum at $x=\dots$ with value $\dots$; global minimum …”.

Worked example 1: $x^3-x$ and $|x^3-x|$ (Übung 8.19, p. 366)

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

  1. Find the local extrema of $f(x)=x^3-x$ on $\mathbb R$.

    Answer

    $f'(x)=3x^2-1=0$ at $x=\pm\frac1{\sqrt3}$. Signs of $f'$: $+$ on $\left(-\infty,-\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)$, $-$ in between, $+$ after. So a local maximum at $-\frac1{\sqrt3}$ with value $\frac2{3\sqrt3}$, and a local minimum at $\frac1{\sqrt3}$ with value $-\frac2{3\sqrt3}$. (Or test 2: $f''(x)=6x$ is $<0$ at $-\frac1{\sqrt3}$ and $>0$ at $\frac1{\sqrt3}$.) No global extrema, since $f\to\pm\infty$ at $\pm\infty$.

  2. Now $g=|f|$ on $[-3,3]$. Where is $g$ not differentiable, and what are all local and global extrema?

    Answer

    $f(x)=x(x-1)(x+1)$ has zeros $0,\pm1$, where $f'\neq0$, so $g$ has corners there (non-differentiable); they are local minima with value $0$, and also global minima. The critical points $\pm\frac1{\sqrt3}$ of $f$ give local maxima of $g$ with value $\frac2{3\sqrt3}\approx0.38$. The endpoints: $g(\pm3)=24$, and $g$ increases towards them, so they are local maxima, and global maxima with value $24$.

Worked example 2: the minimum of $x^x$ on $(0,\infty)$

  1. Differentiate $x^x$ and find the critical point.

    Answer

    $(x^x)'=x^x(\log x+1)$. Since $x^x>0$, it is $0$ exactly when $\log x=-1$, i.e. $x=\frac1e$.

  2. Sign of the derivative on each side? What kind of extremum, and is it global?

    Answer

    $\log x+1<0$ for $x<\frac1e$ and $>0$ for $x>\frac1e$. So $x^x$ is strictly decreasing on $\left(0,\frac1e\right]$ and strictly increasing on $\left[\frac1e,\infty\right)$ (lesson 17, Proof H). That makes $\frac1e$ a global minimum, with value $\left(\frac1e\right)^{1/e}=e^{-1/e}\approx0.69$. (Monotonicity on each side gives “global” for free, without computing limits at the ends.)

3. Convex functions

Definition 8.38 (p. 375). Let $I$ be an interval. $f:I\to\mathbb R$ is convex if

$$\begin{aligned}&f\bigl((1-t)x_0+tx_1\bigr)\le (1-t)f(x_0)+tf(x_1)\\&\text{for all }x_0,x_1\in I,\ t\in[0,1]\end{aligned}.$$

Strictly convex: “$<$” whenever $x_0\neq x_1$ and $t\in(0,1)$. $g$ is (strictly) concave if $-g$ is (strictly) convex.

As $t$ runs from $0$ to $1$, the point $(1-t)x_0+tx_1$ runs from $x_0$ to $x_1$, and the right side runs along the chord (the rope) from $(x_0,f(x_0))$ to $(x_1,f(x_1))$. So: the graph lies below every chord. Examples: $x^2$, $e^x$, $|x|$ are convex; $\log$ and $\sqrt x$ are concave; $x^3$ is neither on $\mathbb R$.

Proof B: convex means increasing secant slopes (Lemma 8.39, p. 376)

Lemma. $f:I\to\mathbb R$ is convex if and only if for all $x_0<x<x_1$ in $I$:

$$\frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0}\le\frac{f(x_1)-f(x)}{x_1-x}.$$

(Strictly convex if and only if this holds with “$<$”.)

The idea. Write the middle point as $x=(1-t)x_0+tx_1$; then $t=\frac{x-x_0}{x_1-x_0}$ and $1-t=\frac{x_1-x}{x_1-x_0}$. Plug these weights into the convexity inequality, clear denominators, and rearrange. Every step is reversible, which gives both directions at once.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Start: fix $x_0<x_1$; the cases $t=0$, $t=1$ of the definition say nothing, so only $t\in(0,1)$, i.e. $x\in(x_0,x_1)$, matters.
  • End: a chain of equivalences from the definition at $t=\frac{x-x_0}{x_1-x_0}$ to the slope inequality.
The storyWhat you write

Every point between the rope's ends corresponds to exactly one share $t$ of the way along.

Fix $x_0<x_1$ in $I$. The map $t\mapsto(1-t)x_0+tx_1$ is a bijection $(0,1)\to(x_0,x_1)$ with inverse $x\mapsto t=\frac{x-x_0}{x_1-x_0}$, and then $1-t=\frac{x_1-x}{x_1-x_0}$. (For $t\in\{0,1\}$ the inequality is an equality.)

“The ground is below the rope” becomes a statement about three heights.

So the convexity inequality for $x_0,x_1$ holds for all $t$ if and only if for all $x\in(x_0,x_1)$:

$$\begin{aligned}f(x)&\le \frac{x_1-x}{x_1-x_0}f(x_0)+\frac{x-x_0}{x_1-x_0}f(x_1)\iff(x_1-x_0)f(x)\\&\le (x_1-x)f(x_0)+(x-x_0)f(x_1)\end{aligned}.$$

Split the middle height into the part looking back and the part looking ahead, and compare the two climbs.

Write

$$(x_1-x_0)f(x)=(x_1-x)f(x)+(x-x_0)f(x).$$

The last inequality becomes

$$\begin{aligned}(x_1-x)\bigl(f(x)-f(x_0)\bigr)&\le (x-x_0)\bigl(f(x_1)-f(x)\bigr)\end{aligned},$$

and dividing by $(x-x_0)(x_1-x)>0$ gives exactly the slope inequality. All steps are equivalences, and strict versions work the same way. ∎

Moves used: compare three slopes

The proof in one piece

Let $x_0<x_1$ in $I$. As $t$ runs through $(0,1)$, $x=(1-t)x_0+tx_1$ runs through $(x_0,x_1)$, with $t=\frac{x-x_0}{x_1-x_0}$, $1-t=\frac{x_1-x}{x_1-x_0}$; for $t\in\{0,1\}$ there is nothing to show. Hence convexity is equivalent to: for all $x_0<x<x_1$ in $I$,

$$\begin{aligned}(x_1-x_0)f(x)&\le (x_1-x)f(x_0)+(x-x_0)f(x_1)\iff(x_1-x)\bigl(f(x)-f(x_0)\bigr)\\&\le (x-x_0)\bigl(f(x_1)-f(x)\bigr)\end{aligned},$$

and dividing by $(x-x_0)(x_1-x)>0$ gives the claim. Strict convexity: replace every $\le$ by $<$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (2 min). Show that the slope of the whole chord, $\frac{f(x_1)-f(x_0)}{x_1-x_0}$, lies between the two slopes of the lemma.

    Answer

    $$\begin{aligned}f(x_1)-f(x_0)&=(f(x)-f(x_0))+(f(x_1)-f(x))\end{aligned},$$so the chord's slope is the weighted average $\frac{x-x_0}{x_1-x_0}\cdot s_{\text{left}}+\frac{x_1-x}{x_1-x_0}\cdot s_{\text{right}}$ of the two slopes, with weights adding to $1$. An average lies between the two. So $s_{\text{left}}\le s_{\text{chord}}\le s_{\text{right}}$: the “three slopes” in order. Felder FS23 uses exactly this.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Check the lemma for $|x|$ with $x_0=-1$, $x=0$, $x_1=2$.

    Answer

    Left slope $\frac{0-1}{1}=-1$, right slope $\frac{2-0}2=1$, and $-1\le1$. ✓

Proof C: convex if and only if $f'$ is increasing (Proposition 8.40, p. 377)

Theorem. Let $I$ be an interval and $f:I\to\mathbb R$ differentiable. Then $f$ is convex if and only if $f'$ is increasing. (Strictly convex if and only if $f'$ is strictly increasing.)

Corollary 8.42. If $f$ is twice differentiable and $f''\ge0$ on $I$, then $f$ is convex; if $f''>0$, strictly convex. (Because $f''\ge0$ makes $f'$ increasing, lesson 17's Proof H.)

The idea. Proof B says convex means “left secant slope ≤ right secant slope”. The mean value theorem turns each secant slope into a value of $f'$, at a point on the left and a point on the right. ⇐: if $f'$ is increasing, the left value is smaller. ⇒: shrink the secants at both ends of $[a,b]$ until they become tangents.

  • Mean value theorem (lesson 17).
  • Proof B and its three slopes.
What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • ⇐: “Let $x_0<x<x_1$ in $I$.” … “so $\frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0}\le\frac{f(x_1)-f(x)}{x_1-x}$, hence convex by Proof B.”
  • ⇒: “Let $a<b$ in $I$.” … “$f'(a)\le f'(b)$.”
  • The gaps: ⇐ needs two mean value theorems; ⇒ needs secant slopes at $a$ and at $b$ that tend to $f'(a)$ and $f'(b)$.
The storyWhat you write

⇐ The climb behind you equals the steepness at some earlier moment, the climb ahead equals the steepness at some later moment.

⇐. Suppose $f'$ is increasing, and let $x_0<x<x_1$ in $I$. By the mean value theorem on $[x_0,x]$ and on $[x,x_1]$ there are $\xi_1\in(x_0,x)$, $\xi_2\in(x,x_1)$ with

$$\begin{aligned}&f'(\xi_1)=\frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0}\\&f'(\xi_2)=\frac{f(x_1)-f(x)}{x_1-x}\end{aligned}.$$

Steepness only grows, so behind is at most ahead: the ropes hang above.

Since $\xi_1<x<\xi_2$ and $f'$ is increasing, $f'(\xi_1)\le f'(\xi_2)$. This is the slope inequality, so $f$ is convex by Proof B.

⇒ In a bowl, take a short rope at the left end, a long rope in the middle, and a short rope at the right end: their steepnesses are in order.

⇒. Suppose $f$ is convex, and let $a<b$ in $I$. For $0<h<\frac{b-a}2$ we have $a<a+h<b-h<b$. Proof B for the triples $(a,a+h,b-h)$ and $(a+h,b-h,b)$ gives

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}h&\le \frac{f(b-h)-f(a+h)}{(b-h)-(a+h)}\\&\le \frac{f(b)-f(b-h)}h\end{aligned}.$$

Shorten the end ropes to nothing: they become the tangents.

Let $h\searrow0$. The left side tends to $f'(a)$ and the right side to $f'(b)$ ($f$ is differentiable), and limits keep $\le$. So $f'(a)\le f'(b)$: $f'$ is increasing. ∎

Moves used: mean value theorem between two points compare three slopes

The proof in one piece

Recall (Proof B): $f$ is convex if and only if $\frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0}\le\frac{f(x_1)-f(x)}{x_1-x}$ for all $x_0<x<x_1$ in $I$.

⇐. Let $f'$ be increasing and $x_0<x<x_1$ in $I$. By the mean value theorem there are $\xi_1\in(x_0,x)$ and $\xi_2\in(x,x_1)$ with $f'(\xi_1)=\frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0}$ and $f'(\xi_2)=\frac{f(x_1)-f(x)}{x_1-x}$. As $\xi_1<\xi_2$, $f'(\xi_1)\le f'(\xi_2)$, so $f$ is convex.

⇒. Let $f$ be convex and $a<b$ in $I$. For $0<h<\frac{b-a}2$, applying the slope inequality to $a<a+h<b-h$ and to $a+h<b-h<b$:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}h&\le \frac{f(b-h)-f(a+h)}{b-a-2h}\\&\le \frac{f(b)-f(b-h)}h\end{aligned}.$$

Letting $h\searrow0$ gives $f'(a)\le f'(b)$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Recall the slope form of convexity (Proof B). Figalli's solution simply wrote “Remember that $f$ is convex if …”. If the exam gives the $t$-definition, derive it in three lines.
  2. ⇐: two mean value theorems, $\xi_1<x<\xi_2$, $f'(\xi_1)\le f'(\xi_2)$.
  3. ⇒: $a<a+h<b-h<b$, two slope inequalities chained, $h\searrow0$.

In short: “slopes; two MVTs; squeeze the end ropes”. This was Figalli FS24 B2, word for word. Figalli HS23 A1 (a) and (b) is the same statement in the form $(f'(y)-f'(x))(y-x)\ge0$ (Exercise 5).

4. Jensen's inequality

Convexity with two points extends to any number of points: “$f$ of an average is at most the average of $f$”.

Proof D: Jensen's inequality (Lemma 8.44, p. 378–379)

Theorem. Let $f:I\to\mathbb R$ be convex, $x_1,\dots,x_n\in I$, and $t_1,\dots,t_n\in[0,1]$ with $\sum_{k=1}^nt_k=1$. Then

$$f\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^nt_kx_k\Bigr)\le\sum_{k=1}^nt_kf(x_k).$$

The idea. Induction on $n$. To go from $n$ to $n+1$ points, merge the last two points into one: their weighted average, carrying their combined weight $t_n+t_{n+1}$. That leaves $n$ points (induction hypothesis), and the merged point splits again by two-point convexity.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Base: $n=1$ is trivial, $n=2$ is the definition.
  • Step: assume it for $n$; given $n+1$ points and weights, show it. If $t_n+t_{n+1}=0$ it's the case $n$; otherwise merge.
The storyWhat you write

Tie the last two posts together into one, standing between them, carrying both their loads.

Assume the claim for $n\ge2$, and let $x_1,\dots,x_{n+1}$, $t_1,\dots,t_{n+1}$ be given. If $s=t_n+t_{n+1}=0$, then $t_n=t_{n+1}=0$ and this is the case $n$. Otherwise put $y=\frac{t_n}sx_n+\frac{t_{n+1}}sx_{n+1}\in I$ (it lies between $x_n$ and $x_{n+1}$). Then

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}t_kx_k=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}t_kx_k+s\,y.$$

Now there are only $n$ posts, which we already know how to handle.

The weights $t_1,\dots,t_{n-1},s$ sum to $1$, so by the induction hypothesis

$$f\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}t_kx_k\Bigr)\le\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}t_kf(x_k)+s\,f(y).$$

Untie the merged post: two-point convexity splits its load back.

By convexity with the weights $\frac{t_n}s+\frac{t_{n+1}}s=1$: $s\,f(y)\le t_nf(x_n)+t_{n+1}f(x_{n+1})$. Together: $f\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}t_kx_k\right)\le\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}t_kf(x_k)$. ∎

Moves used: induction with labelled parts merge two weights into one

The proof in one piece

$$\begin{aligned}f\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}t_kx_k\Bigr)&=f\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}t_kx_k+sy\Bigr)\\&\le \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}t_kf(x_k)+sf(y)\\&\le \sum_{k=1}^{n+1}t_kf(x_k)\end{aligned}.$$

∎

5. Cauchy's mean value theorem

Proof E: Cauchy's mean value theorem (Satz 8.48, p. 379–380)

Theorem. Let $f,g:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ be continuous, differentiable on $(a,b)$. Then there is a $\xi\in(a,b)$ with

$$g'(\xi)\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)=f'(\xi)\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr).$$

If moreover $g'(x)\neq0$ for all $x\in(a,b)$, then $g(a)\neq g(b)$ and $\displaystyle\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}=\frac{f'(\xi)}{g'(\xi)}$.

The idea. The ordinary mean value theorem is the case $g(x)=x$. The same trick works: build a helper that combines $f$ and $g$ so that its values at $a$ and $b$ agree, and apply Rolle. The helper that works is the “cross product” $F=g\cdot(f(b)-f(a))-f\cdot(g(b)-g(a))$. Why one $\xi$ for both functions matters: two separate mean value theorems would give two different points.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: define $$\begin{aligned}F(x)&=g(x)\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)-f(x)\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)\end{aligned}.$$
  • Last line: “$$\begin{aligned}0&=F'(\xi)\\&=g'(\xi)(f(b)-f(a))-f'(\xi)(g(b)-g(a))\end{aligned}.$$”
  • The gap: $F(a)=F(b)$, and the extra claim $g(a)\neq g(b)$.
The storyWhat you write

Two runners. Build one combined runner who starts and ends at the same height.

Let $$F(x)=g(x)\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)-f(x)\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)$$on $[a,b]$: continuous, and differentiable on $(a,b)$. Expanding,

$$F(a)=g(a)f(b)-f(a)g(b)=F(b).$$

The combined runner stood still at some moment. At that moment the two speeds were in the ratio of the total distances.

By Rolle's theorem, $$\begin{aligned}0&=F'(\xi)\\&=g'(\xi)(f(b)-f(a))-f'(\xi)(g(b)-g(a))\end{aligned}$$for some $\xi\in(a,b)$.

If the second runner never stands still, they can't end where they started, so we may divide.

If $g'\neq0$ on $(a,b)$: $g(a)=g(b)$ would give, by Rolle, a point with $g'=0$; so $g(a)\neq g(b)$. Divide the equation by $g'(\xi)\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)\neq0$. ∎

Moves used: a helper function assume the opposite

The proof in one piece

The function

$$F(x)=g(x)\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)-f(x)\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)$$

is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and $F(a)=g(a)f(b)-f(a)g(b)=F(b)$. By Rolle, $F'(\xi)=0$ for some $\xi\in(a,b)$, which is the first claim. If $g'\neq0$ on $(a,b)$, then $g(a)\neq g(b)$ (else Rolle gives a zero of $g'$), and dividing by $g'(\xi)(g(b)-g(a))$ gives the second. ∎

6. L'Hôpital's rule

Theorem (l'Hôpital, Satz 8.49, p. 380–381). Let $f,g:(a,b)\to\mathbb R$ be differentiable with $g(x)\neq0$ and $g'(x)\neq0$ on $(a,b)$. Suppose

  • either $\lim_{x\searrow a}f(x)=\lim_{x\searrow a}g(x)=0$ (the form “$\frac00$”), or $\lim_{x\searrow a}g(x)=\pm\infty$ (the form “$\frac\infty\infty$”),
  • and $L=\lim_{x\searrow a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$ exists in $\mathbb R\cup\{\pm\infty\}$.

Then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\searrow a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=L$ too. The same holds for $x\nearrow b$, for $x\to x_0\in(a,b)$ (where $g(x_0)$, $g'(x_0)$ may be $0$), and for $a=-\infty$ or $b=+\infty$.

Proof F: l'Hôpital for “$\frac00$” (p. 381)

The idea. Set $f(a)=g(a)=0$. Then $\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{g(x)-g(a)}$, which by Cauchy's mean value theorem equals $\frac{f'(\xi)}{g'(\xi)}$ for some $\xi$ between $a$ and $x$. As $x\searrow a$, $\xi$ is squeezed to $a$ too, so the ratio tends to $L$. The two runners: their positions are both near $0$, but the ratio of positions equals the ratio of speeds at some earlier moment.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $\delta>0$ with $\left|\frac{f'(\xi)}{g'(\xi)}-L\right|<\varepsilon$ for all $\xi\in(a,a+\delta)$.” (Here $L\in\mathbb R$; for $L=\pm\infty$ replace “$\varepsilon$-close” by “beyond $M$”.)
  • Last line: “so $\left|\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}-L\right|<\varepsilon$ for all $x\in(a,a+\delta)$.”
  • The gap: connect $\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ to $\frac{f'(\xi)}{g'(\xi)}$ with $\xi\in(a,x)$.
The storyWhat you write

Give both runners a common start, at height zero.

Extend $f,g$ to $[a,b)$ by $f(a)=g(a)=0$. By the “$\frac00$” assumption they are continuous on $[a,b)$. Let $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $\delta\in(0,b-a)$ with $\left|\frac{f'(\xi)}{g'(\xi)}-L\right|<\varepsilon$ for all $\xi\in(a,a+\delta)$.

The ratio of their positions equals the ratio of their speeds at some moment since the start.

Let $x\in(a,a+\delta)$. On $[a,x]$, $f$ and $g$ are continuous, differentiable on $(a,x)$, and $g'\neq0$. By Cauchy's mean value theorem there is $\xi_x\in(a,x)$ with

$$\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{g(x)-g(a)}=\frac{f'(\xi_x)}{g'(\xi_x)}.$$

That moment lies in the window where speeds are already close to $L$.

Since $\xi_x\in(a,x)\subseteq(a,a+\delta)$: $\left|\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}-L\right|<\varepsilon$. As $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $\lim_{x\searrow a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=L$. ∎

Derivative, factored.

Exercise 5 (Figalli HS23, question A1, 7 points)

Open the original: HS23 exam with solutions

Let $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be differentiable. (a) [2 points] If $f$ is convex, show $\bigl(f'(y)-f'(x)\bigr)(y-x)\ge0$ for all $x,y$. (b) [2 points] Conversely, if $\bigl(f'(y)-f'(x)\bigr)(y-x)\ge0$ for all $x,y$, show that $f$ is convex. (c) [3 points] If $f$ is convex, show $f(x+h)\ge f(x)+f'(x)h$ for all $x,h\in\mathbb R$.

Solution

Moves: mean value theorem between two points, and Proof C.

(a) By Proof C, $f'$ is increasing. If $x<y$: $f'(y)-f'(x)\ge0$ and $y-x>0$. If $x>y$: both factors are $\le0$. If $x=y$: $0$. In all cases the product is $\ge0$.

(b) For $x<y$, divide by $y-x>0$: $f'(y)\ge f'(x)$. So $f'$ is increasing, and $f$ is convex by Proof C.

(c) $h=0$: equality. $h>0$: by the mean value theorem, $f(x+h)-f(x)=f'(\xi)h$ with $\xi\in(x,x+h)$; $f'$ is increasing, so $f'(\xi)\ge f'(x)$, and multiplying by $h>0$ gives $f(x+h)-f(x)\ge f'(x)h$. $h<0$: $f(x)-f(x+h)=f'(\xi)(-h)$ with $\xi\in(x+h,x)$, and $f'(\xi)\le f'(x)$, so $f(x)-f(x+h)\le-f'(x)h$, which rearranges to the claim. In words: a convex function lies above its tangents.

Exercise 6 (Felder FS23, question 4.A1, 7 points)

Open the original: FS23 exam with solutions

Prove: if $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ is convex and $f(x)\le2023$ for all $x\in\mathbb R$, then $f$ is constant.

Solution

Frame: assume the opposite; find a point where $f>2023$. Moves: assume the opposite compare three slopes split into cases.

Note that $f$ need not be differentiable, so we use Proof B (three slopes), not derivatives. Suppose $f$ is not constant: there are $x<y$ with $f(x)\neq f(y)$.

Case $f(x)<f(y)$. Let $s=\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{y-x}>0$. For $c>y$, Proof B for $x<y<c$ gives $\frac{f(c)-f(y)}{c-y}\ge s$, so

$$f(c)\ge f(y)+s\,(c-y)\to\infty\quad(c\to\infty).$$

So $f(c)>2023$ for large $c$: contradiction.

Case $f(x)>f(y)$. Let $s=\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{y-x}>0$. For $a<x$, Proof B for $a<x<y$ gives $\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}\le\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{y-x}=-s$, so

$$\begin{aligned}&f(a)\ge f(x)+s\,(x-a)\to\infty\\&(a\to-\infty)\end{aligned}.$$

Contradiction again. So $f$ is constant. (The number $2023$ plays no role: any upper bound works. On a half-line it fails: $e^{-x}$ is convex and bounded on $[0,\infty)$.)

Exercise 7 (Kobel-Keller HS24, question 9, 2 points)

Open the original: HS24 exam

Let $f$ be convex and differentiable on the whole compact interval $[a,b]$. Which statement is correct? (a) The global minimum can only be attained at the boundary. (b) $f$ can have several isolated critical points in $(a,b)$. (c) If $f$ has a critical point in $(a,b)$, the global minimum is attained there. (d) It may happen that $f$ attains only its global maximum.

Solution

(c). If $f'(c)=0$, then by the tangent inequality (Exercise 5 (c)) $f(x)\ge f(c)+f'(c)(x-c)=f(c)$ for all $x$: a global minimum. (a) is false: $x^2$ on $[-1,1]$. (b) is false: $f'$ is increasing, so if $f'(c_1)=f'(c_2)=0$ with $c_1<c_2$, then $f'=0$ on all of $[c_1,c_2]$, and the critical points are not isolated. (d) is false: $f$ is continuous on a compact interval, so it attains both its maximum and its minimum (lesson 8).

Exercise 8 (four minimum questions: Felder FS23 1.A1, PVK MC1, Figalli HS23 MC4 and FS26 MC4)

Open the original: FS23 exam with solutions · HS23 exam with solutions · FS26 exam

Find: (a) $\min\{x\log x\mid x>0\}$; (b) the minimum of $x+\frac4x$ on $(0,\infty)$; (c) the minimum of $x^4-2x^2+5$ on $\mathbb R$; (d) the minimum of $2x^2+\frac1{8x^2}-2$ on $(0,\infty)$.

Solution

(a) $(x\log x)'=\log x+1=0$ at $x=\frac1e$; negative before, positive after. Minimum $\frac1e\log\frac1e=-\frac1e$.

(b) $1-\frac4{x^2}=0$ at $x=2$; negative before, positive after. Minimum $2+2=4$. (Or AM–GM: $x+\frac4x\ge2\sqrt{x\cdot\frac4x}=4$.)

(c) $4x^3-4x=4x(x-1)(x+1)=0$ at $0,\pm1$. The function tends to $\infty$ at $\pm\infty$; values $f(0)=5$, $f(\pm1)=4$. Minimum $4$, at $\pm1$.

(d) $$\begin{aligned}&4x-\frac1{4x^3}=0\\&\iff x^4=\frac1{16}\iff x=\frac12;\end{aligned}$$negative before, positive after. Minimum $2\cdot\frac14+\frac1{8\cdot\frac14}-2=\frac12+\frac12-2=-1$.

Exercise 9 (limits from the older Einsiedler exams and Kobel-Keller HS24)

Open the original: HS24 exam

Compute: (a) $\lim_{x\to0}x\tan\left(x+\frac\pi2\right)$ (2017 summer, 1c); (b) $\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{arcosh}(x)}{\log(x-1)}$ (2018 summer, 1c); (c) $\lim_{x\searrow0}x^{\sqrt{4+x}-2}$ (2012 winter, 2a); (d) $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x-x+x^3/6}{x^5}$ (Kobel-Keller HS24, question 12, 3 points); (e) $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{2\sin x-\sin(2x)}{x-\sin x}$ (Übung 8.51).

Solution

(a) $\tan\left(x+\frac\pi2\right)=\frac{\cos x}{-\sin x}$ (addition formulas), so $$x\tan\left(x+\frac\pi2\right)=-\cos x\cdot\frac x{\sin x}\to-1.$$

(b) “$\frac\infty\infty$”. l'Hôpital with the table in section 7:

$$\frac{1/\sqrt{x^2-1}}{1/(x-1)}=\frac{x-1}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}=\sqrt{\frac{x-1}{x+1}}\to1.$$

(c) “$0^0$”. The exponent: $$\begin{aligned}\left(\sqrt{4+x}-2\right)\log x&=\frac{x}{\sqrt{4+x}+2}\log x\\&=\frac{x\log x}{\sqrt{4+x}+2}\to\frac04\\&=0\end{aligned},$$using $x\log x\to0$ (Worked example 4). So the limit is $e^0=1$.

(d) Series: $\sin x=x-\frac{x^3}6+\frac{x^5}{120}-\dots$, so the numerator is $\frac{x^5}{120}+O(x^7)$, and the limit is $\frac1{120}$. (l'Hôpital works too, but needs five steps.)

(e) Series: numerator $$\begin{aligned}2\left(x-\frac{x^3}6\right)-\left(2x-\frac{8x^3}6\right)+O(x^5)&=x^3+O(x^5);\end{aligned}$$denominator $\frac{x^3}6+O(x^5)$. Limit $6$.

Exercise 10 (2011 summer exam, question 4(b))

Open the original: 2011 summer exam

Let $f:[1,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ satisfy $f(1)=1$ and $f'(x)=\frac1{x^2+f(x)^2}$ for $x\ge1$. Show that $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)$ exists and is at most $1+\frac\pi4$.

Solution

Frame: increasing and bounded above ⇒ limit. Moves: mean value theorem between two points (through lesson 17's Proof H) a helper function approach a supremum.

Increasing. $f'>0$, so $f$ is strictly increasing on the interval $[1,\infty)$, and $f(x)\ge f(1)=1$.

Bounded. Hence $f'(x)\le\frac1{x^2+1}$. The helper $h(x)=f(x)-\arctan x$ has $h'(x)=f'(x)-\frac1{1+x^2}\le0$, so $h$ is decreasing: $h(x)\le h(1)=1-\frac\pi4$. So $$\begin{aligned}f(x)&\le 1-\frac\pi4+\arctan x\\&<1-\frac\pi4+\frac\pi2\\&=1+\frac\pi4\end{aligned}.$$

Limit. Let $S=\sup_{x\ge1}f(x)\le1+\frac\pi4$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, there is $x_\varepsilon$ with $f(x_\varepsilon)>S-\varepsilon$, and since $f$ is increasing, $S-\varepsilon<f(x)\le S$ for all $x\ge x_\varepsilon$. So $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=S\le1+\frac\pi4$.

Bonus: a limit of derivatives is a derivative (Übung 8.52)

Let $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ be continuous, differentiable on $[a,b]\setminus\{x_0\}$, and suppose $A=\lim_{x\to x_0}f'(x)$ exists. Show that $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$ with $f'(x_0)=A$ (so $f'$ is continuous at $x_0$).

Solution

Moves: mean value theorem between two points.

Let $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $\delta>0$ with $|f'(\xi)-A|<\varepsilon$ for $0<|\xi-x_0|<\delta$. For $x\in[a,b]$ with $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$, $f$ is continuous between $x$ and $x_0$ and differentiable strictly between them, so the mean value theorem gives $\xi_x$ strictly between $x$ and $x_0$ with

$$\begin{aligned}&\frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0}=f'(\xi_x)\\&0<|\xi_x-x_0|<\delta\end{aligned}.$$

So the difference quotient is within $\varepsilon$ of $A$: $f'(x_0)=A$. This is the tool for “glued” functions: for $g(x)=x|x|$ (lesson 17, Worked example 5), $g'(x)=2|x|\to0$, so $g'(0)=0$ without computing a limit by hand. It also shows again why $x^2\sin\frac1x$ needed a hand computation: there $\lim f'$ doesn't exist.

9. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

A rule bought for money. In 1694 the Marquis de l'Hôpital, a French nobleman, signed an agreement with the young Johann Bernoulli: a regular payment in exchange for Bernoulli's mathematical discoveries, to use as he pleased. In 1696 l'Hôpital published the first calculus textbook, Analyse des infiniment petits, and the rule appeared in it. He thanked Bernoulli in the preface, but the rule carries his name. Bernoulli's later complaints were dismissed for a long time, until their letters were published in the 20th century and proved him right.

Light solves a minimum problem. Fermat's principle says that light travels along the path that takes the least time. Light going from air into water changes speed, so the fastest path is bent. Setting the derivative of the travel time to zero gives exactly Snell's law of refraction, $\frac{\sin\alpha_1}{v_1}=\frac{\sin\alpha_2}{v_2}$ (Beispiel 8.54 in the script). A lifeguard running along the beach and then swimming to someone in the water solves the same problem.

Convexity everywhere. Jensen's inequality for the concave function $-x\log x$ says that among all probability distributions on $n$ outcomes, the uniform one has the largest entropy $-\sum p_k\log p_k$. That's the starting point of information theory (Shannon, 1948), which is behind data compression, and of the statistical physics in Physics courses. Convex optimisation, minimising convex functions, is what most machine learning does: there, every local minimum is global (Worked example 3).
