Aufgabe 1 · Deforming a metric

Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space. Show that $d_{1/2}(x,y)=\sqrt{d(x,y)}$ and $\tilde d(x,y)=\dfrac{d(x,y)}{1+d(x,y)}$ are metrics on $X$.

You need

The three metric axioms (lesson 10). Positivity and symmetry are immediate; all the work is the triangle inequality.

Hint 1

Show $\sqrt{r+s}\le\sqrt r+\sqrt s$ by squaring. For $\tilde d$, use $\varphi(t)=\frac t{1+t}$: it is increasing and $\varphi(r+s)\le\varphi(r)+\varphi(s)$.

Solution

Both are $\ge0$, symmetric, and $0$ exactly when $d(x,y)=0$, i.e. $x=y$. Triangle inequalities:

$d_{1/2}$. For $r,s\ge0$: $(\sqrt r+\sqrt s)^2=r+s+2\sqrt{rs}\ge r+s$, so $\sqrt{r+s}\le\sqrt r+\sqrt s$. Hence $d_{1/2}(x,z)=\sqrt{d(x,z)}\le\sqrt{d(x,y)+d(y,z)}\le d_{1/2}(x,y)+d_{1/2}(y,z)$ (the first step since $\sqrt{\cdot}$ is increasing).

$\tilde d$. $\varphi(t)=\frac t{1+t}$ is increasing: $\varphi(s)-\varphi(r)=\frac{s-r}{(1+s)(1+r)}\ge0$ for $s\ge r$. And $\varphi(r+s)=\frac r{1+r+s}+\frac s{1+r+s}\le\frac r{1+r}+\frac s{1+s}$. So $\tilde d(x,z)=\varphi(d(x,z))\le\varphi(d(x,y)+d(y,z))\le\tilde d(x,y)+\tilde d(y,z)$. ∎

Aufgabe 2 · The integral as a function of its upper limit

Let $f$ be Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$ and $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$. Show that $F$ is continuous. Is it uniformly continuous, or even Lipschitz?

You need

Integrable functions are bounded, plus additivity and monotonicity of the integral (lesson 9). Lipschitz ⇒ uniformly continuous ⇒ continuous.

Hint 1

$|f|\le M$. Estimate $F(y)-F(x)=\int_x^yf$ for $x<y$.

Solution

$f$ is bounded, say $|f|\le M$. For $a\le x<y\le b$, additivity and monotonicity give $-M(y-x)\le F(y)-F(x)=\int_x^yf(t)\,dt\le M(y-x)$. So $|F(y)-F(x)|\le M|y-x|$ for all $x,y$: $F$ is Lipschitz with constant $M$, hence uniformly continuous (take $\delta=\frac\varepsilon{M+1}$) and continuous. No continuity of $f$ is needed. ∎

Aufgabe 3 · Integral norm and sup norm

On $C([a,b])$ let $\|f\|=\int_a^b|f(x)|\,dx$. 1. Show it is a norm. 2. Find $C$ with $\|f\|\le C\|f\|_\infty$. Is there $K$ with $\|f\|_\infty\le K\|f\|$ for all $f$?

You need

Norm axioms (lesson 10). Definiteness is the only delicate one: a continuous $f\ne0$ is $>c>0$ in absolute value on a small interval.

Hint 1

For “no $K$”: find continuous $f_n$ with $\|f_n\|_\infty=1$ but $\|f_n\|\to0$ (thin spikes).

Solution

1. $|f|$ is continuous, hence integrable, so $\|f\|$ is defined and $\ge0$. Definite: if $f\ne0$, pick $x_0$ with $c:=\frac{|f(x_0)|}2>0$; by continuity $|f|>c$ on some $[u,v]\subseteq[a,b]$ with $u<v$, so $\|f\|\ge c(v-u)>0$. Homogeneous: $\|\lambda f\|=|\lambda|\,\|f\|$. Triangle: $\|f+h\|\le\int(|f|+|h|)=\|f\|+\|h\|$.

2. $|f|\le\|f\|_\infty$ gives $\|f\|\le(b-a)\|f\|_\infty$; $C=b-a$ (optimal: $f\equiv1$).
No $K$ exists. Let $\ell=b-a$ and $f_n(x)=\max\{1-\frac{n(x-a)}\ell,0\}$: continuous, $f_n(a)=1$, zero on $[a+\frac\ell n,b]$. Then $\|f_n\|_\infty=1$ and $\|f_n\|\le\frac\ell n$. A constant $K$ would give $1\le K\frac\ell n$ for all $n$, impossible for $n>K\ell$. So the two norms are not equivalent. ∎

Aufgabe 4 · Metrics and convergence

  1. If $\frac1Cd_1\le d_2\le Cd_1$ for some $C>0$, show a sequence converges for $d_1$ iff it converges for $d_2$, with the same limit.
  2. Find two metrics on $\mathbb R$ with the same convergent sequences but no such constant.
You need

Convergence in a metric space: $d(x_n,x)\to0$ (lesson 10). For 2: a metric that “caps” distances at $1$.

Hint 1

2: try $d_1=|x-y|$ and $d_2=\min\{1,|x-y|\}$. Small distances are the same; only large ones differ.

Solution

1. $d_2(x_n,x)\le Cd_1(x_n,x)$ and $d_1(x_n,x)\le Cd_2(x_n,x)$. So $d_1(x_n,x)\to0\iff d_2(x_n,x)\to0$, for every fixed $x$: same convergent sequences, same limits.

2. $d_1(x,y)=|x-y|$, $d_2(x,y)=\min\{1,|x-y|\}$. $d_2$ is a metric: the only non-obvious part, $\min\{1,r+s\}\le\min\{1,r\}+\min\{1,s\}$, holds since the right side is $\ge1$ if one term is capped, and $=r+s$ otherwise. Same convergence: $d_2\le d_1$; and once $d_2(x_n,x)<1$, $d_1(x_n,x)=d_2(x_n,x)$. No constant: $d_1(0,t)\le Cd_2(0,t)$ would give $t\le C$ for all $t$. ∎

Aufgabe 5 · Mean value theorem for integrals

Let $f$ be continuous and $g\ge0$ Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$. 1. Show $fg$ is integrable. 2. Show there is $\xi\in[a,b]$ with $\int_a^bfg=f(\xi)\int_a^bg$. 3. Does 2. still hold without $g\ge0$?

You need

The ε-criterion (lesson 9), the extreme value theorem (lesson 8) and the IVT (lesson 7). Part 2 is used for the Lagrange remainder in lesson 21.

Hint 1

2: with $m=\min f$, $M=\max f$: $mg\le fg\le Mg$, so $m\int g\le\int fg\le M\int g$. Divide by $\int g$ (if it is not $0$) and use the IVT.

Hint 2

1: shift $f$ to $F=f+A\ge0$. For non-negative step functions $u_i\le v_i$: $v_1v_2-u_1u_2=v_1(v_2-u_2)+u_2(v_1-u_1)$. 3: try $f(x)=g(x)=x$ on $[-1,1]$.

Solution

1. Let $A=\max|f|$ and $F=f+A\ge0$, with $0\le F\le M_1$, $0\le g\le M_2$. Given $\eta>0$ there are step functions $0\le u_1\le F\le v_1\le M_1$, $0\le u_2\le g\le v_2\le M_2$ with $J(v_i)-J(u_i)<\eta$ (cut off at $0$ and $M_i$ if needed). Then $u_1u_2\le Fg\le v_1v_2$ are step functions and $$v_1v_2-u_1u_2=v_1(v_2-u_2)+u_2(v_1-u_1)\le M_1(v_2-u_2)+M_2(v_1-u_1),$$ so $J(v_1v_2)-J(u_1u_2)<(M_1+M_2)\eta$. Choosing $\eta$ small gives the ε-criterion: $Fg$ is integrable, and so is $fg=Fg-Ag$.

2. Let $m=\min f$, $M=\max f$ (extreme value theorem). As $g\ge0$: $mg\le fg\le Mg$, so $mG\le H\le MG$ with $G=\int g\ge0$, $H=\int fg$. If $G=0$ then $H=0$ and any $\xi$ works. If $G>0$, then $\frac HG\in[m,M]$, and the IVT between a min and a max point of $f$ gives $\xi$ with $f(\xi)=\frac HG$.

3. No. On $[-1,1]$ take $f(x)=g(x)=x$. Then $\int_{-1}^1g=0$, so the right side is $0$ for every $\xi$, but $\int_{-1}^1x^2\,dx=\frac23>0$. ∎

Aufgabe 6 · Functions of bounded variation

$f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ has bounded variation if the sums $\sum_i|f(x_i)-f(x_{i-1})|$ over all partitions of $[a,b]$ are bounded. Let $V(x)$ be the supremum of these sums over partitions of $[a,x]$ ($V(a)=0$). Show that for $a\le x<x'\le b$: $$|f(x')-f(x)|+V(x)\le V(x'),$$ by extending a partition of $[a,x]$ to one of $[a,x']$. Conclude that $V$ and $V-f$ are increasing.

You need

Taking a supremum over an inequality that holds for every element (lesson 6). The point of the task: $f=V-(V-f)$ is a difference of monotone functions, so it is integrable (lesson 9, Proof E).

Hint 1

Take any partition of $[a,x]$ and add the point $x'$. The new sum is the old sum plus $|f(x')-f(x)|$, and it is $\le V(x')$. Now take the sup over the old partitions.

Solution

Let $Z=\{a=x_0<\dots<x_n=x\}$ be any partition of $[a,x]$. Adding $x'$ gives a partition of $[a,x']$, so $$\sum_{i=1}^n|f(x_i)-f(x_{i-1})|+|f(x')-f(x)|\le V(x').$$ The last term does not depend on $Z$; taking the supremum over all $Z$ gives $V(x)+|f(x')-f(x)|\le V(x')$. (For $x=a$ use the partition $\{a,x'\}$ and $V(a)=0$.) The same argument shows $V(x)\le V(b)<\infty$.

Monotonicity. Directly, $V(x')\ge V(x)$. For $W=V-f$: $$W(x')-W(x)=V(x')-V(x)-\big(f(x')-f(x)\big)\ge|f(x')-f(x)|-\big(f(x')-f(x)\big)\ge0.$$ So $V$ and $V-f$ are increasing, and $f=V-(V-f)$ is a difference of two increasing functions; hence it is Riemann integrable. ∎
