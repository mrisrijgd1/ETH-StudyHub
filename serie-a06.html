Übungsblatt 6, explained

New metrics from old ones, the integral as a function of its upper limit, two norms on continuous functions, when two metrics have the same convergent sequences, the mean value theorem for integrals, and functions of bounded variation. For every task: the idea in plain words, up to three hints that each give away a little more, then the solution with every step explained next to what you write, and the trap people fall into.

How to use this page.

  1. Read the task and try it on paper for 10 minutes.
  2. Stuck on where to start? Read The idea and open You need.
  3. Still stuck? Open Hint 1, try again, then Hint 2, then Hint 3. Hint 3 is almost the whole proof.
  4. Only then open the solution. The left column says why each step is there, the right column is what you write.

The solutions follow the official Lösungsvorschläge. Script pages refer to the German Einsiedler–Wieser script (printed page numbers). Notation as in the script: $J(s)$ is the integral of a step function $s$, $\underline I$ and $\overline I$ are the lower and upper integral. In Aufgaben 2, 3 and 5, $a<b$. Aufgabe 1 · Deforming a metric

Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space. Show that $$d_{1/2}(x,y):=\sqrt{d(x,y)}$$ and $$\tilde d(x,y):=\frac{d(x,y)}{1+d(x,y)}$$ define metrics on $X$.

The idea

Both new distances are $\varphi(d)$ for a function $\varphi$ that starts at $0$, increases, and bends downwards (like $\sqrt t$ and $\frac t{1+t}$). Such a $\varphi$ is subadditive: $\varphi(r+s)\le\varphi(r)+\varphi(s)$. Then the triangle inequality survives: first use it for $d$, then push it through $\varphi$. The other two axioms are immediate, because $\varphi(t)=0$ only for $t=0$.

$t$ runs from $0$ to $4$. The dashed line $t$ adds up exactly. The curves $\sqrt t$ (accent) and $\frac t{1+t}$ start at $0$, increase, and bend downwards, so the value at $r+s$ is at most the value at $r$ plus the value at $s$.

You need

The metric axioms: $d(x,y)\ge0$ with equality iff $x=y$; symmetry; triangle inequality (script, page 230; lesson 10). This task is the script's exercise “Deformation der Metrik” on page 233.

Hint 1

Positivity and symmetry come straight from $d$. For the triangle inequality, prove a statement about numbers first: for $r,s\ge0$, $\sqrt{r+s}\le\sqrt r+\sqrt s$, and $\varphi(r+s)\le\varphi(r)+\varphi(s)$ with $\varphi(t)=\frac t{1+t}$.

Hint 2

Square: $$\begin{aligned}(\sqrt r+\sqrt s)^2&=r+s+2\sqrt{rs}\\&\ge r+s.\end{aligned}$$ For $\varphi$: $\frac{r+s}{1+r+s}=\frac r{1+r+s}+\frac s{1+r+s}$, and making a denominator smaller makes the fraction bigger. You also need that both functions are increasing.

Hint 3

Chain: $$\begin{aligned}\varphi(d(x,z))&\le\varphi\big(d(x,y)+d(y,z)\big)\\&\le\varphi(d(x,y))+\varphi(d(y,z)).\end{aligned}$$ The first step uses that $\varphi$ is increasing, the second that it is subadditive.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

The easy axioms carry over directly.

Both maps take finite non-negative values and are symmetric, since $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$. Moreover $d_{1/2}(x,y)=0$ holds iff $d(x,y)=0$, i.e. iff $x=y$; the same holds for $\tilde d$. It remains to prove the triangle inequalities.

A fact about numbers: the square root of a sum is at most the sum of the roots. Square both sides (both are $\ge0$).

$d_{1/2}$. For $r,s\ge0$: $$\begin{aligned}(\sqrt r+\sqrt s)^2&=r+s+2\sqrt{rs}\\&\ge r+s.\end{aligned}$$ Both sides being non-negative, $\sqrt{r+s}\le\sqrt r+\sqrt s$.

Triangle inequality for $d$, then the root is increasing, then the number fact.

For $x,y,z\in X$: $$\begin{aligned}&d_{1/2}(x,z)=\sqrt{d(x,z)}\\&\le\sqrt{d(x,y)+d(y,z)}\\&\le\sqrt{d(x,y)}+\sqrt{d(y,z)}\\&=d_{1/2}(x,y)+d_{1/2}(y,z).\end{aligned}$$

For $\tilde d$: $\varphi(t)=\frac t{1+t}$ is increasing…

$\tilde d$. Let $\varphi(t):=\frac t{1+t}$ for $t\ge0$. For $0\le r\le s$: $$\varphi(s)-\varphi(r)=\frac{s-r}{(1+s)(1+r)},$$ which is $\ge0$. So $\varphi$ is increasing.

…and subadditive: split the fraction and shrink each denominator.

For $r,s\ge0$: $$\begin{aligned}&\varphi(r+s)\\&=\frac r{1+r+s}+\frac s{1+r+s}\\&\le\frac r{1+r}+\frac s{1+s}\\&=\varphi(r)+\varphi(s).\end{aligned}$$

Same chain as before.

Hence for $x,y,z\in X$: $$\begin{aligned}\tilde d(x,z)&=\varphi(d(x,z))\\&\le\varphi\big(d(x,y)+d(y,z)\big)\\&\le\varphi(d(x,y))+\varphi(d(y,z))\\&=\tilde d(x,y)+\tilde d(y,z).\end{aligned}$$ So $d_{1/2}$ and $\tilde d$ are metrics on $X$. ∎

Trap

Using only subadditivity. The first step $\varphi(d(x,z))\le\varphi(d(x,y)+d(y,z))$ needs $\varphi$ to be increasing; say it and prove it. (Not every function works: $d^2$ is not a metric in general, e.g. on $\mathbb R$: $(0-2)^2=4>1+1$.)

Aufgabe 2 · The partial integral

Let $f\in\mathcal R([a,b])$ be Riemann integrable. Show that $F:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$, $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$, is continuous. Is $F$ also uniformly continuous, or even Lipschitz continuous? (Lipschitz: there is $L\ge0$ with $|H(x)-H(y)|\le L|x-y|$ for all $x,y$.)

The idea

$F(y)-F(x)$ is the integral of $f$ over the strip from $x$ to $y$. An integrable function is bounded, say $|f|\le M$, so that strip has area at most $M\cdot|y-x|$. That is the Lipschitz inequality with $L=M$, and Lipschitz is stronger than uniformly continuous, which is stronger than continuous. Notice: $f$ itself may jump; $F$ is still continuous.

Even with a jump in $f$, the area between $x$ and $y$ is at most $M(y-x)$.

You need

Riemann integrable functions are bounded (by definition, script, page 183). Integrability on subintervals and additivity $\int_a^y=\int_a^x+\int_x^y$ (script, pages 192–194; this task is the exercise on page 194). Monotonicity: $-M\le f\le M$ gives $-M(y-x)\le\int_x^yf\le M(y-x)$ (script, page 190; lesson 9, Proof D). Uniform continuity: script, page 167; Lipschitz: page 169.

Hint 1

Let $M\ge0$ with $|f|\le M$. For $x<y$, write $F(y)-F(x)$ as one integral.

Hint 2

$F(y)-F(x)=\int_x^yf(t)\,dt$. Bound it from both sides using $-M\le f\le M$.

Hint 3

$|F(y)-F(x)|\le M|y-x|$. For uniform continuity, $\delta=\frac\varepsilon{M+1}$ works for all $x,y$ at once.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Integrable functions are bounded, and restrictions to subintervals are integrable, so $F$ makes sense.

Since $f$ is Riemann integrable, it is bounded: $|f(t)|\le M$ for some $M\ge0$ and all $t\in[a,b]$. Its restriction to each subinterval is integrable, so $F$ is well defined, with $F(a)=0$.

The difference is the integral over the strip; bound it by the strip's height times width.

For $a\le x<y\le b$, additivity and monotonicity give $$\begin{aligned}-M(y-x)&\le F(y)-F(x)\\&=\int_x^yf(t)\,dt\\&\le M(y-x).\end{aligned}$$

Swapping $x,y$ gives the same for $x>y$: that's Lipschitz.

Exchanging $x$ and $y$, we get $|F(y)-F(x)|\le M|y-x|$ for all $x,y\in[a,b]$. So $F$ is Lipschitz continuous with constant $M$.

Lipschitz ⇒ uniformly continuous, with one $\delta$ for all points.

In particular $F$ is uniformly continuous: for $\varepsilon>0$, $\delta:=\frac\varepsilon{M+1}>0$ gives $|F(y)-F(x)|\le M\delta<\varepsilon$ whenever $|x-y|<\delta$. Hence $F$ is also continuous. ∎

Note. Neither continuity of $f$ nor differentiability of $F$ is needed. (Why $M+1$ and not $M$: $M$ could be $0$.)

Trap

Using the fundamental theorem of calculus (“$F'=f$, so $F$ is continuous”). That needs $f$ continuous, which isn't assumed, and it isn't available yet anyway. Boundedness of $f$ is all you need.

Aufgabe 3 · Integral norm and supremum norm

On the real vector space $C([a,b])$ of continuous $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ let $\|f\|:=\int_a^b|f(x)|\,dx$.

  1. Show that $\|\cdot\|$ is a norm on $C([a,b])$.
  2. How does it relate to $\|f\|_\infty:=\sup_{x\in[a,b]}|f(x)|$? Find a constant $C>0$ depending only on $a,b$ with $\|f\|\le C\|f\|_\infty$ for all $f$. Is there a constant $K>0$ independent of $f$ with $\|f\|_\infty\le K\|f\|$ for all $f$? Justify.
The idea

$\|f\|$ is the area between the graph of $|f|$ and the axis; $\|f\|_\infty$ is the height of the highest point. Area $\le$ width $\times$ height, so $\|f\|\le(b-a)\|f\|_\infty$. The other way fails: a very thin, tall spike has height $1$ but almost no area. The only subtle axiom is definiteness: a continuous function that is non-zero somewhere is non-zero on a whole little interval, which has positive area.

$\|f_n\|_\infty=1$ for every $n$, but the area $\|f_n\|=\frac{b-a}{2n}$ goes to $0$.

You need

Norm axioms (script, page 225; lesson 10); the sup norm and the 1-norm on $C([a,b])$ are the script's examples on page 229. Continuous functions are integrable (script, page 205), $|f|$ is integrable and the integral is monotone and linear (script, pages 188–190).

Hint 1

1: Homogeneity and triangle inequality follow from $|\lambda f|=|\lambda||f|$, $|f+h|\le|f|+|h|$ and the integral rules. For definiteness: if $f(x_0)\ne0$, use continuity to find an interval where $|f|>c>0$.

Hint 2

Definiteness: the step function $c\,\mathbb 1_{[u,v]}$ lies below $|f|$, so $\|f\|\ge c(v-u)>0$. 2: compare $|f|$ with the constant upper step function $\|f\|_\infty$.

Hint 3

For the “no $K$” part, take $f_n(x)=\max\{1-\frac{n(x-a)}{b-a},0\}$: height $1$ at $a$, zero from $a+\frac{b-a}n$ on. Bound $\|f_n\|$ by the upper step function $\mathbb 1_{[a,a+(b-a)/n]}$.

Solution

1. A norm.

Why this stepWhat you write

Well defined: $|f|$ is continuous, hence integrable.

For $f\in C([a,b])$, $|f|$ is continuous and therefore Riemann integrable, so $\|f\|$ is a well-defined real number, and $\|f\|\ge0$ since $|f|\ge0$. Also $\|0\|=0$.

Definiteness: a non-zero continuous function stays away from $0$ on a whole small interval, and that interval contributes positive area.

Let $f\ne0$, say $c:=\frac{|f(x_0)|}2>0$. By continuity $|f(x)|>c$ for all $x$ in a relative neighbourhood of $x_0$, which (as $a<b$) contains an interval $[u,v]\subseteq[a,b]$ with $u<v$, one-sided if $x_0$ is an endpoint. The step function $s:=c\,\mathbb 1_{[u,v]}\le|f|$ gives $$\begin{aligned}\|f\|&=\underline I(|f|)\ge J(s)\\&=c(v-u)>0.\end{aligned}$$ So $\|f\|=0\iff f=0$.

Constants come out of the integral.

For $\lambda\in\mathbb R$: $|\lambda f(x)|=|\lambda||f(x)|$, so by linearity $\|\lambda f\|=|\lambda|\,\|f\|$.

Pointwise triangle inequality, then monotonicity and linearity of the integral.

For $f,h\in C([a,b])$: $|f+h|\le|f|+|h|$ pointwise, so $$\begin{aligned}\|f+h\|&\le\int_a^b\big(|f|+|h|\big)\,dx\\&=\|f\|+\|h\|.\end{aligned}$$ Hence $\|\cdot\|$ is a norm.

2. Comparison.

Area $\le$ width $\times$ height: the constant $\|f\|_\infty$ is an upper step function for $|f|$.

Since $f$ is continuous on the compact $[a,b]$, $\|f\|_\infty=\max|f|<\infty$. The constant step function $o:=\|f\|_\infty$ satisfies $|f|\le o$, so $$\|f\|\le(b-a)\|f\|_\infty$$ for all $f\in C([a,b])$. So $C=b-a$ works, and it is optimal: for $f\equiv1$, $\|f\|=b-a$ and $\|f\|_\infty=1$.

The other way: thinner and thinner spikes of height $1$.

Let $\ell:=b-a$ and $f_n(x):=\max\big\{1-\frac{n(x-a)}\ell,0\big\}$ for $n\ge1$. Each $f_n$ is continuous, $f_n(a)=1$, $0\le f_n\le1$, and $f_n=0$ on $[a+\frac\ell n,b]$. So $\|f_n\|_\infty=1$.

Their area tends to $0$: they fit under a step function of width $\frac\ell n$.

The upper step function $o_n:=\mathbb 1_{[a,a+\ell/n]}$ satisfies $0\le f_n\le o_n$, so $0\le\|f_n\|\le J(o_n)=\frac\ell n\to0$.

A constant $K$ would force $1\le\frac{K\ell}n$ for all $n$: impossible.

If there were $K>0$ with $\|f\|_\infty\le K\|f\|$ for all $f$, then for every $n$: $$1=\|f_n\|_\infty\le K\|f_n\|\le\frac{K\ell}n,$$ which fails for $n>K\ell$. So no such $K$ exists: the two norms are not equivalent. ∎

Consequence for convergence. Uniform convergence $h_n\to h$ implies $$\begin{aligned}\|h_n-h\|&\le(b-a)\|h_n-h\|_\infty\\&\to0.\end{aligned}$$ The converse fails: $\|f_n\|\to0$ but $\|f_n\|_\infty=1$ for all $n$.

Trap

In definiteness, writing “$\int|f|=0$ implies $|f|=0$” without proof. This is exactly where continuity is used: for a non-continuous function it is false (change $0$ at one point to $1$; the integral stays $0$).

Aufgabe 4 · Metrics and convergence

Let $X$ be a set and $d_1,d_2$ two metrics on $X$.

  1. Suppose there is $C>0$ such that for all $x,y\in X$ $$\begin{aligned}\tfrac1C\,d_1(x,y)&\le d_2(x,y)\\&\le C\,d_1(x,y).\end{aligned}$$ Call this (5.4). Show that a sequence converges with respect to $d_1$ iff it converges with respect to $d_2$, and that the limits then agree.
  2. Find two metrics on $X=\mathbb R$ with the same convergent sequences (in the sense above) for which no constant as in (5.4) exists.
The idea

Convergence only asks whether distances to the limit go to $0$. If one distance is at most $C$ times the other, “goes to $0$” transfers. But (5.4) asks for much more than needed: it controls all distances, also huge ones, while convergence only cares about small ones. So cap the usual distance at $1$: small distances stay the same (same convergence), but large distances are squashed, so no constant can compare them.

You need

Convergence in a metric space: $x_n\to x$ iff $d(x_n,x)\to0$ (script, page 236; lesson 10). Limits are unique (lesson 10, Proof B). Metric axioms: script, page 230.

Hint 1

1: Fix a point $x$ and compare $d_2(x_n,x)$ with $d_1(x_n,x)$ using (5.4) in both directions. 2: Try $d_1(x,y)=|x-y|$ and $d_2(x,y)=\min\{1,|x-y|\}$.

Hint 2

2: For the triangle inequality of $d_2$, show for $r,s\ge0$: $$\begin{aligned}\min\{1,r+s\}&\le\min\{1,r\}\\&\quad+\min\{1,s\}.\end{aligned}$$ For convergence: once $d_2(x_n,x)<1$, $d_1$ and $d_2$ agree.

Hint 3

2: If (5.4) held, then for large $t$: $\frac tC=\frac1Cd_1(0,t)\le d_2(0,t)=1$. Take $t>C$.

Solution

1.

Why this stepWhat you write

Read (5.4) as two bounds, one for each direction.

Let $(x_n)_n$ be a sequence in $X$ and $x\in X$. By (5.4), $$\begin{aligned}d_2(x_n,x)&\le C\,d_1(x_n,x),\\d_1(x_n,x)&\le C\,d_2(x_n,x).\end{aligned}$$

If one distance goes to $0$, so does $C$ times it, and the other is squeezed below.

If $d_1(x_n,x)\to0$, the first inequality gives $d_2(x_n,x)\to0$; the second gives the converse. So for every $x\in X$: $x_n\to x$ in $d_1$ $\iff$ $x_n\to x$ in $d_2$.

That is a statement for each candidate limit, so the limits agree too.

In particular, the sequence converges in one metric iff it converges in the other, and the limit is the same point. ∎

2. Let $d_1(x,y):=|x-y|$ and $d_2(x,y):=\min\{1,|x-y|\}$ on $\mathbb R$.

$d_2$ is a metric: only the triangle inequality needs work; reduce it to a fact about numbers.

Non-negativity, symmetry and $d_2(x,y)=0\iff x=y$ are clear. For $r,s\ge0$: $$\begin{aligned}\min\{1,r+s\}&\le\min\{1,r\}\\&\quad+\min\{1,s\}\end{aligned}$$ (if one of $r,s$ is $\ge1$, the right side is $\ge1$; otherwise it equals $r+s$). With $r:=|x-y|$, $s:=|y-z|$ and $|x-z|\le r+s$: $$\begin{aligned}d_2(x,z)&\le\min\{1,r+s\}\\&\le d_2(x,y)+d_2(y,z).\end{aligned}$$

Same convergence: $d_2\le d_1$ gives one direction; for the other, small $d_2$-distances are $d_1$-distances.

Since $d_2\le d_1$, $d_1$-convergence implies $d_2$-convergence to the same limit. Conversely, if $d_2(x_n,x)\to0$, then eventually $d_2(x_n,x)<1$, and for those $n$, $d_1(x_n,x)=d_2(x_n,x)$; so $d_1(x_n,x)\to0$.

No constant: large distances are capped at $1$ by $d_2$ but not by $d_1$.

If some $C>0$ satisfied (5.4), then for $t>\max\{1,C\}$: $\frac tC=\frac1Cd_1(0,t)\le d_2(0,t)=1$, contradicting $t>C$. So no such constant exists. ∎

Trap

In 2, forgetting to prove that your $d_2$ is a metric. A “metric” you invent must satisfy all axioms, and the triangle inequality for $\min\{1,|x-y|\}$ needs the short case argument above.

Aufgabe 5 · Mean value theorem for integrals

Let $f\in C([a,b])$ be continuous and $g\in\mathcal R([a,b])$ non-negative and Riemann integrable.

  1. Show that the product $fg$ is Riemann integrable.
  2. Prove the mean value theorem for integrals: there is $\xi\in[a,b]$ with $\displaystyle\int_a^bf(x)g(x)\,dx=f(\xi)\int_a^bg(x)\,dx$.
  3. Does 2 remain true without “$g$ non-negative”?
The idea

2 is the heart: $f$ lies between its minimum $m$ and maximum $M$, and since $g\ge0$, multiplying by $g$ keeps that order: $mg\le fg\le Mg$. Integrate: $\int fg$ is between $m\int g$ and $M\int g$, so it is $\int g$ times some value between $m$ and $M$, and the IVT says $f$ takes that value. For 1, squeeze both factors between step functions and control the product of the squeezes. For 3, a sign-changing $g$ can have $\int g=0$ while $\int fg>0$.

You need

Extreme value theorem and IVT (script, pages 159 and 166). The ε-criterion: bounded $h$ is integrable iff for every $\varepsilon>0$ there are step functions $u\le h\le o$ with $J(o)-J(u)<\varepsilon$ (script, page 184; lesson 9, Proof B). Continuous functions are integrable (script, page 205), and the integral is linear and monotone (script, pages 188–190).

Hint 1

1: First make $f$ non-negative: $F:=f+A\ge0$ with $A:=\max|f|$. Then $fg=Fg-Ag$. Squeeze $u_1\le F\le v_1$, $u_2\le g\le v_2$ with all four between $0$ and bounds $M_1$, $M_2$.

Hint 2

1: Then $u_1u_2\le Fg\le v_1v_2$ (non-negativity!) and $$\begin{aligned}&v_1v_2-u_1u_2\\&=v_1(v_2-u_2)+u_2(v_1-u_1).\end{aligned}$$ 2: Let $G=\int g$, $H=\int fg$. Show $mG\le H\le MG$, and treat $G=0$ separately.

Hint 3

2: If $G>0$, $\frac HG\in[m,M]$; the IVT between a minimum point and a maximum point gives $\xi$. 3: On $[-1,1]$ take $f(x)=g(x)=x$.

Solution

1. $fg$ is integrable.

Why this stepWhat you write

Shift $f$ up to be non-negative; products of non-negative functions keep inequalities.

Let $A:=\max_{[a,b]}|f|$ and $F:=f+A\ge0$; $F$ is continuous, hence integrable. Choose $M_1,M_2>0$ with $0\le F\le M_1$ and $0\le g\le M_2$.

Squeeze each factor by step functions; clip them to $[0,M_i]$, which only makes the squeeze tighter.

Let $\eta>0$. By the ε-criterion there are step functions $u_1\le F\le v_1$, $u_2\le g\le v_2$ with $J(v_i)-J(u_i)<\eta$. Replacing $u_i$ by $\max\{u_i,0\}$ and $v_i$ by $\min\{v_i,M_i\}$ we may assume $0\le u_i\le v_i\le M_i$; the differences don't grow.

Products of step functions are step functions (use a common refinement), and non-negativity lets us multiply the inequalities.

Products of step functions are step functions (on a common refinement), and by non-negativity $u_1u_2\le Fg\le v_1v_2$.

Control the gap of the product by the two gaps.

Since $v_1\le M_1$ and $u_2\le M_2$: $$\begin{aligned}&v_1v_2-u_1u_2\\&=v_1(v_2-u_2)+u_2(v_1-u_1)\\&\le M_1(v_2-u_2)+M_2(v_1-u_1).\end{aligned}$$ By linearity and monotonicity of $J$: $$\begin{aligned}&J(v_1v_2)-J(u_1u_2)\\&<(M_1+M_2)\eta.\end{aligned}$$

Choose $\eta$ small; undo the shift by linearity.

For $\eta<\frac\varepsilon{M_1+M_2}$ this is $<\varepsilon$, so $Fg$ is integrable by the ε-criterion. Since $fg=Fg-Ag$, linearity shows $fg$ is integrable.

2. The mean value theorem.

$f$ attains min and max; multiplying by $g\ge0$ keeps $m\le f\le M$.

By the extreme value theorem, $m:=\min_{[a,b]}f$ and $M:=\max_{[a,b]}f$ exist. Since $g\ge0$: $mg\le fg\le Mg$.

Integrate (monotonicity, linearity).

With $G:=\int_a^bg\,dx\ge0$ and $H:=\int_a^bfg\,dx$, monotonicity gives $mG\le H\le MG$.

Don't divide by $0$: if $G=0$, any $\xi$ works.

If $G=0$, then $H=0$ and every $\xi\in[a,b]$ satisfies $H=f(\xi)G$.

Otherwise $\frac HG$ is a value between min and max, and the IVT says $f$ takes it.

If $G>0$, then $\frac HG\in[m,M]$. The IVT, applied between a minimum point and a maximum point of $f$, gives $\xi\in[a,b]$ with $f(\xi)=\frac HG$, i.e. $\int_a^bfg\,dx=f(\xi)\int_a^bg\,dx$.

3. No.

A $g$ whose positive and negative parts cancel, while $fg$ is a non-zero square.

On $[-1,1]$ let $f(x)=g(x)=x$. By symmetry $\int_{-1}^1g\,dx=0$, so the right side is $0$ for every $\xi$. But $fg=x^2$ is continuous, non-negative and not identically $0$, so $\int_{-1}^1x^2\,dx>0$ (by the definiteness argument in Aufgabe 3). No $\xi$ works. ∎

The two grey areas of $g$ cancel; $x^2$ has positive area.

Trap

Dividing by $\int g$ without checking it is non-zero. And in 1, multiplying inequalities like $u_1\le F$ and $u_2\le g$ without non-negativity: $u_1u_2\le Fg$ can fail for negative factors, which is why $f$ is shifted first.

Aufgabe 6 · Functions of bounded variation

Let $a<b$ and $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$. For a partition $P$: $a=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_n$ write $$S(P):=\sum_{i=1}^n|f(x_i)-f(x_{i-1})|$$ (its variation sum). $f$ has bounded variation if $\sup S(P)<\infty$, the supremum over all partitions of $[a,b]$ (that is, $x_n=b$). For such $f$ let $V(f)(x)$ be the supremum of $S(P)$ over all partitions of $[a,x]$, for $x\in(a,b]$, and $V(f)(a):=0$. Show that for $a\le x<x'\le b$ $$|f(x')-f(x)|+V(f)(x)\le V(f)(x'),$$ by extending an arbitrary partition of $[a,x]$ to one of $[a,x']$. Conclude that $V(f)$ and $V(f)-f$ are increasing. (So $f=V(f)-\big(V(f)-f\big)$ is a difference of two increasing functions, and hence Riemann integrable.)

The idea

$V(f)(x)$ is the total “up and down distance” the graph travels from $a$ to $x$. Going on from $x$ to $x'$ adds at least the direct distance $|f(x')-f(x)|$: that's the inequality. So $V(f)$ only grows, and it grows at least as fast as $f$ does, which is exactly why $V(f)-f$ also never decreases.

Any partition of $[a,x]$ plus the point $x'$ is a partition of $[a,x']$, with one extra term.

You need

Supremum (script, page 105): if every element of a set is $\le s$, then the supremum is $\le s$. Monotone functions are integrable (script, page 199; lesson 9, Proof E), and sums/differences of integrable functions are integrable (script, page 188). This task is the script's exercise on pages 210–211.

Hint 1

Take any partition $a=x_0<\dots<x_n=x$ of $[a,x]$ and append $x'$. What is the variation sum of the new partition, and why is it $\le V(f)(x')$?

Hint 2

You get $S(P)+|f(x')-f(x)|\le V(f)(x')$ for every partition $P$ of $[a,x]$. Now take the supremum over these partitions. Treat $x=a$ separately.

Hint 3

Write $V:=V(f)$ and $\Delta:=f(x')-f(x)$. Then $V(x')-V(x)\ge|\Delta|\ge0$. For $W:=V-f$: $W(x')-W(x)\ge|\Delta|-\Delta\ge0$.

Solution

Write $V:=V(f)$ and $T:=V(b)<\infty$.

Why this stepWhat you write

First, $V(x)$ is finite: every partition of $[a,x]$ extends to one of $[a,b]$, and the extra term is $\ge0$.

For $a<x<b$, every partition of $[a,x]$ extends to a partition of $[a,b]$ by appending $b$. The extra summand is $\ge0$, so every variation sum over $[a,x]$ is $\le T$. Hence $0\le V(x)\le T$ for all $x\in[a,b]$.

Extend an arbitrary partition of $[a,x]$ by $x'$; its variation sum is one of the sums in the sup defining $V(x')$.

Let $a<x<x'\le b$ and let $P$: $a=x_0<\dots<x_n=x$ be any partition of $[a,x]$. Appending $x'$ gives a partition of $[a,x']$ with variation sum $S(P)+|f(x')-f(x)|$, so $$S(P)+|f(x')-f(x)|\le V(x').$$

The last term doesn't depend on the partition; take the sup over all partitions of $[a,x]$.

Taking the supremum over all partitions $P$ of $[a,x]$ gives (1): $$V(x)+|f(x')-f(x)|\le V(x').$$ For $x=a$, (1) follows from the partition $\{a,x'\}$ and $V(a)=0$.

$V$ increases: the added term is $\ge0$.

From (1): $V(x')\ge V(x)$ for $x<x'$, so $V$ is increasing.

$V-f$ increases: $V$ grows by at least $|\Delta f|$, which is at least $\Delta f$.

For $W:=V-f$, $x<x'$ and $\Delta:=f(x')-f(x)$, by (1): $$\begin{aligned}&W(x')-W(x)\\&=V(x')-V(x)-\Delta\\&\ge|\Delta|-\Delta\ge0.\end{aligned}$$ So $W$ is increasing.

Payoff: $f$ is a difference of two increasing functions, both integrable.

Hence $f=V-W$ is the difference of two increasing functions. Monotone functions on $[a,b]$ are Riemann integrable and differences of integrable functions are integrable, so $f$ is Riemann integrable. ∎

Note. The official solution also proves the integrability directly with step functions on an equally spaced partition (the same construction as for monotone functions, applied to $V$ and $W$ at once). Citing “monotone ⇒ integrable” and linearity is the short way and is fully correct.

Trap

Taking the supremum on both sides at once and losing the extra term. Fix the partition of $[a,x]$ first, write the inequality with the extra term $|f(x')-f(x)|$, and only then take the supremum on the left; the right side $V(x')$ does not depend on the partition.
