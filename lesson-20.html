Roads Without End

Improper integrals: integrals over an infinite interval like $[1,\infty)$, or of a function that blows up at an endpoint, defined as limits of ordinary integrals. When do they converge? The $x^{-\alpha}$ test, the comparison test, absolute convergence, and the integral test that ties improper integrals to series. Then the Gamma function, which extends $n!$ to all positive reals. Recap of lesson 19 (5 min)

  • Fundamental theorem: $F(x)=\int_a^xf$ has $F'(x_0)=f(x_0)$ wherever $f$ is continuous; $\int_a^bf=F(b)-F(a)$ for any antiderivative $F$ of a continuous $f$.
  • Tools: by parts $\int uv'=uv-\int u'v$; substitution $\int_a^bg(\varphi)\varphi'=\int_{\varphi(a)}^{\varphi(b)}g$; partial fractions; $x=a\sin\theta$, $x=a\tan\theta$, $u=\tan\frac x2$.

  1. Compute $\int_1^b\frac{dx}{x^2}$ and $\int_1^b\frac{dx}x$ for $b>1$. What happens as $b\to\infty$?

    Answer

    $1-\frac1b\to1$, and $\log b\to\infty$. Today's whole lesson in one example: the area under $\frac1{x^2}$ over $[1,\infty)$ is finite, under $\frac1x$ it isn't.

  2. Which method for $\int\frac{dx}{x(1+x)}$, and what is it?

    Answer

    Partial fractions: $\frac1x-\frac1{1+x}$, so $\log\frac x{1+x}+C$ for $x>0$.

  3. Lesson 14: when does $\sum\frac1{n^p}$ converge?

    Answer

    Exactly for $p>1$ (condensation). Today the integral test gives a second proof, and the same threshold appears for $\int_1^\infty x^{-p}$.

1. The story: roads without end

East of the river town, a road runs into the plains and never stops. The road-wardens sell lamp oil to travellers: on each mile you need a little oil, less and less the further you go, because the road gets straighter and quieter. Can a traveller set out with a finite flask and still light the road forever?

The old wardens say it depends on how fast the need shrinks. If the oil for mile $x$ shrinks like $\frac1x$, every flask runs dry: the needs add up without bound, slowly, like the halves and thirds and quarters of the harmonic road. If it shrinks like $\frac1{x^2}$, one flask is enough for eternity. And the wardens have a second trick: to know whether a road with oil needs $f(1),f(2),f(3),\dots$ at the milestones can be lit, you don't have to add the milestones. You measure the oil along the smooth road instead, and the two answers always agree.

In maths: the oil along the road is an improper integral $\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_1^bf$. It converges for $f=x^{-2}$ and diverges for $f=x^{-1}$. The second trick is the integral test: for decreasing $f\ge0$, $\sum f(n)$ converges exactly when $\int_1^\infty f$ does.

2. Integrals over infinite intervals

Definition. Let $f:[a,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ be integrable on every $[a,b]$, $b>a$. The improper integral is

$$\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_a^bf(x)\,dx,$$

if the limit exists. If it is a real number, the improper integral converges; otherwise it diverges (and if the limit is $\pm\infty$, we write $\int_a^\infty f=\pm\infty$). $\int_{-\infty}^bf$ is defined the same way with $a\to-\infty$.

Both ends infinite: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f=\int_{-\infty}^0f+\int_0^\infty f$, and it exists only if both halves converge, with two independent limits.

Proof A: the $x^{-\alpha}$ test for integrals (Beispiel 9.30, p. 438, and Übung 9.42, p. 442)

Theorem. Let $\alpha\in\mathbb R$.

  1. $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^\alpha}$ converges if and only if $\alpha>1$, and then it equals $\frac1{\alpha-1}$. For $\alpha\le1$ it is $+\infty$.
  2. $\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^\alpha}$ converges if and only if $\alpha<1$, and then it equals $\frac1{1-\alpha}$. For $\alpha\ge1$ it is $+\infty$.

The idea. Compute $\int_1^b$ (or $\int_a^1$) with the antiderivative, then let $b\to\infty$ (or $a\searrow0$). Everything depends on whether the exponent $1-\alpha$ of the antiderivative $\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}$ is negative or positive, and on the special case $\alpha=1$ with $\log$. The two parts are mirror images: at $\infty$ you need fast decay (large $\alpha$), at $0$ you need a mild blow-up (small $\alpha$).

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Start: “For $b>1$, $\int_1^bx^{-\alpha}\,dx=\dots$” (or for $0<a<1$, $\int_a^1$).
  • End: the limit as $b\to\infty$ (or $a\searrow0$) in each case.
  • The gap: three cases: $\alpha>1$, $\alpha<1$, $\alpha=1$.
The storyWhat you write

Measure the oil up to milestone $b$.

1. For $b>1$ and $\alpha\neq1$: $\int_1^bx^{-\alpha}dx=\left[\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\right]_1^b=\frac{b^{1-\alpha}-1}{1-\alpha}$. For $\alpha=1$: $\int_1^b\frac{dx}x=\log b$.

Fast-shrinking need: the measurements settle down.

If $\alpha>1$, then $1-\alpha<0$ and $b^{1-\alpha}\to0$, so the integrals tend to $\frac{-1}{1-\alpha}=\frac1{\alpha-1}$.

Slow-shrinking need: the measurements grow without bound.

If $\alpha<1$, then $b^{1-\alpha}\to\infty$ and the integrals tend to $+\infty$. If $\alpha=1$, $\log b\to\infty$.

Near the start of a road that begins with a cliff, the roles swap.

2. For $0<a<1$ and $\alpha\neq1$: $\int_a^1x^{-\alpha}dx=\frac{1-a^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}$, and $\int_a^1\frac{dx}x=-\log a$. As $a\searrow0$: $a^{1-\alpha}\to0$ if $\alpha<1$ (limit $\frac1{1-\alpha}$), $a^{1-\alpha}\to\infty$ if $\alpha>1$, and $-\log a\to\infty$. ∎

Moves used: split into cases

The proof in one piece

For $b>1$: $\int_1^bx^{-\alpha}dx=\frac{b^{1-\alpha}-1}{1-\alpha}$ if $\alpha\neq1$, and $\log b$ if $\alpha=1$. As $b\to\infty$, $b^{1-\alpha}\to0$ for $\alpha>1$ and $\to\infty$ for $\alpha<1$, and $\log b\to\infty$. So the integral converges exactly for $\alpha>1$, with value $\frac1{\alpha-1}$. For $0<a<1$: $\int_a^1x^{-\alpha}dx=\frac{1-a^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}$ if $\alpha\neq1$, and $-\log a$ if $\alpha=1$; as $a\searrow0$ this converges exactly for $\alpha<1$, to $\frac1{1-\alpha}$. ∎

    Answer

    For none: it splits into $\int_0^1+\int_1^\infty$, which needs $\alpha<1$ and $\alpha>1$ at the same time.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Compute $\int_0^\infty e^{-x}dx$ and $\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}$.

    Answer

    $$\lim_b\bigl[-e^{-x}\bigr]_0^b=\lim_b(1-e^{-b})=1.$$$\lim_b\arctan b=\frac\pi2$.

Worked example 1: two problem points, and a trap

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

  1. $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}$.

    Answer

    Both halves converge: $\int_0^\infty=\frac\pi2$ and, by symmetry, $\int_{-\infty}^0=\frac\pi2$. So the integral is $\pi$.

  2. Does $\int_{-\infty}^\infty x\,dx$ exist? What is $\lim_{c\to\infty}\int_{-c}^cx\,dx$?

    Answer

    It doesn't exist: already $\int_0^\infty x\,dx=\lim\frac{b^2}2=\infty$. But $\int_{-c}^cx\,dx=0$ for every $c$, so the symmetric limit is $0$. That's why the definition uses two independent limits: with $\int_{-c}^{c+1}x\,dx=c+\frac12\to\infty$, a different way of going to infinity would give a different “value”.

  3. True or false: if $\int_1^\infty f$ converges, then $f(x)\to0$.

    Answer

    False! Unlike series. Take $f$ with a thin spike of height $1$ and width $\frac1{n^2}$ at each integer $n$, and $0$ elsewhere: the integral is at most $\sum\frac1{n^2}<\infty$, but $f(n)=1$. Exercise 8 has a continuous example where the integrand is even unbounded.

3. Comparison and absolute convergence

Most exam questions only ask whether an improper integral converges, and most integrands have no nice antiderivative. As with series (lesson 14), non-negative functions are the easy case.

Proof B: non-negative integrands and the comparison test (Lemma 9.33, p. 439)

Theorem. Let $f,g:[a,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ be integrable on every $[a,b]$.

  1. If $f\ge0$, then $\int_a^\infty f$ either converges or is $+\infty$, and $\int_a^\infty f=\sup_{b>a}\int_a^bf$. It converges if and only if the integrals $\int_a^bf$ are bounded.
  2. Comparison test. If $0\le f\le g$ and $\int_a^\infty g$ converges, then $\int_a^\infty f$ converges (and is $\le\int_a^\infty g$). If $\int_a^\infty f=\infty$, then $\int_a^\infty g=\infty$.

The same holds for integrals over $(A,b]$ with a problem point at $A$.

The idea. For $f\ge0$, $b\mapsto\int_a^bf$ is increasing (more road, more oil). An increasing function converges as $b\to\infty$ exactly when it is bounded, and the limit is its supremum, exactly as for monotone sequences. The comparison test follows: the integrals of $f$ are bounded by those of $g$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Goal of 1: with $I(b)=\int_a^bf$ and $S=\sup_bI(b)$: “for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $B$ with $|I(b)-S|\le\varepsilon$ for $b\ge B$” if $S<\infty$; “$I(b)\to\infty$” if $S=\infty$.
  • Goal of 2: “$\int_a^bf$ is bounded”, then use 1.
The storyWhat you write

The oil used so far only ever grows.

1. Let $I(b)=\int_a^bf$. For $b<b'$, $I(b')-I(b)=\int_b^{b'}f\ge0$, so $I$ is increasing. Let $S=\sup_{b>a}I(b)\in(-\infty,\infty]$.

If some flask is enough for all distances, the usage creeps up to the flask's smallest sufficient size.

If $S<\infty$: given $\varepsilon>0$, there is $B$ with $I(B)>S-\varepsilon$ ($S$ is the least upper bound). For $b\ge B$, $S-\varepsilon<I(B)\le I(b)\le S$. So $I(b)\to S$.

If no flask is enough, the usage passes every size.

If $S=\infty$: for every $M$ there is $B$ with $I(B)>M$, and then $I(b)>M$ for all $b\ge B$. So $I(b)\to\infty$.

A traveller who needs less than a lucky one is lucky too.

2. If $0\le f\le g$, monotonicity of the integral gives $\int_a^bf\le\int_a^bg\le\int_a^\infty g$ (by 1 for $g$). So the integrals of $f$ are bounded and, by 1, $\int_a^\infty f$ converges, with $\int_a^\infty f\le\int_a^\infty g$. The second statement is the contrapositive. ∎

Moves used: approach a supremum monotone and bounded find a majorant (or minorant)

The proof in one piece

$I(b)=\int_a^bf$ is increasing since $f\ge0$. Let $S=\sup I$. If $S<\infty$, for $\varepsilon>0$ choose $B$ with $I(B)>S-\varepsilon$; then $S-\varepsilon<I(b)\le S$ for $b\ge B$, so $I(b)\to S$. If $S=\infty$, for each $M$ some $I(B)>M$, hence $I(b)>M$ for $b\ge B$. For 2: $\int_a^bf\le\int_a^bg\le\int_a^\infty g$, so $\int_a^bf$ is bounded and 1 applies. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Show that $\int_1^\infty e^{-x^2}dx$ converges.

    Answer

    For $x\ge1$, $x^2\ge x$, so $0\le e^{-x^2}\le e^{-x}$, and $\int_1^\infty e^{-x}=\frac1e$ converges. By comparison, $\int_1^\infty e^{-x^2}$ converges. With symmetry and the bounded piece $\int_{-1}^1$, so does the whole bell curve $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2}dx$ (its value $\sqrt\pi$ comes in the second semester).

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Does $\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{\log x}$ converge?

    Answer

    No. $\log x\le x$, so $\frac1{\log x}\ge\frac1x\ge0$, and $\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}x=\infty$. By comparison (second statement), $\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{\log x}=\infty$.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

How to compare: the limit form

Often you only know how $f$ behaves near the problem point. If $f,g>0$ and $\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\to L$ with $0<L<\infty$ at the problem point, then $\frac L2g\le f\le2Lg$ close to it, so $\int f$ and $\int g$ converge or diverge together. The comparison functions are almost always $x^{-\alpha}$ (Proof A) or $e^{-x}$.

  • $\frac{x+3}{x^3+1}$ at $\infty$: behaves like $\frac1{x^2}$, converges.
  • $\frac1{\sqrt{x+x^3}}$ at $0$: like $\frac1{\sqrt x}$, converges; at $\infty$: like $x^{-3/2}$, converges.
  • $\frac{\sin x}{x^2}$ at $0$: like $\frac1x$, diverges.

Bounded pieces on compact intervals never cause trouble: only the problem points matter, and each one must be checked separately.

Proof C: absolute convergence implies convergence (Übung 9.43, p. 443)

Theorem. Let $f:[a,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ be integrable on every $[a,b]$. If $\int_a^\infty|f|$ converges (“$\int f$ converges absolutely”), then $\int_a^\infty f$ converges.

The idea. Signs can only help: cancellations make the integral smaller, not larger. Formally, shift $f$ up by $|f|$: the function $f+|f|$ is non-negative and at most $2|f|$, so its integral converges by comparison. Then $f=(f+|f|)-|f|$ is a difference of two convergent integrals.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Goal: “$\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_a^bf$ exists in $\mathbb R$.”
  • Known: only Proof B, which needs $f\ge0$. The gap: write $f$ with non-negative functions.
The storyWhat you write

Lift the road so it never dips below ground.

$|f|$ is integrable on each $[a,b]$ (lesson 9), and $0\le f+|f|\le2|f|$.

The lifted road needs at most twice the honest oil.

$\int_a^\infty2|f|$ converges, so by comparison (Proof B) $\int_a^\infty(f+|f|)$ converges.

Take the lift back off.

For every $b$: $\int_a^bf=\int_a^b(f+|f|)-\int_a^b|f|$. Both terms converge as $b\to\infty$, so $\int_a^\infty f$ converges, and $\left|\int_a^\infty f\right|\le\int_a^\infty|f|$. ∎

Moves used: find a majorant (or minorant) add and subtract two versions

The proof in one piece

$|f|$ is integrable on each $[a,b]$ and $0\le f+|f|\le2|f|$. As $\int_a^\infty2|f|$ converges, $\int_a^\infty(f+|f|)$ converges by comparison. Hence $\int_a^bf=\int_a^b(f+|f|)-\int_a^b|f|$ converges as $b\to\infty$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Show that $\int_1^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x^2}dx$ converges.

    Answer

    $\left|\frac{\sin x}{x^2}\right|\le\frac1{x^2}$, and $\int_1^\infty x^{-2}$ converges (Proof A). So it converges absolutely, hence converges by Proof C.

  2. Rung 2 (3 min). Show that $\int_1^\infty\frac{\sin x}x dx$ converges. (Hint: integrate by parts first.)

    Answer

    $$\begin{aligned}\int_1^b\frac{\sin x}xdx&=\left[-\frac{\cos x}x\right]_1^b-\int_1^b\frac{\cos x}{x^2}dx\end{aligned}.$$The bracket is $\cos1-\frac{\cos b}b\to\cos1$. The last integral converges absolutely, as in Rung 1. So the limit exists. (It does not converge absolutely, so the converse of Proof C fails, just as for series.)

  3. Rung 3 (2 min). Write the proof from the frame alone.

4. The integral test for series

Proof D: the integral test (Satz 9.35, p. 440–441)

Theorem. Let $f:[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ be decreasing. Then

$$\sum_{n=2}^\infty f(n)\le\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx\le\sum_{n=1}^\infty f(n).$$

In particular, $\sum_{n=1}^\infty f(n)$ converges if and only if $\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx$ converges.

You'll need these (lessons 9, 11, 14)
  • Monotone functions are integrable on every $[1,b]$ (lesson 9).
  • Monotone and bounded sequences converge, and a series of non-negative terms converges if and only if its partial sums are bounded.

The idea. The picture: on each $[n,n+1]$, the area under $f$ lies between the short bar $f(n+1)$ and the tall bar $f(n)$. Add up over $n=1,\dots,N$: the integral $\int_1^{N+1}f$ is squeezed between two partial sums. If one side is bounded, so is the other.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Two directions: “$\int$ converges ⇒ partial sums bounded” and “series converges ⇒ $\int_1^bf$ bounded”. Each is the bounded-criterion for non-negative terms (series) or integrands (Proof B).
  • The gap: one inequality per interval $[n,n+1]$, then sum.
The storyWhat you write

On each mile, the oil needed lies between the needs at its two milestones.

For $n\in\mathbb N$ and $x\in[n,n+1]$, $f$ decreasing gives $f(n+1)\le f(x)\le f(n)$. By monotonicity of the integral, $f(n+1)\le\int_n^{n+1}f\le f(n)$.

Add the miles up.

Summing over $k=1,\dots,N$ and using interval additivity:

$$\sum_{n=2}^{N+1}f(n)\le\int_1^{N+1}f\le\sum_{n=1}^Nf(n).\qquad(*)$$

If the smooth road can be lit, so can the milestones.

If $\int_1^\infty f$ converges: by $(*)$ and $f\ge0$, $\sum_{n=2}^{N+1}f(n)\le\int_1^\infty f$ for all $N$. The partial sums are bounded, so the series converges, and $\sum_{n\ge2}f(n)\le\int_1^\infty f$.

And if the milestones can be lit, so can the road.

If $\sum f(n)$ converges: for $b>1$ let $N=\lfloor b\rfloor$. Then $\int_1^bf\le\int_1^{N+1}f\le\sum_{n=1}^Nf(n)\le\sum_{n=1}^\infty f(n)$. By Proof B, $\int_1^\infty f$ converges and is $\le\sum_{n\ge1}f(n)$. ∎

Moves used: sandwich go to partial sums monotone and bounded

The proof in one piece

Since $f$ is decreasing, $f(n+1)\le\int_n^{n+1}f\le f(n)$ for each $n$. Summing from $1$ to $N$: $\sum_{n=2}^{N+1}f(n)\le\int_1^{N+1}f\le\sum_{n=1}^Nf(n)$. If $\int_1^\infty f$ converges, the partial sums of $\sum_{n\ge2}f(n)$ are bounded by it (as $f\ge0$), so the series converges. If $\sum f(n)$ converges, then $\int_1^bf\le\int_1^{\lfloor b\rfloor+1}f\le\sum_{n\ge1}f(n)$ for every $b$, so $\int_1^\infty f$ converges by Proof B. The inequalities pass to the limits. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. One interval: $f(n+1)\le\int_n^{n+1}f\le f(n)$ (decreasing).
  2. Sum: $\sum_{2}^{N+1}f(n)\le\int_1^{N+1}f\le\sum_1^Nf(n)$.
  3. Both directions: bounded on one side ⇒ bounded on the other; non-negative ⇒ converges.

In short: “short bars, curve, tall bars.” For the 2018 winter exam's estimate (Exercise 7), you only need $(*)$ with the exact integral.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why is $f$ integrable on $[1,b]$, and where is “decreasing” used besides that?

    Answer

    Monotone functions on compact intervals are integrable (lesson 9). And decreasing gives $f(n+1)\le f(x)\le f(n)$ on $[n,n+1]$, the heart of the proof.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Use the integral test to show again that $\sum\frac1{n^p}$ converges exactly for $p>1$ ($p>0$).

    Answer

    $f(x)=x^{-p}$ is positive and decreasing for $p>0$, so $\sum n^{-p}$ converges iff $\int_1^\infty x^{-p}$ does, i.e. iff $p>1$ (Proof A). (For $p\le0$ the terms don't tend to $0$.)

  3. Rung 3 (5 min). Write the whole proof from the exam box alone.

Worked example 2: series via integrals

  1. Does $\sum_{n\ge2}\frac1{n\log n}$ converge? And $\sum_{n\ge2}\frac1{n(\log n)^2}$?

    Answer

    Both $f$ are positive and decreasing on $[2,\infty)$. Substituting $u=\log x$, $du=\frac{dx}x$: $$\begin{aligned}\int_2^b\frac{dx}{x\log x}&=\log\log b-\log\log2\to\infty\end{aligned},$$so the first diverges. $\int_2^b\frac{dx}{x(\log x)^2}=\frac1{\log2}-\frac1{\log b}\to\frac1{\log2}$, so the second converges. (Lesson 14 got these by condensation.)

  2. Show $\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1n=\log N+O(1)$: more precisely, $\log(N+1)\le\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1n\le1+\log N$.

    Answer

    Use $(*)$ with $f=\frac1x$. The right inequality of $(*)$ with $N$: $$\log(N+1)=\int_1^{N+1}\frac{dx}x\le\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1n.$$The left inequality with $N-1$: $\sum_{n=2}^N\frac1n\le\int_1^N\frac{dx}x=\log N$, so $\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1n\le1+\log N$. The harmonic sum grows like $\log N$: to reach $100$ you'd need about $e^{100}\approx10^{43}$ terms.

5. Integrals of unbounded functions

Definition. Let $f:[a,B)\to\mathbb R$ be integrable on every $[a,b]$ with $b<B$ (typically $f$ is unbounded near $B$). Then

$$\int_a^Bf(x)\,dx=\lim_{b\nearrow B}\int_a^bf(x)\,dx,$$

if the limit exists; similarly $\int_A^bf=\lim_{a\searrow A}\int_a^bf$ for a problem point at the left end. With several problem points (an infinite end, a blow-up, a point inside), split the integral so that each piece has exactly one problem point, and require every piece to converge.

If $f$ is bounded and the problem was only that $f$ isn't defined at $B$, nothing new happens: the limit exists and equals the ordinary Riemann integral. Proof B (comparison) and Proof C (absolute convergence) work the same way for these integrals.

Worked example 3: integrands unbounded at an endpoint

  1. $\int_0^1\log x\,dx$.

    Answer

    $\log x\to-\infty$ as $x\searrow0$. With the antiderivative $x\log x-x$ (lesson 19): $$\int_a^1\log x\,dx=-1-a\log a+a\to-1,$$using $a\log a\to0$. So the integral is $-1$.

  2. $\int_{-1}^1\frac{dx}x$. (Careful!)

    Answer

    The problem point $0$ is inside, so split: $\int_{-1}^0\frac{dx}x+\int_0^1\frac{dx}x$. Already $\int_0^1\frac{dx}x=\lim_{a\searrow0}(-\log a)=+\infty$, and $\int_{-1}^0=-\infty$. The integral does not exist, and we don't call it $0$, even though $\frac1x$ is odd. (Like lesson 19's warning about $\int_{-1}^1\frac1{x^2}$.)

  3. The length of a circle: the upper half of the unit circle is the graph of $f(x)=\sqrt{1-x^2}$, and its length is $\int_{-1}^1\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}\,dx$. Compute it.

    Answer

    $f'(x)=\frac{-x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$, so $1+f'^2=\frac1{1-x^2}$, which blows up at $\pm1$. Split at $0$: $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}=\lim_{b\nearrow1}\arcsin b=\frac\pi2$, and the same on $[-1,0]$. So the half circle has length $\pi$, the full circle $2\pi$.

  4. $\int_0^{\pi/2}\tan x\,dx$.

    Answer

    $\int_0^b\tan x\,dx=-\log\cos b\to+\infty$ as $b\nearrow\frac\pi2$. Diverges.

6. The Gamma function

One improper integral deserves its own name: it turns the factorial $n!$ into a smooth function of a real variable.

Proof E: the Gamma function extends the factorial (Section 9.3.3, p. 443–444)

Theorem. For $s>0$ the improper integral

$$\Gamma(s)=\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-x}\,dx$$

converges. It satisfies $\Gamma(s+1)=s\,\Gamma(s)$ and $\Gamma(1)=1$, so $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$ for all $n\in\mathbb N_0$.

The idea. Two problem points, so two comparisons: near $0$ the integrand is at most $x^{s-1}$, a mild blow-up since $s-1>-1$ (Proof A). Near $\infty$ the exponential beats every power: $x^{s-1}e^{-x}\le Ce^{-x/2}$. The functional equation is one integration by parts, and the factorials follow by induction.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Convergence: split $\int_0^\infty=\int_0^1+\int_1^\infty$ and show both converge.
  • Functional equation: by parts on $[a,b]$, then $a\searrow0$, $b\to\infty$.
  • Factorials: $\Gamma(1)$ by hand, then induction.
The storyWhat you write

At the start of the road, the cliff is gentle enough.

Near $0$. For $0<x\le1$: $0\le x^{s-1}e^{-x}\le x^{s-1}=x^{-(1-s)}$ with $1-s<1$. By Proof A and comparison, $\int_0^1x^{s-1}e^{-x}dx$ converges.

Far out, the oil need dies faster than any power can grow.

Near $\infty$. $x^{s-1}e^{-x/2}\to0$ as $x\to\infty$ (exp beats powers, lesson 16), so it is bounded on $[1,\infty)$ by some $C$. Then $0\le x^{s-1}e^{-x}\le Ce^{-x/2}$, and $\int_1^\infty Ce^{-x/2}dx=2Ce^{-1/2}$ converges. By comparison, $\int_1^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-x}dx$ converges.

One step further along costs exactly $s$ times the previous trip.

Functional equation. By parts with $u=x^s$, $v'=e^{-x}$, for $0<a<b$:

$$\begin{aligned}&\int_a^bx^se^{-x}dx\\&\quad=\bigl[-x^se^{-x}\bigr]_a^b+s\int_a^bx^{s-1}e^{-x}dx.\end{aligned}$$

As $a\searrow0$ and $b\to\infty$, $a^se^{-a}\to0$ and $b^se^{-b}\to0$, so $\Gamma(s+1)=s\,\Gamma(s)$.

The first trip costs one flask, and every later one multiplies.

$\Gamma(1)=\int_0^\infty e^{-x}dx=1$. Induction: $\Gamma(1)=1=0!$, and if $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$, then $\Gamma(n+2)=(n+1)\Gamma(n+1)=(n+1)!$. ∎

Moves used: find a majorant (or minorant) induction with labelled parts

The proof in one piece

On $(0,1]$, $0\le x^{s-1}e^{-x}\le x^{s-1}$, and $\int_0^1x^{s-1}dx$ converges as $s-1>-1$. On $[1,\infty)$, $x^{s-1}e^{-x/2}$ is continuous with limit $0$, hence bounded by some $C$, so $0\le x^{s-1}e^{-x}\le Ce^{-x/2}$, which has a convergent integral. By comparison $\Gamma(s)$ converges. Integration by parts gives $\int_a^bx^se^{-x}=[-x^se^{-x}]_a^b+s\int_a^bx^{s-1}e^{-x}$; letting $a\searrow0$, $b\to\infty$ gives $\Gamma(s+1)=s\Gamma(s)$. With $\Gamma(1)=1$, induction gives $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Compute $\int_0^\infty x^3e^{-x}dx$ without integrating.

    Answer

    $\Gamma(4)=3!=6$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Why does $\int_0^1x^{s-1}e^{-x}dx$ diverge for $s=0$?

    Answer

    For $0<x\le1$, $e^{-x}\ge e^{-1}$, so $x^{-1}e^{-x}\ge\frac1{ex}\ge0$, and $\int_0^1\frac{dx}x=\infty$. By comparison (second statement) it diverges. So $s>0$ is really needed.

  3. Rung 3 (4 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Why it's cool. $n!$ only makes sense for whole numbers: what would $\left(\frac12\right)!$ be? The Gamma function answers it: $\left(\frac12\right)!=\Gamma\left(\frac32\right)=\frac12\Gamma\left(\frac12\right)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2$, because $\Gamma\left(\frac12\right)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-u^2}du=\sqrt\pi$ (after substituting $x=u^2$). A factorial of one half involves $\pi$, the bell curve and a circle. The same Gamma function appears in the volume of a ball in any dimension, in probability, and in the Riemann zeta function.

7. Worked examples: converges or not?

A mixed set to revise the whole lesson before the exercises. In the exam, the work is a quick, reliable decision for each problem point.

The decision list for “does $\int f$ converge?”

  1. Find all problem points: $\pm\infty$, and every point where $f$ is unbounded (zeros of a denominator, $\log$ at $0$). Split so that each piece has one.
  2. At each problem point, find the dominant behaviour: like $x^{-\alpha}$ (or $|x-c|^{-\alpha}$), like $e^{-x}$, or with oscillation.
  3. Compare (Proof B or its limit form) with Proof A: at $\infty$ need $\alpha>1$; at a finite point need $\alpha<1$.
  4. Oscillating sign ($\sin x$, $\cos x$): try absolute convergence first (Proof C); if that fails, integrate by parts (Proof C, Rung 2).
  5. One divergent piece is enough for the whole integral to diverge.

Worked example 4: which converge?

  1. $\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{\sqrt x\,(1+x)}$.

    Answer

    Two problem points. At $0$: like $x^{-1/2}$, $\alpha=\frac12<1$, converges. At $\infty$: like $x^{-3/2}$, $\alpha>1$, converges. So it converges. (Its value: $u=\sqrt x$ gives $\int_0^\infty\frac{2\,du}{1+u^2}=\pi$.)

  2. $\int_0^\infty\frac{x}{1+x^2}dx$.

    Answer

    At $\infty$ like $\frac1x$: diverges. Indeed $\int_0^b=\frac12\log(1+b^2)\to\infty$.

  3. $\int_1^\infty\frac{\cos x}{\sqrt x}\,dx$.

    Answer

    Not absolutely convergent by the simple bound ($|\cos x|/\sqrt x\le x^{-1/2}$ gives nothing). By parts: $\left[\frac{\sin x}{\sqrt x}\right]_1^b+\frac12\int_1^b\frac{\sin x}{x^{3/2}}dx$; the bracket converges, and the integral converges absolutely ($\alpha=\frac32$). So it converges.

  4. $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^2+\sqrt x}$.

    Answer

    Problem point $0$, where $\sqrt x$ dominates: $\frac1{x^2+\sqrt x}\le\frac1{\sqrt x}$. Converges by comparison.

  5. $\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\log x\,dx$.

    Answer

    At $0$: $|e^{-x}\log x|\le|\log x|$, and $\int_0^1|\log x|=1$ converges (Worked example 3). At $\infty$: $|e^{-x}\log x|\le e^{-x}x\le Ce^{-x/2}$, converges. So it converges absolutely. (Its value is $-\gamma\approx-0.577$, Euler's constant from the Bonus.)

  6. $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{dx}{|x|+1}$.

    Answer

    At $\infty$ like $\frac1x$: $\int_0^b\frac{dx}{x+1}=\log(b+1)\to\infty$. Diverges (one divergent piece is enough).

Exercise 1 (Felder HS22, 3.MC3, 2 points)

Open the original: HS22 exam

Which of these improper integrals converges? (A) $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x}}$ (B) $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{dx}{|x|+1}$ (C) $\int_{-1}^1\frac x{x^2-1}dx$ (D) $\int_0^\infty\sin x\,dx$

Solution

(A). $$\begin{aligned}\int_0^b(1-x)^{-1/2}dx&=\bigl[-2\sqrt{1-x}\bigr]_0^b\\&=2-2\sqrt{1-b}\to2\end{aligned}$$as $b\nearrow1$. (It's $|x-1|^{-1/2}$ near the problem point $1$, and $\frac12<1$.)

(B) diverges (Worked example 4, item 6). (C) has problem points $\pm1$; near $1$, $\frac x{x^2-1}=\frac x{(x-1)(x+1)}$ behaves like $\frac1{2(x-1)}$, so $\int\frac{dx}{x-1}$ diverges ($\alpha=1$). (D) $\int_0^b\sin x\,dx=1-\cos b$ has no limit.

Exercise 2 (PVK mock HS25, MC9, 2 points)

Open the original: PVK mock exam · solutions

Which improper integral converges? (A) $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}x$ (B) $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^{3/2}}$ (C) $\int_0^1\frac{dx}x$ (D) $\int_0^\infty e^x\,dx$

Solution

(B): $\alpha=\frac32>1$ at $\infty$, value $\frac1{\alpha-1}=2$ (Proof A). (A) and (C) are the borderline $\alpha=1$, both $+\infty$. (D) $e^b-1\to\infty$.

Exercise 3 (Figalli FS24, MC10, 2 points)

Open the original: FS24 exam with solutions

Which converges? (A) $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x^2}dx$ (B) $\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2}$ (C) $\int_0^\infty\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2}dx$ (D) $\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x}$

Solution

(C). Two problem points. At $0$: $\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2}\to\frac12$ (lesson 18 or the series $1-\cos x=\frac{x^2}2-\dots$), so the integrand is bounded near $0$: no real problem. At $\infty$: $0\le\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2}\le\frac2{x^2}$, converges by comparison.

(A) At $0$, $\frac{\sin x}{x^2}\ge\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac1x$ for $0<x\le\frac\pi2$ (since $\sin x\ge\frac2\pi x$ there, concavity), so it diverges at $0$, even though it's fine at $\infty$. (B) diverges at $0$ ($\alpha=2\ge1$). (D) diverges at $\infty$ (like $\frac1x$).

Exercise 4 (Figalli FS24, MC5, 2 points)

Open the original: FS24 exam with solutions

Compute $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}$. (A) $2\pi$ (B) $\pi$ (C) $2$ (D) $1$

Solution

(B). Problem points $0$ and $1$, each like $|x-c|^{-1/2}$, so it converges. Value: $x=u^2$, $dx=2u\,du$, limits $0$ and $1$:

$$\begin{aligned}\int_0^1\frac{2u\,du}{u\sqrt{1-u^2}}&=2\int_0^1\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\\&=2\cdot\frac\pi2\\&=\pi.\end{aligned}$$

(The last integral is improper at $1$; $\lim_{b\nearrow1}2\arcsin b=\pi$.)

Exercise 5 (Figalli FS26, MC9, 2 points)

Open the original: FS26 exam

Compute $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x(2-x)}}$. (A) $\pi$ (B) $\frac\pi2$ (C) $2$ (D) $1$

Solution

(B). Complete the square: $x(2-x)=1-(x-1)^2$. Substitute $u=x-1$, limits $-1$ and $0$:

$$\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^0\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}&=\arcsin0-\lim_{a\searrow-1}\arcsin a\\&=0-\left(-\frac\pi2\right)\\&=\frac\pi2.\end{aligned}$$

Exercise 6 (PVK mock HS25, question A1, 9 points)

Open the original: PVK mock exam · solutions

Let $f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ be continuously differentiable with $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=0$ and $\int_0^\infty|f'(x)|\,dx<\infty$. (A) Prove that $f$ is bounded. (B) Show that $|f(x)|\le\int_x^\infty|f'(s)|\,ds$ for all $x\ge0$. (C) Deduce: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $R>0$ with $|f(x)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x\ge R$.

Solution

Frame: the fundamental theorem (lesson 19, Proof B, part 3) between $x$ and a far point $y$, then $y\to\infty$. Moves: insert a middle point find a majorant (or minorant).

Let $M=\int_0^\infty|f'|$. For $x\ge0$, $\int_x^\infty|f'|=M-\int_0^x|f'|$ converges, it is $\le M$, and it is decreasing in $x$ with limit $0$ as $x\to\infty$ (by the definition of $\int_0^\infty$).

(B) first. Let $0\le x<y$. By the fundamental theorem, $f(x)=f(y)-\int_x^yf'(s)\,ds$, so

$$\begin{aligned}|f(x)|&\le |f(y)|+\int_x^y|f'(s)|\,ds\\&\le |f(y)|+\int_x^\infty|f'(s)|\,ds.\end{aligned}$$

Let $y\to\infty$: $f(y)\to0$, so $|f(x)|\le\int_x^\infty|f'(s)|\,ds$.

(A). By (B), $|f(x)|\le\int_x^\infty|f'|\le M$ for all $x\ge0$.

(C). Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $R$ with $\int_R^\infty|f'|<\varepsilon$ (possible since $\int_x^\infty|f'|\to0$). For $x\ge R$: $|f(x)|\le\int_x^\infty|f'|\le\int_R^\infty|f'|<\varepsilon$.

(The order matters: proving (B) first makes (A) and (C) one line each. In the exam, you may prove the parts in any order, as long as nothing is circular.)

Exercise 7 (2018 winter exam, question 7, 6 points)

Open the original: 2018 winter exam

(a) State the integral test for series. (b) Use the proof of the integral test to show $\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1{\sqrt n}=2\sqrt N+O(1)$ as $N\to\infty$, i.e. $\left|2\sqrt N-\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1{\sqrt n}\right|$ is bounded by a constant independent of $N$.

Solution

Frame: Proof D, the inequality $(*)$. Moves: sandwich.

(a) The statement of Proof D: for $f:[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ decreasing, $\sum_{n\ge2}f(n)\le\int_1^\infty f\le\sum_{n\ge1}f(n)$, and the series converges if and only if the integral does.

(b) $f(x)=x^{-1/2}$ is positive and decreasing, and $\int_1^Nx^{-1/2}dx=2\sqrt N-2$. Let $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1{\sqrt n}$. As in the proof, $f(n+1)\le\int_n^{n+1}f\le f(n)$; summing over $n=1,\dots,N-1$:

$$S_N-1\le2\sqrt N-2\le S_N-\frac1{\sqrt N}.$$

The left inequality gives $S_N\le2\sqrt N-1$, the right one $S_N\ge2\sqrt N-2+\frac1{\sqrt N}\ge2\sqrt N-2$. So $-2\le S_N-2\sqrt N\le-1$, and $\left|2\sqrt N-S_N\right|\le2$ for all $N$.

Exercise 8 (2011 summer exam, question 5b)

Open the original: 2011 summer exam

Does $\int_0^\infty x\sin\left(x^3\right)dx$ converge?

Solution

Frame: oscillation at $\infty$: substitute to see the oscillation, then integrate by parts (Proof C, Rung 2).

On $[0,1]$ the integrand is continuous: no problem. For $b>1$, substitute $u=x^3$, $x=u^{1/3}$, $dx=\frac13u^{-2/3}du$:

$$\int_1^bx\sin(x^3)\,dx=\frac13\int_1^{b^3}u^{-1/3}\sin u\,du.$$

By parts with $v'=\sin u$: $$\begin{aligned}&\int_1^Bu^{-1/3}\sin u\,du\\&\quad=\bigl[-u^{-1/3}\cos u\bigr]_1^B\\&\qquad-\frac13\int_1^Bu^{-4/3}\cos u\,du.\end{aligned}$$The bracket is $\cos1-B^{-1/3}\cos B\to\cos1$. The last integral converges absolutely, since $|u^{-4/3}\cos u|\le u^{-4/3}$ and $\frac43>1$. So the limit exists: yes, it converges.

Remarkable: the integrand $x\sin(x^3)$ doesn't tend to $0$; it swings between $\pm x$, so it's not even bounded. The swings just get so fast that the positive and negative parts cancel.

Exercise 9 (Übung 9.42)

Compute $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt x}$ and decide whether $\int_0^{\pi/2}\tan x\,dx$ converges. Then compute $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x(1+x)}$.

Solution

$\int_a^1x^{-1/2}dx=2-2\sqrt a\to2$. $\int_0^{\pi/2}\tan x$ diverges (Worked example 3, item 4).

Partial fractions: $$\begin{aligned}\int_1^b\left(\frac1x-\frac1{1+x}\right)dx&=\left[\log\frac x{1+x}\right]_1^b\\&=\log\frac b{1+b}-\log\frac12\\&\to0+\log2\\&=\log2.\end{aligned}$$(Each of $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}x$ and $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x}$ diverges on its own: combine before taking the limit.)

Bonus: Euler's constant (Übung 9.36, extended)

Let $a_N=\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1n-\log N$. Show that $(a_N)$ is decreasing and bounded below by $0$, so it converges. (Its limit $\gamma\approx0.5772$ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant.)

Solution

Moves: monotone and bounded sandwich.

Decreasing: $a_{N+1}-a_N=\frac1{N+1}-\int_N^{N+1}\frac{dx}x\le0$, since $\frac1x\ge\frac1{N+1}$ on $[N,N+1]$.

Bounded below: by Worked example 2, item 2, $\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1n\ge\log(N+1)>\log N$, so $a_N>0$.

Monotone and bounded, so $a_N\to\gamma\in[0,1]$. Nobody knows whether $\gamma$ is rational.

9. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

The painter's paradox. Spin the graph of $\frac1x$ for $x\ge1$ around the $x$-axis: you get “Gabriel's horn” (Torricelli, 1643). Its volume is $\pi\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2}=\pi$, finite. Its surface area is at least $2\pi\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}x=\infty$. So you could fill the horn with $\pi$ litres of paint, but you could never paint its inside wall. Torricelli's contemporaries found this deeply unsettling: it was one of the first times infinity behaved so differently from intuition.

Transcendence with Gamma-style integrals. In 1893 Hilbert used improper integrals like $\int_0^\infty x^ne^{-x}dx=n!$ to give short proofs that $e$ and $\pi$ are transcendental: they are not zeros of any polynomial with integer coefficients. The proofs are readable with what you know now (the script recommends them). And yet nobody knows whether $e+\pi$ is transcendental, or even irrational.
