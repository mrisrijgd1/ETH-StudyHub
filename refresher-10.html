1. Warm-up (5 min)

Three quick questions. Answer each in your head, then open it.

  1. Compute $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2}$.

    Answer

    $\int_1^b\frac{dx}{x^2}=1-\frac1b\to1$ as $b\to\infty$. So it converges, with value $1$ ($\alpha=2>1$).

  2. Does $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt x}$ converge?

    Answer

    Yes: at the finite problem point $0$ you need $\alpha<1$, and here $\alpha=\frac12$. Value: $\bigl[2\sqrt x\bigr]_a^1=2-2\sqrt a\to2$.

  3. Write the Taylor polynomial of order 3 of $e^x$ at $0$.

    Answer

    $1+x+\frac{x^2}2+\frac{x^3}6$: every derivative of $e^x$ is $1$ at $0$, so the coefficients are $\frac1{k!}$.

2. Recap card: lesson 20 (10 min)

Read this once, slowly. Everything in bold must come out exactly in the exam.

Lesson 20: improper integrals

  • Definition: $\int_a^\infty f=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_a^bf$, if $f$ is integrable on every $[a,b]$ and the limit exists in $\mathbb R$. Same at a finite problem point: $\int_a^bf=\lim_{c\searrow a}\int_c^bf$. With two problem points, split, and each piece must converge.
  • The $x^{-\alpha}$ test: $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^\alpha}$ converges iff $\alpha>1$ (value $\frac1{\alpha-1}$); $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^\alpha}$ converges iff $\alpha<1$ (value $\frac1{1-\alpha}$). $\alpha=1$ diverges at both ends.
  • Comparison: if $0\le f\le g$ and $\int g$ converges, so does $\int f$; if $\int f=\infty$, then $\int g=\infty$. Limit form: if $f,g>0$ and $\frac fg\to L\in(0,\infty)$, they converge or diverge together.
  • Absolute convergence: $\int|f|$ converges $\Rightarrow\int f$ converges.
  • Integral test: for $f:[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ decreasing, $\sum_{n\ge2}f(n)\le\int_1^\infty f\le\sum_{n\ge1}f(n)$; so the series and the integral converge together.
  • Gamma: $\Gamma(s)=\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-x}dx$ converges for $s>0$, $\Gamma(s+1)=s\Gamma(s)$, $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$.
  • Decision list: find every problem point and split; find the dominant behaviour at each ($x^{-\alpha}$, $e^{-x}$, oscillation); compare with the $x^{-\alpha}$ test; one divergent piece is enough to diverge.

3. Recap card: lesson 21 (10 min)

Lesson 21: Taylor expansions

  • Taylor polynomial: $P_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k$.
  • Integral remainder: for $f$ $(n+1)$ times continuously differentiable on $(a,b)$, $f(x)=P_n(x)+R_n(x)$ with $R_n(x)=\int_{x_0}^xf^{(n+1)}(t)\frac{(x-t)^n}{n!}dt$.
  • Estimate: $|R_n(x)|\le\frac{M_{n+1}|x-x_0|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}$, with $M_{n+1}$ the maximum of $|f^{(n+1)}|$ between $x_0$ and $x$. So $f(x)=P_n(x)+O\bigl((x-x_0)^{n+1}\bigr)$. Lagrange form: $R_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-x_0)^{n+1}$ for some $\xi$ between $x_0$ and $x$.
  • Building blocks at $0$: $e^u$, $\sin u$, $\cos u$, $\log(1+u)=u-\frac{u^2}2+\frac{u^3}3-\dots$, $(1+u)^\alpha=1+\alpha u+\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}2u^2+\dots$, $\frac1{1-u}=1+u+u^2+\dots$
  • Rules for $O$: $x^kO(x^m)=O(x^{k+m})$; $O(x^k)+O(x^m)=O(x^{\min(k,m)})$, so the worst error wins: expand every piece to the same order. Substitute ($u=x^2$, $u=-x$), multiply, compose; drop every power above $n$.
  • Limits by expansion: expand numerator and denominator until the first non-zero term; that term decides.
  • Higher-derivative test: if $f'(x_0)=\dots=f^{(n-1)}(x_0)=0\ne f^{(n)}(x_0)$: $n$ even gives an extremum (minimum if $f^{(n)}(x_0)>0$), $n$ odd gives none.

5. Write it on paper: three exam proofs (30 min)

For each proof: read the claim and the frame, close your eyes for ten seconds, then write the whole proof on paper without looking at the lesson. Only then open the fold and mark yourself with the grader's list. Proofs 1 and 2 each came as a 5–6 point exam question.

Proof 1: the integral test (Satz 9.35, 2018 winter exam)

Claim. Let $f:[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ be decreasing. Then $\sum_{n=2}^\infty f(n)\le\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx\le\sum_{n=1}^\infty f(n)$, and $\sum f(n)$ converges if and only if $\int_1^\infty f$ converges. (Lesson 20, Proof D, script p. 440–441. The 2018 winter exam, question 7, asked for the statement and then used the proof: that is Exercise 7 in section 8.)

What the definitions force

Two directions, and each one is “bounded and non-negative ⇒ converges” (for series: bounded partial sums; for integrals: lesson 20, Proof B). The gap: one inequality per interval $[n,n+1]$, then add them up.

The proof and the grader's list

$f$ is monotone, so it is integrable on every $[1,b]$. Since $f$ is decreasing, $f(n+1)\le f(x)\le f(n)$ for $x\in[n,n+1]$, so $f(n+1)\le\int_n^{n+1}f\le f(n)$. Summing over $n=1,\dots,N$: $$\begin{aligned}\sum_{n=2}^{N+1}f(n)&\le\int_1^{N+1}f\\&\le\sum_{n=1}^Nf(n).\qquad(*)\end{aligned}$$

If $\int_1^\infty f$ converges: as $f\ge0$, $\int_1^{N+1}f\le\int_1^\infty f$, so by the left side of $(*)$ the partial sums of the non-negative series $\sum_{n\ge2}f(n)$ are bounded; hence it converges.

If $\sum f(n)$ converges: for every $b\ge1$, $\int_1^bf\le\int_1^{\lfloor b\rfloor+1}f\le\sum_{n\ge1}f(n)$ by the right side of $(*)$, so the integrals of the non-negative $f$ are bounded and $\int_1^\infty f$ converges (lesson 20, Proof B). Letting $N\to\infty$ in $(*)$ gives the two inequalities. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • the statement with all hypotheses (non-negative, decreasing);
  • integrable because monotone;
  • the one-interval inequality $f(n+1)\le\int_n^{n+1}f\le f(n)$, with “decreasing” as the reason;
  • the summed inequality $(*)$;
  • both directions, each with “non-negative and bounded ⇒ converges”.

In short: “short bars, curve, tall bars”.

Proof 2: Taylor's theorem with integral remainder (Theorem 9.46, 2017 summer exam)

Claim. Let $f:(a,b)\to\mathbb R$ be $(n+1)$ times continuously differentiable and $x_0\in(a,b)$. Then for all $x\in(a,b)$: $$\begin{aligned}f(x)&=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k\\&\quad+\int_{x_0}^xf^{(n+1)}(t)\frac{(x-t)^n}{n!}dt.\end{aligned}$$ (Lesson 21, Proof A, script p. 447–448. The 2017 summer exam, question 12, asked exactly this for 5 points: it is Exercise 6 of the lesson.)

What the definitions force

A statement for every $n$: induction on $n$. Base $n=0$: it is the fundamental theorem. Step: integrate the old remainder by parts. The one thing to remember: $v(t)=-\frac{(x-t)^n}{n!}$, with $x$ fixed.

The proof and the grader's list

Induction on $n\ge0$, for all $(n+1)$ times continuously differentiable $f$ at once.

Base $n=0$: $f$ is continuously differentiable, so by the fundamental theorem $f(x)=f(x_0)+\int_{x_0}^xf'(t)\,dt$, which is the claim.

Step: let $n\ge1$, assume the claim for $n-1$, and let $f$ be $(n+1)$ times continuously differentiable. By the hypothesis, $f(x)=P_{n-1}(x)+\int_{x_0}^xf^{(n)}(t)\frac{(x-t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}dt$. Integrate by parts with $u=f^{(n)}$, $u'=f^{(n+1)}$ (continuous), $v(t)=-\frac{(x-t)^n}{n!}$, $v'(t)=\frac{(x-t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}$: $$\begin{aligned}&\int_{x_0}^xf^{(n)}(t)\frac{(x-t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}dt\\&\quad=\left[-f^{(n)}(t)\frac{(x-t)^n}{n!}\right]_{t=x_0}^{t=x}\\&\qquad+\int_{x_0}^xf^{(n+1)}(t)\frac{(x-t)^n}{n!}dt\\&\quad=\frac{f^{(n)}(x_0)}{n!}(x-x_0)^n+R_n(x).\end{aligned}$$ The bracket is $0$ at $t=x$. Inserting gives $f(x)=P_n(x)+R_n(x)$. ∎

A grader gives the points for (the usual split of the 5 points):

  • the statement, with the remainder written out;
  • the base case, named as the fundamental theorem;
  • the choice of $u$ and $v$, with $v'$ checked (the chain rule's minus cancels the minus in front);
  • the bracket evaluated: $0$ at $t=x$, the new term at $t=x_0$;
  • the conclusion $P_{n-1}+\text{new term}=P_n$.

In short: “fundamental theorem, then parts with $v=-\frac{(x-t)^n}{n!}$”.

Proof 3: the Taylor estimate (Korollar 9.47)

Claim. In the setting of Proof 2, let $M_{n+1}$ be the maximum of $|f^{(n+1)}|$ on the interval between $x_0$ and $x$. Then $|f(x)-P_n(x)|\le\frac{M_{n+1}|x-x_0|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}$. (Lesson 21, Proof B, script p. 448–449. It is what you use in Exercise 5 of the lesson, and in every “with a guaranteed error” question.)

What the definitions force

Start from the remainder of Proof 2. The gap: bound $|f^{(n+1)}(t)|$ by $M_{n+1}$ and integrate the rest exactly. Two cases, $x\ge x_0$ and $x<x_0$.

The proof and the grader's list

$M_{n+1}$ exists: $f^{(n+1)}$ is continuous on a compact interval. By Proof 2, $R_n(x)=\int_{x_0}^xf^{(n+1)}(t)\frac{(x-t)^n}{n!}dt$. For $x\ge x_0$, $(x-t)^n\ge0$ on $[x_0,x]$, so $$\begin{aligned}|R_n(x)|&\le\frac{M_{n+1}}{n!}\int_{x_0}^x(x-t)^ndt\\&=\frac{M_{n+1}(x-x_0)^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.\end{aligned}$$ For $x<x_0$ the same computation on $[x,x_0]$ with $|x-t|^n=(t-x)^n$ gives $\frac{M_{n+1}(x_0-x)^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}$. ∎

A grader gives the points for: why $M_{n+1}$ exists; the remainder from Proof 2; the bound and the exact integral $\int_{x_0}^x(x-t)^ndt=\frac{(x-x_0)^{n+1}}{n+1}$; the other case.

6. Two drills (15 min)

Drill A: does it converge? (and the value, where easy)

For each: name the problem point(s) and the comparison, then decide. Open the fold when you have your answer.

  1. $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2+x}$

    Answer

    Converges: $0\le\frac1{x^2+x}\le\frac1{x^2}$. Value by partial fractions, $\frac1x-\frac1{x+1}$: $\left[\log\frac x{x+1}\right]_1^b\to0-\log\frac12=\log2$.

  2. $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^{2/3}}$

    Answer

    Converges: problem point $0$, $\alpha=\frac23<1$. Value $\frac1{1-2/3}=3$.

  3. $\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{x\log x}$

    Answer

    Diverges, even though it decays faster than $\frac1x$. Substitution $u=\log x$: $\int_2^b\frac{dx}{x\log x}=\log\log b-\log\log2\to\infty$. (So “a bit faster than $\frac1x$” is not enough; you need a power $\alpha>1$.)

  4. $\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x}\,dx$

    Answer

    Converges: it is $\Gamma(3)=2!=2$. (At $\infty$, $x^2e^{-x}\le\frac{C}{x^2}$ because $e^{-x}$ beats every power.)

  5. $\int_0^1\log x\,dx$

    Answer

    Converges, to $-1$: $\bigl[x\log x-x\bigr]_c^1=-1-(c\log c-c)\to-1$, since $c\log c\to0$ as $c\searrow0$.

Drill B: expand it

Use only the building blocks and the $O$ rules: no derivatives.

  1. $e^x\sin x$ at $0$, up to order 3.

    Answer

    $\left(1+x+\frac{x^2}2\right)\left(x-\frac{x^3}6\right)+O(x^4)=x+x^2+\frac{x^3}2-\frac{x^3}6+O(x^4)=x+x^2+\frac{x^3}3+O(x^4)$.

  2. $\sqrt{1+2x}$ at $0$, up to order 2.

    Answer

    $(1+u)^{1/2}=1+\frac u2-\frac{u^2}8+O(u^3)$ with $u=2x$: $1+x-\frac{x^2}2+O(x^3)$.

  3. $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1-\cos x}{x\sin x}$

    Answer

    Numerator $\frac{x^2}2+O(x^4)$, denominator $x\bigl(x+O(x^3)\bigr)=x^2+O(x^4)$. The first terms decide: $\frac12$.

  4. $\frac1{1-x^2}$ at $0$, up to order 4.

    Answer

    The geometric series with $u=x^2$: $1+x^2+x^4+O(x^6)$.

7. Spot the mistake (5 min)

A student computes a limit and hands in this. The answer is wrong. Where exactly does it break?

“Solution.” Since $\sin x=x+O(x^2)$, $$\begin{aligned}\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x-x}{x^3}&=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x-x}{x^3}\\&=0.\end{aligned}$$

The mistake

The error term was thrown away. With $\sin x=x+O(x^2)$ the numerator is only $O(x^2)$, and $\frac{O(x^2)}{x^3}$ can be anything: this expansion says nothing about the limit. The denominator is $x^3$, so the numerator must be expanded to order 3: $\sin x-x=-\frac{x^3}6+O(x^5)$. Then $\frac{\sin x-x}{x^3}=-\frac16+O(x^2)\to-\frac16$.

Exam lesson: never drop the $O$ while computing, and expand until the first term that doesn't cancel. The power in the denominator tells you how far to go.
