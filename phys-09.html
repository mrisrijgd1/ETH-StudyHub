Why it matters for the exam

Celestial mechanics has its own multiple-choice block in almost every exam (HS23, FS25, FS26: eleven questions, all below), and a long problem in most (the satellite in FS24, the asteroid in FS25, the space elevator in FS26). Nearly all of it is three tools: the circular-orbit condition, energy conservation with $U=-\frac{GMm}r$, and Kepler's third law. Everything else is on the formula sheet.

Recap of lesson 8 (3 min)

  1. What is conserved in every collision, and what only in an elastic one?

    Answer

    Total momentum, always. Total kinetic energy only in an elastic collision.

  2. A body hits an equal body at rest, head-on and elastically. What happens?

    Answer

    They exchange velocities: the first stops, the second moves on with $v_1$.

  3. Which law makes a planet move faster near the sun, and which quantity does it conserve?

    Answer

    The area law (Kepler's second law, lesson 7): gravity is a central force, so the angular momentum $\vec L$ about the sun is constant, and $L=mrv$ at the closest and farthest points.

1. The story: the astronomer's tower

In the highest tower of the castle lives the old astronomer. On clear nights she lets the children look at the moon. “Why doesn't it fall down?” asks Tomas. She takes him to the battlements and asks the armourer to fire the little cannon horizontally, first with a small charge, then with more and more. Each ball lands farther away. “Now imagine a cannon so strong,” she says, “that the ball falls exactly as fast as the round earth curves away beneath it. It falls forever and never lands. That is the moon.”

“And with an even bigger charge?” The ball would fly a stretched loop and come back; with bigger still, it would leave and never return. On her desk lie the books of an astronomer who measured the planets for twenty years and found three simple rules in the numbers. She can now explain every one of them with a single law of force.

In physics: Newton's law of gravitation is a central force $\propto\frac1{r^2}$. Being central, it conserves angular momentum (lesson 7); being conservative, it conserves energy (lesson 6). From these two conservation laws alone follow Kepler's laws: orbits are ellipses (or, with enough energy, parabolas and hyperbolas), they sweep equal areas in equal times, and $T^2\propto a^3$. The cannon is Newton's own thought experiment.

2. Newton's law of gravitation

Two point masses at distance $r$ attract each other with (section 7.1, page 163)

$$\vec F(r)=-G\,\frac{m_1m_2}{r^2}\,\hat r,\qquad U(r)=-G\,\frac{m_1m_2}{r},\qquad G=6.67\cdot10^{-11}\,\frac{\mathrm{N\,m^2}}{\mathrm{kg^2}}.$$

The script writes $G_N$; the zero of $U$ is at infinity, so $U$ is negative everywhere (lesson 6). The force is minus the gradient, $\vec F=-\nabla U$.

  • Inverse square: double the distance, a quarter of the force; triple it, a ninth.
  • Near the ground: at the earth's surface $F=mg$ with $g=\frac{GM_E}{R_E^2}\approx9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. This gives the handy shortcut $GM_E=gR_E^2$, and with $R_E\approx6.4\cdot10^6\,\mathrm m$ you get $GM_E\approx4\cdot10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$.
  • Field and specific potential (lesson 6): the force per mass $\vec g(\vec r)=\frac{\vec F}m=-\nabla V$ with $V=-\frac{GM}r$ describes the field of $M$ for every test body at once.

3. The motion of a planet

Setting up (section 7.2, pages 164–166). Sun ($M$) and planet ($m$) form a closed system, so the centre of mass moves uniformly (lesson 7); put it at rest at the origin. Since $M\gg m$, the sun barely moves: treat it as fixed. (Exactly, the relative motion is that of the reduced mass $\mu$ of lesson 7.)

  • The orbit lies in a plane. The force is central, so $\vec L=m\,\vec r\times\vec v$ is constant. $\vec r$ and $\vec v$ always lie in the plane perpendicular to the fixed $\vec L$.
  • Area law (Kepler 2): $\dot A=\frac L{2m}$, and in polar coordinates $L=mr^2\dot\varphi$.
  • Energy (page 166): with $v^2=\dot r^2+r^2\dot\varphi^2$ and $\dot\varphi=\frac L{mr^2}$, $$E=\tfrac12m\dot r^2+\frac{L^2}{2mr^2}-G\frac{mM}r=\text{const}.$$

The effective potential (section 7.3, page 171). The middle term depends only on $r$ (for fixed $L$), so it can be added to the potential:

$$E_p^{\text{eff}}(r)=-G\frac{mM}r+\frac{L^2}{2mr^2},\qquad E=\tfrac12m\dot r^2+E_p^{\text{eff}}(r).$$

Now the radial motion is a one-dimensional problem in the potential $E_p^{\text{eff}}$, read exactly like in lesson 6: the body can only be where $E_p^{\text{eff}}(r)\le E$, and it turns around (in $r$) where they are equal. The term $\frac{L^2}{2mr^2}$ is a “centrifugal barrier”: it keeps a body with $L\neq0$ from falling into the centre.

  • $E=E_0$, the minimum of $E_p^{\text{eff}}$: $r$ stays fixed, a circle with radius $r_0=\frac{L^2}{GMm^2}$.
  • $E_0<E<0$: $r$ swings between two turning points, perihelion and aphelion: an ellipse. The body is bound.
  • $E\ge0$: only one turning point; the body comes in once and leaves for ever (parabola for $E=0$, hyperbola for $E>0$).

Try it: the effective potential

Units with $G=M=m=1$. Choose the angular momentum $L$ and the energy $E$ (the horizontal line). The marked points are the turning points of the radial motion.

Lower the energy until the line touches the bottom of the well: the two turning points merge into one radius, a circular orbit. Raise it above zero: the outer turning point disappears, and the body escapes.

4. Kepler's laws and the orbit shapes

Solving the energy equation (the script does it with a clever substitution on pages 166–168; you never need to reproduce it) gives the orbit in polar coordinates around the sun:

$$r(\varphi)=\frac{p}{1+\varepsilon\cos\varphi},\qquad p=a(1-\varepsilon^2),$$

a conic section with the sun at a focus. Its size and shape follow from the conserved quantities (page 169):

Orbit shapes (formula sheet, page 4, right) $$a=\frac{GmM}{2|E|}\iff E=-\frac{GMm}{2a},\qquad \varepsilon^2=1+\frac{2EL^2}{G^2M^2m^3}.$$
  • $E<0$, $\varepsilon=0$: circle. $E<0$, $0<\varepsilon<1$: ellipse (bound: “gebunden”).
  • $E=0$, $\varepsilon=1$: parabola. $E>0$, $\varepsilon>1$: hyperbola.

Perihelion $r_P=a(1-\varepsilon)$, aphelion $r_A=a(1+\varepsilon)$. The energy depends only on the semi-major axis $a$, not on the eccentricity.

Kepler's three laws (pages 163–169):

  1. The planets move on ellipses with the sun in one focus.
  2. The line from the sun to a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times: $\frac{dA}{dt}=\frac{L}{2m}$ (lesson 7).
  3. The squares of the periods are proportional to the cubes of the semi-major axes: $$\frac{T^2}{a^3}=\frac{4\pi^2}{GM},$$ the same constant for every body orbiting the same $M$. (The script gets it from $T=\frac{\pi ab}{L/2m}$, the ellipse's area divided by the areal velocity.)

The circular orbit, the exam's favourite. Gravity provides the centripetal force (in the rotating frame of lesson 4: gravity and centrifugal force balance):

$$G\frac{Mm}{r^2}=m\frac{v^2}r=m\omega^2r\quad\Rightarrow\quad v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}r},\qquad T^2=\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3.$$

Farther out, slower and with a longer period. On a circle $T=\frac12mv^2=\frac{GMm}{2r}$ and $U=-\frac{GMm}r$, so $E=-\frac{GMm}{2r}=-T$: the special case $a=r$ of the box.

Speed anywhere on an orbit. Energy conservation with $E=-\frac{GMm}{2a}$:

$$\tfrac12mv^2-\frac{GMm}r=-\frac{GMm}{2a}\quad\Rightarrow\quad v^2=GM\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right).$$

Escape velocity. To reach infinity you need $E\ge0$: $\frac12mv^2\ge\frac{GMm}r$, so $v_{\text{esc}}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}r}=\sqrt2\,v_{\text{circ}}$. From the earth's surface, $\sqrt{2gR_E}\approx11.2\,\mathrm{km/s}$. The mass $m$ always cancels: whether an orbit is bound doesn't depend on the body's mass.

Recipe: an orbit problem

  1. Circular orbit? Set gravity equal to the centripetal force, $\frac{GMm}{r^2}=m\omega^2r=m\frac{v^2}r$. Use $\omega=\frac{2\pi}T$.
  2. Two points of one orbit? Energy: $\frac12mv_1^2-\frac{GMm}{r_1}=\frac12mv_2^2-\frac{GMm}{r_2}$. At perihelion and aphelion ($\vec v\perp\vec r$) also angular momentum: $r_Pv_P=r_Av_A$.
  3. Bound or not? Sign of $E=\frac12mv^2-\frac{GMm}r$, or compare $v$ with $\sqrt{2GM/r}$.
  4. Period or size? $E=-\frac{GMm}{2a}$ gives $a$; Kepler 3 gives $T$.
  5. Changing orbits? The energy needed is $\Delta E=E_2-E_1$ with $E=-\frac{GMm}{2a}$ for each orbit.

Worked example 1: the geostationary orbit

A geostationary satellite circles above the equator once per day ($T\approx8.6\cdot10^4\,\mathrm s$, one sidereal day), so it stays above the same point. Find its radius. Use $GM_E\approx4\cdot10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$.

  1. Set up the circular-orbit condition with $\omega=\frac{2\pi}T$.

    Answer

    $\frac{GM_Em}{r^2}=m\omega^2r$, so $r^3=\frac{GM_E}{\omega^2}=\frac{GM_ET^2}{4\pi^2}$.

  2. Put in numbers.

    Answer

    $r^3=\frac{4\cdot10^{14}\cdot7.4\cdot10^9}{40}\,\mathrm{m^3}\approx7.4\cdot10^{22}\,\mathrm{m^3}$, so $r\approx4.2\cdot10^7\,\mathrm m=42\,000\,\mathrm{km}$: about $6.6$ earth radii from the centre, $36\,000\,\mathrm{km}$ above the ground.

  3. How fast does it move?

    Answer

    $v=\omega r=\frac{2\pi\cdot4.2\cdot10^7}{8.6\cdot10^4}\,\mathrm{m/s}\approx3.1\,\mathrm{km/s}$, much slower than the $7.9\,\mathrm{km/s}$ of a satellite just above the ground.

Worked example 2: is the comet bound?

A comet is at distance $r=10^{11}\,\mathrm m$ from the sun and moves with $v=100\,\mathrm{km/s}$. Use $GM_\odot\approx1.4\cdot10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$. What kind of orbit is it on?

  1. Compute the escape velocity at that distance.

    Answer

    $v_{\text{esc}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\cdot1.4\cdot10^{20}}{10^{11}}}\,\mathrm{m/s}$ $=\sqrt{2.8\cdot10^9}\,\mathrm{m/s}$ $\approx53\,\mathrm{km/s}$.

  2. Conclude.

    Answer

    $v>v_{\text{esc}}$, so $E>0$: a hyperbola. The comet passes once and leaves the solar system. Its mass doesn't matter, and neither does the direction of $\vec v$.

Try it: the cannon on the tower

A body starts at distance $r_0$ with a horizontal speed, given as a multiple of the circular speed $v_{\text{circ}}=\sqrt{GM/r_0}$. Press Fire.

Below 1, the start is the aphelion of an ellipse; above 1, it is the perihelion. At $\sqrt2\approx1.41$ the ellipse opens into a parabola: the escape velocity. The period grows like $a^{3/2}$, Kepler's third law.

5. The field of extended bodies

For a body with a mass distribution the potentials of all its pieces add up (section 7.4, formula sheet page 4 left): $U(\vec r)=-Gm\int\frac{\rho(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r-\vec r\,'|}\,d^3r'$. For spheres the integral has a beautiful answer (pages 173–178):

Shell theorem
  • Outside a homogeneous spherical shell (or a sphere made of shells), the field is the same as if all the mass sat at the centre: $g(r)=\frac{GM}{r^2}$.
  • Inside a homogeneous spherical shell, the field is exactly zero: the pulls of the near and far parts cancel.
  • Hence, inside a homogeneous sphere of radius $R$, only the mass $M(r)=M\frac{r^3}{R^3}$ inside radius $r$ pulls: $g(r)=\frac{GM(r)}{r^2}=\frac{GM}{R^3}\,r$, growing linearly from zero at the centre to $\frac{GM}{R^2}$ at the surface.

Try it: the field inside and outside

Compare three bodies of the same mass $M$ and radius $R$. The curve is the size of the gravitational force on a test mass at distance $r$ from the centre.

Outside $R$ the three curves are identical. Inside they differ completely: linear for the full sphere, zero for the shell, ever larger for the point.

Worked example 3: a tunnel through the earth

Imagine a straight tunnel through the centre of a homogeneous earth (radius $R$, surface gravity $g$), without air or friction. A stone is dropped in. Describe its motion.

  1. What force acts on the stone at distance $x$ from the centre (on either side)?

    Answer

    Only the mass inside radius $|x|$ pulls: $F=-\frac{GMm}{R^3}x=-\frac{mg}{R}x$, pointing to the centre. A spring force with $D=\frac{mg}R$.

  2. Write the equation of motion and the period.

    Answer

    $m\ddot x=-\frac{mg}Rx$: a harmonic oscillation (lesson 12) with $\omega=\sqrt{\frac gR}$. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $R=6.4\cdot10^6\,\mathrm m$: $\omega=1.25\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm s^{-1}$ and $T=\frac{2\pi}\omega\approx5000\,\mathrm s\approx84\,\mathrm{min}$. The stone reaches the other side after 42 minutes.

  3. Compare with a satellite skimming the surface.

    Answer

    $GM/R^2=g=\omega^2R$ gives the same $\omega=\sqrt{g/R}$: the same 84 minutes. The tunnel motion is the shadow of that circular orbit on a diameter.

Why it's cool. Stars far out in a galaxy should orbit slower, like the outer planets, since almost all the visible mass sits in the centre: $v=\sqrt{GM/r}$. They don't: the measured rotation curves stay flat far beyond the visible disk (script, page 179). With the shell theorem that means $M(r)$ keeps growing like $r$, where there is no light at all. That missing mass is the “dark matter” that astronomers have been hunting for fifty years; this one formula from your exam is the main evidence for it.

6. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed.

Exercise 1 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.3(a), 1 point, original)

What is true of circular orbits of a moon around a celestial body? (A) The period doesn't depend on the distance to the body. (B) At every moment the centrifugal force equals the gravitational force in magnitude. (C) The angular velocity doesn't depend on the distance to the body. (D) The orbital speed grows with the distance to the body.

Solution

B. In the moon's rotating frame it is at rest, so gravity and centrifugal force balance (the circular-orbit condition). (A), (C): $T^2\propto r^3$, so $T$ and $\omega$ depend on $r$. (D) $v=\sqrt{GM/r}$ decreases with $r$.

Exercise 2 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.3(b), 1 point, original)

What is true of the gravitational fields of extended bodies? (A) Outside a homogeneous sphere, its field can always be described as if the whole mass sat at the centre of mass. (B) The gravitational potential of an extended body is a vector field. (C) The gravitational field inside an extended body is not defined. (D) The gravitational field of an extended body is the same at every point of space.

Solution

A. The shell theorem. (B) The potential is a scalar field; its gradient is the vector field. (C) It is defined (and computed in section 5). (D) It falls off with distance.

Exercise 3 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.3(c), 1 point, original)

Which is not a statement of Kepler's laws? (A) The orbits of celestial bodies are ellipses. (B) The squares of the periods of two different bodies are in the same ratio as the cubes of their semi-major axes. (C) The central body sits in one of the foci. (D) The angular velocity increases with increasing distance.

Solution

D. The opposite holds: by the area law, $\dot\varphi=\frac L{mr^2}$ decreases with distance. (A), (C) are the first law, (B) the third.

Exercise 4 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.3(d), 1 point, original)

The distance between two celestial bodies is tripled. How does the gravitational force between them change? (A) It decreases by a factor of 9. (B) It decreases by a factor of 3. (C) It stays the same. (D) It increases by a factor of 3.

Solution

A. $F\propto\frac1{r^2}$: $\frac1{3^2}=\frac19$.

Exercise 5 (FS23, Konzeptfrage 1.6(a) “Gravitationspotential”, 2 points, original)

Four graphs show a gravitational force as a function of the distance from the centre, with $R$ marked: (i) rising along a curve that gets steeper up to $R$, then falling like $\frac1{r^2}$; (ii) zero up to $R$, then jumping up and falling like $\frac1{r^2}$; (iii) rising in a straight line from zero up to $R$, then falling like $\frac1{r^2}$; (iv) rising along a curve up to $R$, then falling in a straight line to zero. Which one belongs to a spherical body of radius $R$ with constant, homogeneous density $\rho$? (A) i) (B) ii) (C) iii) (D) iv)

Solution

C. Inside, $F=\frac{GMm}{R^3}r$: a straight line through the origin. Outside, $\frac{GMm}{r^2}$, continuous at $R$. (ii) is the thin shell. Check yourself with the “Try it” above.

Exercise 6 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.2(a), 1 point, original)

A planet moves on an ellipse around the sun ($M=2\cdot10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}$). At which point of its orbit is its kinetic energy smallest? (A) At aphelion (farthest from the sun). (B) The kinetic energy is constant. (C) Halfway between perihelion and aphelion. (D) At perihelion (closest to the sun).

Solution

A. $T=E-U=E+\frac{GMm}r$ is smallest where $r$ is largest. Same as the area law: slowest far away.

Exercise 7 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.2(c), 1 point, original)

The semi-major axis of the planet's orbit is $a=10^9\,\mathrm{km}$. What is roughly its period $T$? Use $G\approx7\cdot10^{-11}\,\frac{\mathrm{m^3}}{\mathrm{kg\,s^2}}$. (A) $\frac\pi{14}\cdot10^6\,\mathrm s$ (B) $\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{1.4}}\cdot10^8\,\mathrm s$ (C) $\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{14}}\cdot10^5\,\mathrm s$ (D) $\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{1.4}}\cdot10^{12}\,\mathrm s$

Solution

B. $GM=7\cdot10^{-11}\cdot2\cdot10^{30}$ $=1.4\cdot10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$ and $a=10^{12}\,\mathrm m$. Kepler 3:

$$T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{10^{36}}{1.4\cdot10^{20}}}\,\mathrm s=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{1.4}}\cdot10^8\,\mathrm s.$$

About 17 years: an orbit like Saturn's. Watch the km-to-m conversion.

Exercise 8 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.2(d), 1 point, original)

A comet moves in the sun's field. At a distance $r=100\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{km}$ from the sun its orbital speed is $v=100\,\mathrm{km/s}$. Which is correct? (A) The orbit is a hyperbola, because the sun's attraction is too weak for a closed orbit. (B) The orbit is a parabola, because its eccentricity is $\varepsilon=1$. (C) The orbit is an ellipse, because it is bound to the sun. (D) It can't be decided, because the comet's mass is unknown.

Solution

A. That's Worked example 2: $v_{\text{esc}}\approx53\,\mathrm{km/s}<v$, so $E>0$. (D) The mass cancels in the comparison of $\frac12mv^2$ with $\frac{GMm}r$.

Exercise 9 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.4(a), 1 point, original)

An asteroid is in the field of the sun (mass $M$, radius $R$, $G$ known). Only its present distance $r$ from the sun and the velocity component $v_\perp>0$ perpendicular to the sun–asteroid line are known. Assuming Kepler's laws, which is correct? (A) You don't need $M$ and $R$ to determine the basic orbit shape (ellipse, parabola, hyperbola). (B) The minimum distance of the asteroid from the sun can't be computed from the given information. (C) Since $v_\perp>0$, the asteroid can never collide with the sun. (D) Since $v_\perp>0$, the asteroid can't be at its farthest point right now.

Solution

B. $v_\perp$ gives $L=mrv_\perp$, but the energy needs the full speed, $v^2=v_\perp^2+v_\parallel^2$, and the radial part is unknown. Without $E$, neither $a$ nor the perihelion is fixed. (A) The shape depends on the sign of $E=\frac12mv^2-\frac{GMm}r$, which needs $M$. (C) If the perihelion is smaller than $R$, it hits the sun. (D) At the aphelion the velocity is purely perpendicular.

Exercise 10 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.4(b), 1 point, original)

You've found the orbit to be an ellipse. Which additional information do you need to determine the period $T$? ($M$, $R$, $G$, $r$ and $v_\perp$ are known.) (A) The radial velocity component $v_\parallel$ (towards or away from the sun). (B) The mass $m$ of the asteroid. (C) The angular momentum $L$ of the asteroid. (D) The period $T$ and semi-major axis $a$ of another body orbiting the sun.

Solution

A. With $v_\parallel$ you have $v^2$, hence $E$ per mass, hence $a$ from $E=-\frac{GMm}{2a}$, hence $T$ from Kepler 3. (B) The mass cancels. (C) $L/m=rv_\perp$ is already known and doesn't fix $a$. (D) Gives only $\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}$, which you already know.

Exercise 11 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.4(c), 1 point, original)

What holds for the asteroid on its elliptic orbit around the sun? (A) At its closest point it has a larger orbital angular momentum $L$ than at its farthest point. (B) If you only knew the shape of the orbit, $x(y)$, you couldn't always determine the exact position of the sun. (C) Its kinetic energy is constant at all times. (D) During one revolution the sun switches between the two foci of the ellipse.

Solution

B. The shape alone has two foci, and the sun could be in either; you'd need to know where the body is fastest. (A) $L$ is constant. (C) $T$ is largest at perihelion. (D) The sun stays in one focus.

Exercise 12 (FS24, problem 3 “Satellit”, parts (a)–(e), 7 points, original)

A satellite of mass $m$ is on a circular orbit around the earth (mass $M_E$) with a period of two hours. (a) Compute its angular velocity $\omega_{S1}$ in rad/h. [1 point] (b) Give an expression for its distance $r_{S1}$ from the earth's centre. [2 points] (c) Give an expression for its orbital speed $v_{S1}$. [1 point] You now move it to a geostationary orbit (one revolution per day), a circle with radius $r_{S2}$ and speed $v_{S2}$. (d) How much energy $\Delta E$ is needed? Give it in terms of the two radii and speeds. [2 points] (e) Is the orbital speed now larger or smaller than before? Explain with the relation between speed and radius. [1 point]

Solution

(a) $\omega_{S1}=\frac{2\pi}{T}=\frac{2\pi}{2\,\mathrm h}=\pi\,\mathrm{rad/h}$.

(b) Circular orbit: $\frac{GM_Em}{r_{S1}^2}=m\omega_{S1}^2r_{S1}$, so $r_{S1}=\left(\frac{GM_E}{\omega_{S1}^2}\right)^{1/3}$.

(c) $v_{S1}=\omega_{S1}r_{S1}=\left(GM_E\,\omega_{S1}\right)^{1/3}$, or equivalently $\sqrt{GM_E/r_{S1}}$.

(d) The total energy on each orbit is $E=\frac12mv^2-\frac{GM_Em}r$, so

$$\Delta E=\tfrac12m\left(v_{S2}^2-v_{S1}^2\right)-GM_Em\left(\frac1{r_{S2}}-\frac1{r_{S1}}\right).$$

With $v^2=\frac{GM_E}r$ on a circle this simplifies to $\Delta E=\frac{GM_Em}2\left(\frac1{r_{S1}}-\frac1{r_{S2}}\right)$ $=\frac12m\left(v_{S1}^2-v_{S2}^2\right)>0$.

(e) Smaller: $v=\sqrt{GM_E/r}$ decreases with $r$, and $r_{S2}=12^{2/3}r_{S1}\approx5.2\,r_{S1}$ (Kepler 3 with twelve times the period). You add energy and end up slower: the potential energy grows by twice what the kinetic energy loses.

Exercise 13 (FS25, problem 4 “Asteroid”, parts (a)–(d), 6 points, original)

An asteroid of mass $m$ is on a collision course with the earth (mass $M$, radius $R$): unperturbed, it would hit the surface at point K. Its orbit is an ellipse with a focus at the earth's centre and semi-major axis $a-\frac R2$. At point T, which lies on the ellipse's minor axis (straight “above” the ellipse's centre in the figure), a rocket is to hit it; afterwards its orbit is an ellipse with semi-major axis $a+\frac R2$. Express everything with $a$ and $R$.

(a) What are the potential energies $E_{\text{pot}}^T$ and $E_{\text{pot}}^K$? Hint: the distance focus 1 – any point of the ellipse – focus 2 always equals twice the semi-major axis. [2 points] (b) By what factor is the asteroid faster at K than at T? Simplify $v_K/v_T$. Hint: $E_{\text{ges}}=-G\frac{Mm}{2a}$. [2 points] (c) The rocket hits it in a perfectly elastic collision and puts it on the new orbit. By how much did its total energy change? [1 point] (d) What is its speed $v_T'$ at T just after the collision, if $v_T$ was its speed just before? [1 point]

Solution

(a) K lies on the earth's surface: $E_{\text{pot}}^K=-\frac{GMm}R$. T lies on the minor axis, equally far from both foci; since the two distances add up to $2\left(a-\frac R2\right)$, each is $a-\frac R2$:

$$E_{\text{pot}}^T=-\frac{GMm}{a-\frac R2}=-\frac{2GMm}{2a-R}.$$

(b) With $E=-\frac{GMm}{2(a-R/2)}=-\frac{GMm}{2a-R}$, energy conservation gives $\frac12mv^2=E-E_{\text{pot}}$. At T: $\frac12mv_T^2=\frac{GMm}{2a-R}$. At K: $\frac12mv_K^2=\frac{GMm}R-\frac{GMm}{2a-R}$. Dividing:

$$\frac{v_K^2}{v_T^2}=\frac{2a-R}R-1=\frac{2(a-R)}R\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{v_K}{v_T}=\sqrt{\frac{2(a-R)}R}.$$

(c) $\Delta E_{\text{ges}}=-\frac{GMm}{2a+R}+\frac{GMm}{2a-R}=\frac{2GMmR}{4a^2-R^2}$. Positive: the larger orbit has more energy.

(d) At T the position, hence the potential energy, is the same just before and after; all the extra energy is kinetic: $\frac12mv_T'^2=\frac12mv_T^2+\Delta E_{\text{ges}}$. With $v_T^2=\frac{2GM}{2a-R}$:

$$v_T'=\sqrt{v_T^2+\frac{4GMR}{4a^2-R^2}}=v_T\sqrt{\frac{2a+3R}{2a+R}}.$$

Parts (e) and (f) need the exact position of point P from the figure; try them with the original once you're confident.

Exercise 14 (FS26, problem 3 “Weltraumlift”, parts (d) and (e), 4 points, original)

A rigid rod sticks radially out from the equator of a planet (mass $M$, radius $R$) that rotates with constant $\omega$. A massless lift carries a test mass $m$ along the rod, rotating with the planet. In the rotating frame, the mass at radius $r$ feels $F_{\text{eff}}(r)=-\frac{GMm}{r^2}+m\omega^2r$ (outwards positive; part (c), lesson 4), and the geostationary radius is $r_S=\left(\frac{GM}{\omega^2}\right)^{1/3}$ (part (b)). (d) Compute the mechanical work $W$ the lift motor must do to carry $m$ from $r=R$ to $r=r_S$, and compare with the work without rotation, $GMm\left(\frac1R-\frac1{r_S}\right)$ (part (a), lesson 5). [2 points] (e) At the end of the rod, $r=r_{\text{end}}$, the mass is released. What is its speed $v_0$ in the inertial frame? How long must the rod be at least for the mass to escape the planet's gravity? [2 points]

Solution

(d) Carrying slowly, the motor balances $F_{\text{eff}}$: it pushes outward with $-F_{\text{eff}}$.

$$W=\int_R^{r_S}\left(\frac{GMm}{r^2}-m\omega^2r\right)dr=GMm\left(\frac1R-\frac1{r_S}\right)-\tfrac12m\omega^2\left(r_S^2-R^2\right).$$

Less than without rotation: the centrifugal force helps. (In the inertial frame the rest of the energy comes from the planet's rotation, through the sideways push of the rod, lesson 7.)

(e) The mass co-rotates: $v_0=\omega r_{\text{end}}$, perpendicular to the rod. It escapes if $E\ge0$:

$$\tfrac12m\omega^2r_{\text{end}}^2\ge\frac{GMm}{r_{\text{end}}}\iff r_{\text{end}}\ge\left(\frac{2GM}{\omega^2}\right)^{1/3}=2^{1/3}\,r_S\approx1.26\,r_S.$$

Scoring yourself: the exercises make 29 points. With 19 you're done. Exercises 12–14 are the long-problem style; if (d) of Exercise 12 works without looking, orbit energies are yours.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

A planet found with a pencil. In the 1840s Uranus drifted slightly off its Kepler orbit. Le Verrier assumed an unknown outer planet was pulling it, computed where it had to be, and wrote to the Berlin observatory. Galle pointed the telescope there on the night the letter arrived, 23 September 1846, and found Neptune within one degree.

The planet that wasn't. Mercury's perihelion also creeps forward, by 43 arcseconds per century more than all the planets can explain (script, page 171). Le Verrier tried the same trick and predicted a planet “Vulcan” inside Mercury's orbit. It was never found. The answer came in 1915: Einstein's general relativity gives exactly $43.03''$ per century. Newton's law is an approximation, and Mercury, closest to the sun, is where it first shows.
