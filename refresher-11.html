1. Warm-up (5 min)

Three quick questions. Answer each in your head, then open it.

  1. Is $y=e^{3x}$ a solution of $y'=3y$? Name all solutions.

    Answer

    Yes: $(e^{3x})'=3e^{3x}$. All solutions are $y=Ce^{3x}$, $C\in\mathbb R$ (lesson 22, Proof B with $f=-3$, $g=0$).

  2. Which type is $y'=x^2y+x^2$?

    Answer

    Both separable ($y'=x^2(y+1)$) and linear ($y'-x^2y=x^2$). Separating is faster here: $y=Ce^{x^3/3}-1$.

  3. Write the characteristic polynomial of $y''+2y'+5y=0$ and its roots.

    Answer

    $p(T)=T^2+2T+5$, roots $-1\pm2i$. Real solutions: $e^{-x}\bigl(C_1\cos2x+C_2\sin2x\bigr)$.

2. Recap card: lesson 22 (15 min)

Read this once, slowly. Everything in bold must come out exactly in the exam.

Lesson 22: differential equations

  • Separablemaximal interval (the largest interval containing $x_0$ where the formula makes sense). Every zero $c$ of $g$ gives the constant solution $y\equiv c$.
  • Linear first order, $y'+f(x)y=g(x)$: normal form first (watch the sign of $f$); homogeneous solutions $Ae^{-F(x)}$; variation of constants: $y_p=A(x)e^{-F(x)}$ gives $A'=g\,e^{F}$; then fit $A$ to the initial value. With $f=\frac kx$: $y_{\text{hom}}=Ax^{-k}$.
  • Superposition: general solution = one particular solution + all homogeneous solutions. For a sum of forcing terms, add the particular solutions.
  • Second order, $y''+a_1y'+a_0y=0$: roots of $p(T)=T^2+a_1T+a_0$. Two real roots: $C_1e^{\alpha_1x}+C_2e^{\alpha_2x}$. Double root: $(C_1+C_2x)e^{\alpha x}$. Complex $\beta\pm i\gamma$: $e^{\beta x}(C_1\cos\gamma x+C_2\sin\gamma x)$.
  • Ansatz table (script p. 405): polynomial of degree $n$ → polynomial of degree $n$; $q(x)e^{\alpha x}$ → $Q(x)e^{\alpha x}$; $q(x)\cos\gamma x$ or $\sin$ → $Q_1\cos\gamma x+Q_2\sin\gamma x$. Resonance: if the exponent ($0$, $\alpha$ or $i\gamma$) is a root of $p$ of multiplicity $\ell$, multiply the Ansatz by $x^\ell$.
  • Initial values come last: fit $C_1,C_2$ to the whole solution $y_p+y_h$, never to $y_h$ alone.
  • Decision list: constant coefficients in front of $y'',y',y$ → characteristic polynomial + Ansatz. First order and linear in $y$ → variation of constants. Right side factors as $f(x)g(y)$ → separate. Multiple choice → you may also check each option against the equation and the initial values.

4. Write it on paper: two short proofs (15 min, plus 10 optional)

These are not the usual exam questions, but they are why the recipes work, and writing them once makes the recipes stick. For each proof: read the claim and the frame, close your eyes for ten seconds, then write the whole proof on paper. Only then open the fold and mark yourself with the grader's list.

Proof 1: separation of variables

Claim. Let $f:I\to\mathbb R$, $g:J\to\mathbb R$ be continuous on open intervals, with $g(y)\ne0$ for all $y\in J$. Let $F'=f$ and $H'=\frac1g$. A differentiable $y:I'\to J$ on an interval $I'\subseteq I$ solves $y'=f(x)g(y)$ if and only if $H(y(x))=F(x)+C$ for a constant $C$. (Lesson 22, Proof A.)

What the definitions force

Two directions. “⇒”: show that $H(y(x))-F(x)$ has derivative $0$ on an interval. “⇐”: differentiate $H(y(x))=F(x)+C$. The tool both times: the chain rule.

The proof and the grader's list

“⇒”: by the chain rule, $(H\circ y)'(x)=\frac{y'(x)}{g(y(x))}=f(x)=F'(x)$ for all $x\in I'$. So $(H\circ y-F)'=0$ on the interval $I'$, and $H\circ y-F$ is constant (mean value theorem).

“⇐”: differentiating $H(y(x))=F(x)+C$ gives $\frac{y'(x)}{g(y(x))}=f(x)$, that is $y'=f(x)g(y)$. ∎

(Why you may solve for $y$: $g$ is continuous without zeros on the interval $J$, so it has constant sign by the intermediate value theorem; hence $H$ is strictly monotone and invertible.)

A grader gives the points for: both directions; the chain rule for $(H\circ y)'$; “derivative $0$ on an interval ⇒ constant”.

Proof 2: all solutions of $y'+f(x)y=g(x)$ (Lemma 8.60)

Claim. Let $f,g:I\to\mathbb R$ be continuous on an interval $I$, $F'=f$, and $G'=g\,e^F$. Then the solutions of $y'+f(x)y=g(x)$ on $I$ are exactly $y=e^{-F}(G+C)$, $C\in\mathbb R$. (Lesson 22, Proof B, script p. 398–399.)

What the definitions force

Three steps, the same as the recipe: all homogeneous solutions; one particular solution by varying the constant; then “general = particular + homogeneous”. The key line: the product rule gives $(ye^F)'=(y'+fy)e^F$.

The proof and the grader's list

Homogeneous. For differentiable $y$, $(ye^F)'=(y'+fy)e^F$. So $y'+fy=0$ iff $ye^F$ is constant on the interval $I$, i.e. $y=Ae^{-F}$.

Particular. For $y_p=Ge^{-F}$: $y_p'+fy_p=G'e^{-F}-fGe^{-F}+fGe^{-F}=ge^Fe^{-F}=g$.

All. If $y$ is any solution, then $y-y_p$ solves the homogeneous equation (the equation is linear), so $y-y_p=Ce^{-F}$. Conversely, $y_p+Ce^{-F}$ is a solution. So the solutions are exactly $e^{-F}(G+C)$. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • the product-rule identity $(ye^F)'=(y'+fy)e^F$ and “constant on an interval”;
  • the particular solution checked by plugging in;
  • both inclusions of “general = particular + homogeneous”.

In short: “solve without $g$, then let the constant vary”.

Proof 3 (optional, 10 min): the second-order homogeneous equation (Section 8.5.4)

Claim. If $p(T)=T^2+a_1T+a_0=(T-\alpha_1)(T-\alpha_2)$, the solutions of $y''+a_1y'+a_0y=0$ are exactly $C_1e^{\alpha_1x}+C_2e^{\alpha_2x}$ if $\alpha_1\ne\alpha_2$, and $(C_1+C_2x)e^{\alpha x}$ if $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha$. (Lesson 22, Proof D, script p. 401–402.)

What the definitions force

Factor the equation like the polynomial: with $z=y'-\alpha_2y$, the equation becomes $z'-\alpha_1z=0$. So it's two first-order equations in a row, and Proof 2 solves each.

The proof and the grader's list

Since $a_1=-(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)$ and $a_0=\alpha_1\alpha_2$, the function $z=y'-\alpha_2y$ satisfies $z'-\alpha_1z=y''+a_1y'+a_0y=0$, so $z=Ae^{\alpha_1x}$ (Proof 2, with complex constants). Then $$\begin{aligned}(ye^{-\alpha_2x})'&=(y'-\alpha_2y)e^{-\alpha_2x}\\&=Ae^{(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)x}.\end{aligned}$$ If $\alpha_1\ne\alpha_2$, integrate: $ye^{-\alpha_2x}=\frac A{\alpha_1-\alpha_2}e^{(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)x}+C_2$, so $y=C_1e^{\alpha_1x}+C_2e^{\alpha_2x}$. If $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha$: $ye^{-\alpha x}=Ax+C_1$, so $y=(C_1+C_2x)e^{\alpha x}$. Conversely these functions are solutions: plug in ($L(e^{\alpha x})=p(\alpha)e^{\alpha x}$, and $L(xe^{\alpha x})=(p(\alpha)x+p'(\alpha))e^{\alpha x}$, which is $0$ at a double root). ∎

A grader gives the points for: the substitution $z=y'-\alpha_2y$ and the factorised equation; solving for $z$, then for $y$; both cases; the converse.

5. Drill: eight equations (25 min)

For each: first name the type (separable, linear first order, or second order with constant coefficients) and, for second order, the Ansatz. Then solve on paper and check the initial values. Open the fold when your answer is done.

  1. $y'=xy$, $y(0)=2$.

    Type and answer

    Separable: $\int\frac{dy}y=\int x\,dx$, $\log|y|=\frac{x^2}2+c$, so $y=Ke^{x^2/2}$; $y(0)=2$ gives $y=2e^{x^2/2}$ on all of $\mathbb R$.

  2. $y'+y=x$, $y(0)=0$.

    Type and answer

    Linear, $f=1$, $F=x$: $y_{\text{hom}}=Ae^{-x}$. Vary: $A'=xe^x$, $A=(x-1)e^x$, so $y_p=x-1$. Then $y=x-1+Ce^{-x}$, and $y(0)=0$ gives $C=1$: $y=x-1+e^{-x}$.

  3. $y'=y^2$, $y(0)=1$. Also give the maximal interval.

    Type and answer

    Separable: $-\frac1y=x+C$, and $y(0)=1$ gives $C=-1$: $y=\frac1{1-x}$. It blows up at $x=1$, so the maximal interval containing $0$ is $(-\infty,1)$.

  4. $y''-3y'+2y=0$, $y(0)=0$, $y'(0)=1$.

    Type and answer

    $p(T)=T^2-3T+2=(T-1)(T-2)$: $y=C_1e^x+C_2e^{2x}$. $C_1+C_2=0$, $C_1+2C_2=1$: $C_2=1$, $C_1=-1$. So $y=e^{2x}-e^x$.

  5. $y''+4y=0$, $y(0)=1$, $y'(0)=2$.

    Type and answer

    Roots $\pm2i$: $y=C_1\cos2x+C_2\sin2x$. $y(0)=C_1=1$, $y'(0)=2C_2=2$. So $y=\cos2x+\sin2x$.

  6. $y''-2y'+y=0$, $y(0)=1$, $y'(0)=0$.

    Type and answer

    Double root $1$: $y=(C_1+C_2x)e^x$. $y(0)=C_1=1$, $y'(0)=C_1+C_2=0$, so $C_2=-1$: $y=(1-x)e^x$.

  7. Find all solutions of $y''+y=x$.

    Type and answer

    Homogeneous: roots $\pm i$, $C_1\cos x+C_2\sin x$. Forcing term: a polynomial of degree 1, and $0$ is not a root of $p$, so $y_p=ax+b$: $ax+b=x$, $y_p=x$. All solutions: $y=x+C_1\cos x+C_2\sin x$.

  8. $y''-4y=e^{2x}$, $y(0)=0$, $y'(0)=\frac14$.

    Type and answer

    $p(T)=(T-2)(T+2)$, and the exponent $2$ is a simple root: resonance, so $y_p=axe^{2x}$. Then $y_p''=a(4+4x)e^{2x}$ and $y_p''-4y_p=4ae^{2x}$, so $a=\frac14$. General: $y=\frac x4e^{2x}+C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{-2x}$. $y(0)=C_1+C_2=0$, $y'(0)=\frac14+2C_1-2C_2=\frac14$, so $C_1=C_2=0$: $y=\frac x4e^{2x}$.

Seven or more right, with the type named first? Then differential equations are safe points for you. Fewer? Redo the ones you missed tomorrow, without looking.

6. Spot the mistake (5 min)

A student solves an initial value problem and hands in this. The answer is wrong. Where exactly does it break?

“Solution” of $y''-y=2$, $y(0)=0$, $y'(0)=0$. The homogeneous solutions are $C_1e^x+C_2e^{-x}$. The initial values give $C_1+C_2=0$ and $C_1-C_2=0$, so $C_1=C_2=0$. A particular solution is $y_p=-2$. So $y=-2$.

The mistake

The constants were fitted to the homogeneous part alone. The initial values belong to the whole solution $y=-2+C_1e^x+C_2e^{-x}$. Quick check: the answer $y=-2$ has $y(0)=-2\ne0$.

Correct: $y(0)=-2+C_1+C_2=0$ and $y'(0)=C_1-C_2=0$, so $C_1=C_2=1$ and $y=e^x+e^{-x}-2=2\cosh x-2$.

Exam lesson: the order is always homogeneous → particular → add → then initial values. And check the initial values in your final answer: it takes ten seconds.
