Serie 3, explained: Bases

Why a basis gives every vector exactly one set of coordinates, the Steinitz exchange done by hand, three different bases of the Fibonacci space, an unusual basis of the polynomials, and one set of numbers that is a vector space over four different fields, with a different basis each time.

🌧

You're done when
you can (1) prove that coordinates with respect to a basis are unique; (2) carry out the Steinitz exchange step by step on concrete vectors; (3) find a basis of a space of sequences by looking at what determines a sequence; (4) prove that a family of polynomials is independent and spanning by looking at degrees; (5) explain why the same set can have different bases, and even different dimensions, over different fields.

Aufgabe 15 · Coordinates in a basis are unique

Let $B\subseteq V$ be a basis of the vector space $V$ over $K$. Show: every $x\in V$ can be written in exactly one way (up to the order of the summands) as a linear combination of vectors of $B$ with non-zero coefficients.

In short: Existence is “$B$ spans”. Uniqueness: subtract two expressions and use independence.

The idea

Two ways of writing the same vector give, after subtraction, a way of writing $0$. Independence says the only way to write $0$ is with all coefficients $0$. The only fiddly part is bookkeeping: the two expressions may use different basis vectors. Put all vectors that occur in either expression into one list $z_1,\dots,z_k$, with coefficient $0$ where a vector does not occur.

You need

Lesson 6, section 4: vectors $v_1,\dots,v_n$ are linearly independent iff $\sum\lambda_iv_i=0$ forces all $\lambda_i=0$; equivalently, no $v_k$ lies in the span of the others (or of the earlier ones). Lesson 6, section 5: a basis is a linearly independent spanning set.

Hint 1

Let $x=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_ix_i=\sum_{j=1}^m\mu_jy_j$ with $x_i,y_j\in B$ and all coefficients non-zero. List the distinct vectors of $\{x_i\}\cup\{y_j\}$ as $z_1,\dots,z_k$ and rewrite both sums over the $z_l$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Existence: $B$ spans $V$. The zero vector is the empty combination.

Since $\langle B\rangle=V$, every $x\in V$ is a linear combination of vectors of $B$; dropping terms with coefficient $0$ leaves non-zero coefficients. For $x=0_V$ this is the empty combination, which is unique.

Uniqueness: take two expressions and put all occurring vectors in one list.

Let $x\ne0_V$ and $x=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_ix_i=\sum_{j=1}^m\mu_jy_j$ with distinct $x_i\in B$, distinct $y_j\in B$, and $\lambda_i,\mu_j\in K\setminus\{0\}$. Let $z_1,\dots,z_k$ be the distinct vectors in $\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}\cup\{y_1,\dots,y_m\}$, and let $\alpha_l:=\lambda_i$ if $z_l=x_i$, else $0$; $\beta_l:=\mu_j$ if $z_l=y_j$, else $0$.

Subtract: a combination of distinct basis vectors equal to $0$.

Then $x=\sum_{l=1}^k\alpha_lz_l=\sum_{l=1}^k\beta_lz_l$, so $0_V=\sum_{l=1}^k(\alpha_l-\beta_l)z_l$.

Independence forces equal coefficients. Non-zero coefficients then match the same vectors.

The $z_l$ are distinct elements of the basis $B$, hence linearly independent, so $\alpha_l=\beta_l$ for all $l$. Since all $\lambda_i,\mu_j\ne0$, a vector $z_l$ occurs in the first sum iff $\alpha_l\ne0$ iff $\beta_l\ne0$ iff it occurs in the second, with the same coefficient. So $n=m$ and the two expressions agree up to order. ∎

Trap

Writing both expressions with the same vectors $b_1,\dots,b_n$ from the start (“$x=\sum\lambda_ib_i=\sum\mu_ib_i$”). That is the right idea, but you must say why you may: pad with zero coefficients. And “non-zero coefficients” matters: without it, $x=1\cdot b_1=1\cdot b_1+0\cdot b_2$ would be two different expressions.

Aufgabe 16 · The Steinitz exchange by hand

In $V=\mathbb Q^4$ let $x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4$ be the standard basis vectors, $y_1:=(1,1,1,0)^T$, $y_2:=(0,1,1,0)^T$. Then $\{x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4\}$ is a basis and $y_1,y_2$ are independent. Using the Steinitz exchange theorem, replace two of $x_1,\dots,x_4$ by $y_1,y_2$ so that $\{y_1,y_2,x_{i_1},x_{i_2}\}$ is a basis. Official hint: start with the sequence $S_2=(y_2,x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)$.

In short: Put $y_2$ in front, delete the first $x_i$ that depends on the vectors before it; then put $y_1$ in front and repeat. Result: $\{y_1,y_2,x_2,x_4\}$.

The idea

The exchange in the lecture works like a queue. Put the new vector at the front of a spanning list. The list is now dependent, so some vector depends on the ones before it, and the first such vector is never a $y$ (the $y$'s are independent). Delete that vector: the span does not change. Repeat with the next $y$. You only need to test, left to right, whether each $x_i$ is a combination of what comes before it.

You need

Lesson 7, section 3 (the exchange: spanning lists are at least as long as independent lists). Deleting a vector that is a combination of the others keeps the span (lesson 7, section 2).

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Step 1: the official hint. $y_2$ is a combination of the $x$'s, so the list is dependent. Test left to right.

Step 1. $S_2:=(y_2,x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)$ spans $V$ and is dependent. $\{y_2,x_1\}$ and $\{y_2,x_1,x_2\}$ are independent, but $y_2=(0,1,1,0)^T=x_2+x_3$, so $x_3=y_2-x_2$ is a combination of the vectors before it.

Delete it: the span stays $V$.

Delete $x_3$: $S_2'=(y_2,x_1,x_2,x_4)$ and $\langle S_2'\rangle=\langle S_2\rangle=V$.

Step 2: put $y_1$ in front and test again.

Step 2. $S_1:=(y_1,y_2,x_1,x_2,x_4)$ spans $V$ and is dependent. Already $\{y_1,y_2,x_1\}$ is dependent: $y_1=(1,1,1,0)^T=x_1+y_2$, so $x_1=y_1-y_2$.

Delete $x_1$. Four vectors spanning a 4-dimensional space form a basis.

Delete $x_1$: $S_1'=(y_1,y_2,x_2,x_4)$ with $\langle S_1'\rangle=V$. Four vectors spanning the 4-dimensional space $V$ are a basis, so $\{y_1,y_2,x_2,x_4\}$ is a basis of $V$. ∎

Correction to the official solution: the Musterlösung writes “$x_3=y_2-x_3$” in step 1; it should be $x_3=y_2-x_2$, since $y_2=x_2+x_3$. Check: $y_2-x_2=(0,0,1,0)^T=x_3$.

Trap

Deleting a $y$, or deleting any dependent vector instead of following the rule. The exchange always removes an $x$: the first vector (from the left) that is a combination of the ones before it. In step 2, $x_2$ is not removed because $\{y_1,y_2,x_1\}$ is already dependent, so $x_1$ goes first.

Aufgabe 17 · Three bases of the Fibonacci space

Find 3 different bases of the vector space $\mathrm{Fib}$ from Aufgabe 11.

In short: A Fibonacci-type sequence is fixed by its first two terms. So two sequences whose starting pairs are independent in $\mathbb R^2$ form a basis.

The idea

Once you know $f_0$ and $f_1$, the recursion $f_{n+2}=f_{n+1}+f_n$ fixes everything else. So $\mathrm{Fib}$ “is” $\mathbb R^2$ in disguise: the map $(f_n)\mapsto(f_0,f_1)$ is a bijection that respects addition and scaling. A basis of $\mathbb R^2$ therefore gives a basis of $\mathrm{Fib}$. The most interesting choice uses the two solutions $\varphi,\psi$ of $t^2=t+1$: the geometric sequences $(1,t,t^2,\dots)$ are Fibonacci-type, and in that basis you get the closed formula for the Fibonacci numbers.

You need

Lesson 6, section 5: a basis is a linearly independent spanning set. Serie 2, Aufgabe 11 ($\mathrm{Fib}$ is a vector space). Lesson 6, section 5.

Hint 1

Write $F_{a,b}$ for the sequence in $\mathrm{Fib}$ with $f_0=a$, $f_1=b$. Show it exists and is unique (induction). Then show: if $(a,b),(c,d)$ are independent in $\mathbb R^2$, $\{F_{a,b},F_{c,d}\}$ is a basis.

Hint 2

Candidates: $\{F_{1,0},F_{0,1}\}$, $\{F_{1,1},F_{2,1}\}$, and $\{F_{1,\varphi},F_{1,\psi}\}$ with $\varphi,\psi=\frac{1\pm\sqrt5}2$. Why is $F_{1,t}=(1,t,t^2,\dots)$ for $t^2=t+1$?

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

A Fibonacci-type sequence is determined by its first two terms.

Lemma. For $a,b\in\mathbb R$ there is exactly one $F_{a,b}=(f_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$ with $f_0=a$, $f_1=b$. Existence: define recursively $f_{i+2}:=f_{i+1}+f_i$. Uniqueness: if $(f_n),(g_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$ agree at $0$ and $1$, induction on $n$ shows $f_n=g_n$ and $f_{n+1}=g_{n+1}$, since $f_{n+2}=f_{n+1}+f_n=g_{n+1}+g_n=g_{n+2}$.

Independent starting pairs give a basis: coordinates of any $f$ come from solving for its first two terms.

Claim. If $(a,b),(c,d)$ are linearly independent in $\mathbb R^2$, then $\{F_{a,b},F_{c,d}\}$ is a basis of $\mathrm{Fib}$. Spanning: for $f\in\mathrm{Fib}$ choose $\lambda,\mu$ with $\lambda(a,b)+\mu(c,d)=(f_0,f_1)$ (possible, as the pairs span $\mathbb R^2$). Then $\lambda F_{a,b}+\mu F_{c,d}$ and $f$ are in $\mathrm{Fib}$ with the same first two terms, so they are equal. Independence: $\lambda F_{a,b}+\mu F_{c,d}=0$ gives $\lambda(a,b)+\mu(c,d)=(0,0)$ in the first two terms, so $\lambda=\mu=0$.

Two easy bases: standard starting pairs, and $(1,1),(2,1)$.

$B_1:=\{F_{1,0},F_{0,1}\}=\{(1,0,1,1,2,3,\dots),(0,1,1,2,3,5,\dots)\}$ and $B_2:=\{F_{1,1},F_{2,1}\}=\{(1,1,2,3,5,\dots),(2,1,3,4,7,\dots)\}$; the pairs $(1,0),(0,1)$ and $(1,1),(2,1)$ are independent.

The geometric basis: $t^2=t+1$ makes $(t^n)$ Fibonacci-type.

Let $\varphi:=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2$, $\psi:=\frac{1-\sqrt5}2$, the solutions of $t^2=t+1$. For these $t$: $t^{i+2}=t^it^2=t^i(t+1)=t^{i+1}+t^i$, so $(t^n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$, and by the lemma $(t^n)=F_{1,t}$. Since $\varphi\ne\psi$, $(1,\varphi),(1,\psi)$ are independent, and $B_3:=\{F_{1,\varphi},F_{1,\psi}\}$ is a basis.

They are different: no two of the six sequences have the same starting pair.

The six sequences have distinct starting pairs, so $B_1,B_2,B_3$ are three different bases. ∎

Why $B_3$ is worth it (official remark): writing the Fibonacci numbers $F_{0,1}$ in the basis $B_3$ gives $F_{0,1}=\frac1{\sqrt5}F_{1,\varphi}-\frac1{\sqrt5}F_{1,\psi}$, and reading off the $n$-th term: $$f_n=\frac1{\sqrt5}\Big(\varphi^n-\psi^n\Big).$$ This is Binet's formula. So $\mathrm{Fib}$ has dimension 2, and infinitely many bases. Serie 6, Aufgabe 29 finds $\varphi,\psi$ again as eigenvalues.

Trap

Giving two bases that are the same set in a different order, or a “basis” like $\{F_{1,1},F_{2,2}\}$ whose starting pairs are proportional. And checking only that each sequence lies in $\mathrm{Fib}$: you must also show they span and are independent; the claim about starting pairs does both.

Aufgabe 18 · An unusual basis of the polynomials

In $V:=\mathbb Q[X]$ let $x_0:=1$, $x_1:=1+2X$, $x_2:=1+3X+3X^2$, $x_3:=1+4X+4X^2+4X^3$, …, in general $x_n:=1+(n+1)\sum_{i=1}^nX^i$. (a) Show that $\mathcal X=\{x_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$ is linearly independent. (b) Show that $\langle\mathcal X\rangle=\mathbb Q[X]$. So $\mathcal X$ is a basis of $\mathbb Q[X]$.

In short: $x_n$ has degree exactly $n$. (a) the highest-degree vector in a combination cannot be cancelled; (b) by induction every $X^n$ is a combination of $x_0,\dots,x_n$.

The idea

Look at degrees. $x_n$ has degree $n$, with top coefficient $n+1\ne0$, and it is the only vector in the family that contains $X^n$ among $x_0,\dots,x_n$. So in a combination $\sum_{i\le n}\lambda_ix_i$ with $\lambda_n\ne0$, the $X^n$ term cannot cancel. For (b), climb a ladder: if $1,X,\dots,X^n$ are already combinations of $x_0,\dots,x_n$, then $x_{n+1}$ minus known pieces leaves $(n+2)X^{n+1}$.

You need

Lesson 6, section 4: vectors $v_1,\dots,v_n$ are linearly independent iff $\sum\lambda_iv_i=0$ forces all $\lambda_i=0$; equivalently, no $v_k$ lies in the span of the others (or of the earlier ones). Lesson 6, section 5: a basis is a linearly independent spanning set. The monomials $1,X,X^2,\dots$ form a basis of $\mathbb Q[X]$ (lesson 6, section 5). Induction (lesson 3, section 5).

Hint 1

(a) Suppose $\sum_{i=0}^n\lambda_ix_i=0$ with $\lambda_n\ne0$. Which of $x_0,\dots,x_n$ contain $X^n$? What is the coefficient of $X^n$ in the sum?

Hint 2

(b) It is enough to get every $X^k$ (they span $\mathbb Q[X]$). Induction on $n$: assume $1,X,\dots,X^n\in\langle x_0,\dots,x_n\rangle$ and write $x_{n+1}=1+(n+2)(X+\dots+X^n)+(n+2)X^{n+1}$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

(a) It is enough to show every finite part $x_0,\dots,x_n$ is independent. Look at the top degree.

(a) It suffices to show that $x_0,\dots,x_n$ are independent for every $n$. Suppose $\sum_{i=0}^k\lambda_ix_i=0$ with $k\le n$ and $\lambda_k\ne0$ (the highest index with non-zero coefficient).

Only $x_k$ contains $X^k$ among $x_0,\dots,x_k$; its coefficient is $k+1$ (or $1$ for $k=0$).

If $k=0$: $\lambda_0\cdot1=0$, so $\lambda_0=0$, a contradiction. If $k\ge1$: among $x_0,\dots,x_k$ only $x_k$ has a term $X^k$, with coefficient $k+1$. So the coefficient of $X^k$ in the sum is $\lambda_k(k+1)\ne0$, a contradiction. Hence all $\lambda_i=0$, and $\mathcal X$ is linearly independent.

(b) The monomials span, so it is enough to reach each $X^n$. Induction on $n$.

(b) Since $\{X^k:k\in\mathbb N\}$ spans $\mathbb Q[X]$ and $\langle\mathcal X\rangle$ is a subspace, it suffices to show $X^k\in\langle\mathcal X\rangle$ for all $k$. We prove by induction: $1,X,\dots,X^n\in\langle x_0,\dots,x_n\rangle$. For $n=0$: $1=x_0$.

Step: peel off the known terms of $x_{n+1}$.

Assume it for $n$, and let $y:=1+(n+2)(X+\dots+X^n)$. Then $$(n+2)X^{n+1}=x_{n+1}-y,$$ and $y\in\langle x_0,\dots,x_n\rangle$ by the hypothesis, so the right side lies in $\langle x_0,\dots,x_{n+1}\rangle$. Dividing by $n+2\ne0$ gives $X^{n+1}\in\langle x_0,\dots,x_{n+1}\rangle$. So $\langle\mathcal X\rangle=\mathbb Q[X]$, and $\mathcal X$ is a basis. ∎

Source: this follows the official solution (independence via “$x_{n+1}$ is not in the span of $x_0,\dots,x_n$”, spanning by induction), written more compactly.

Trap

In (a), checking only that no single $x_n$ is a multiple of another. Independence is about all finite combinations. And in (b), forgetting that you need every polynomial: the induction gives the monomials, and the monomials give everything.

Aufgabe 19 · One space, four fields

Let $V:=\{q_0+q_1\sqrt2+q_2\sqrt3+q_3\sqrt6:q_0,\dots,q_3\in\mathbb Q\}$ with the addition of $\mathbb R$. Let $K_0:=\mathbb Q$, $K_1:=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$, $K_2:=\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)$, $K_3:=\mathbb Q(\sqrt6)$, and $V_i$ the vector space $V$ over $K_i$.

(a) Find a basis $B_0$ of $V_0$. (b) Find a basis $B_1$ of $V_1$ that is not a basis of $V_2$. (c) Find a basis $B_3$ of $V_3$. (d) Find a basis $B_{2,3}$ of $V_1$ that is also a basis of $V_2$ but not of $V_3$.

In short: Over $\mathbb Q$: $\{1,\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt6\}$. Over the bigger fields the dimension is 2: (b) $\{1,\sqrt3\}$, (c) $\{\sqrt2,\sqrt6\}$, (d) $\{\sqrt2,\sqrt3\}$.

The idea

The vectors are the same numbers each time; only the scalars change. With bigger scalars you need fewer basis vectors: over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$, the scalar $a+b\sqrt2$ times $1$ already gives $a+b\sqrt2$, so $\{1,\sqrt3\}$ covers all four rational coordinates. Everything rests on one fact: $1,\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt6$ are independent over $\mathbb Q$. Then every check is “multiply out, compare the four rational coordinates”. A set stops being a basis when one of its vectors becomes a scalar multiple of another, as $\sqrt3=\sqrt3\cdot1$ does over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)$.

You need

Lesson 6, section 4: vectors $v_1,\dots,v_n$ are linearly independent iff $\sum\lambda_iv_i=0$ forces all $\lambda_i=0$; equivalently, no $v_k$ lies in the span of the others (or of the earlier ones). Lesson 6, section 5: a basis is a linearly independent spanning set. Fields like $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)=\{a+b\sqrt2:a,b\in\mathbb Q\}$ (lesson 4, section 8). $\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt6$ are irrational.

Hint 1

Key lemma: if $q_0+q_1\sqrt2+q_2\sqrt3+q_3\sqrt6=0$ with $q_i\in\mathbb Q$, all $q_i=0$. First show $\sqrt3\notin\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ by squaring $\sqrt3=a+b\sqrt2$.

Hint 2

With the lemma: write $\alpha:=q_0+q_1\sqrt2$, $\beta:=q_2+q_3\sqrt2$; the sum is $\alpha+\beta\sqrt3$. If $\beta\ne0$, then $\sqrt3=-\alpha/\beta\in\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$.

Hint 3

For (b)–(d), multiply out a general combination, e.g. $(a+b\sqrt2)\cdot1+(c+d\sqrt2)\sqrt3=a+b\sqrt2+c\sqrt3+d\sqrt6$, and use the lemma for both spanning and independence. Use $\sqrt2\sqrt3=\sqrt6$, $\sqrt2\sqrt6=2\sqrt3$, $\sqrt3\sqrt6=3\sqrt2$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

The key lemma, part 1: $\sqrt3$ is not in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$. Square and use irrationality.

Lemma. If $q_0+q_1\sqrt2+q_2\sqrt3+q_3\sqrt6=0$ with $q_i\in\mathbb Q$, then all $q_i=0$. First, $\sqrt3\notin\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$: if $\sqrt3=a+b\sqrt2$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Q$, then $3=a^2+2b^2+2ab\sqrt2$. If $ab\ne0$, $\sqrt2=\frac{3-a^2-2b^2}{2ab}\in\mathbb Q$; if $b=0$, $\sqrt3=a\in\mathbb Q$; if $a=0$, $\sqrt6=\sqrt2\sqrt3=2b\in\mathbb Q$. All contradict irrationality.

Part 2: group the sum as $\alpha+\beta\sqrt3$ with $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$.

Let $\alpha:=q_0+q_1\sqrt2$, $\beta:=q_2+q_3\sqrt2$, so the sum is $\alpha+\beta\sqrt3=0$. If $\beta\ne0$, then $\sqrt3=-\alpha\beta^{-1}\in\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ (a field), impossible. So $\beta=0$, hence $\alpha=0$. From $q_2+q_3\sqrt2=0$: $q_3=0$ (else $\sqrt2\in\mathbb Q$), then $q_2=0$; likewise $q_0=q_1=0$.

(a) Over $\mathbb Q$: the four numbers span by definition of $V$ and are independent by the lemma.

(a) $B_0=\{1,\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt6\}$: every element of $V$ is a $\mathbb Q$-combination of these, and by the lemma they are independent over $\mathbb Q$.

(b) Over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$: multiply out a combination of $1,\sqrt3$; the four rational coordinates are free.

(b) $B_1=\{1,\sqrt3\}$. For $\lambda=a+b\sqrt2$, $\mu=c+d\sqrt2\in K_1$: $\lambda\cdot1+\mu\sqrt3=a+b\sqrt2+c\sqrt3+d\sqrt6$. Any $v=q_0+q_1\sqrt2+q_2\sqrt3+q_3\sqrt6$ is reached with $a,b,c,d=q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3$; and $\lambda\cdot1+\mu\sqrt3=0$ forces $a=b=c=d=0$ by the lemma, i.e. $\lambda=\mu=0$.

Not a basis over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)$: there $\sqrt3$ is a scalar.

Over $K_2=\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)$: $\sqrt3=\sqrt3\cdot1$, so $\sqrt3\cdot1+(-1)\cdot\sqrt3=0$ is a non-trivial relation, and $B_1$ is dependent in $V_2$.

(c) Over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt6)$: same method with $\sqrt2,\sqrt6$.

(c) $B_3=\{\sqrt2,\sqrt6\}$. For $\lambda=a+b\sqrt6$, $\mu=c+d\sqrt6$: $\lambda\sqrt2+\mu\sqrt6=6d+a\sqrt2+2b\sqrt3+c\sqrt6$. Any $v$ is reached with $a=q_1$, $b=\frac{q_2}2$, $c=q_3$, $d=\frac{q_0}6$; and $=0$ forces $6d=a=2b=c=0$. So $B_3$ is a basis of $V_3$.

(d) $\{\sqrt2,\sqrt3\}$ over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ and over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)$.

(d) $B_{2,3}=\{\sqrt2,\sqrt3\}$. Over $K_1$: $(a+b\sqrt2)\sqrt2+(c+d\sqrt2)\sqrt3=2b+a\sqrt2+c\sqrt3+d\sqrt6$. Over $K_2$: $(a+b\sqrt3)\sqrt2+(c+d\sqrt3)\sqrt3=3d+a\sqrt2+c\sqrt3+b\sqrt6$. In both cases all four rational coordinates can be hit, and $=0$ forces $a=b=c=d=0$ by the lemma, so $B_{2,3}$ is a basis of $V_1$ and of $V_2$.

Not over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt6)$: $\frac{\sqrt6}2$ is a scalar that turns $\sqrt2$ into $\sqrt3$.

Over $K_3$: $\frac12\sqrt6\in K_3$ and $\frac12\sqrt6\cdot\sqrt2=\frac12\cdot2\sqrt3=\sqrt3$, so $\sqrt3$ is a $K_3$-multiple of $\sqrt2$, and $B_{2,3}$ is dependent in $V_3$. ∎

Trap

Proving independence of $\{1,\sqrt3\}$ over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ by saying “$\sqrt3$ is irrational”. Over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ the scalars are not rational; you need that $\sqrt3$ is not in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$, which is the lemma. In general: always ask which field the scalars come from.
