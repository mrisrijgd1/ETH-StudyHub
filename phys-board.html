What Prof. Rupp teaches vs the script

Prof. Rupp has no script of her own yet; she roughly follows Ensslin's script. This page compares her lectures 1–17 (15 Sep to 7 Nov) with that script: what is the same, what she added, what she left out, where the letters differ, and what all that means for the exam.

The short answer. Almost everything she wrote on the board is in the script, and the script up to the start of rotating rigid bodies (up to §8.4, plus the start of §8.5) was almost all on the board. Three things change:

  1. The order. Her chapter 4 “Conservation laws” gathers energy, momentum, collisions, the rocket, angular momentum and Kepler's laws, which the script spreads over chapters 3–7.
  2. Some letters, for example $D$ instead of $k$ for the spring constant (table below).
  3. A few extras she added, and a few script parts she left out.

What you do: keep learning from the script and our Physics lessons (they already use her letters wherever the formula sheet does). Add the seven extras in “Learn these extras”. Skip what is in “Safe to skip”. Nothing else changes.

1 · Learn these extras (on the board, not or barely in the script)

These seven came up on the board and matter for her exam: either her practice exam (12 Nov) asks them, or old exams do. Each has a short explanation here, so you need no other source.

1. Units and dimension checks, every time

Her practice exam says it outright: write the result as a formula first, then put in numbers, and always give units; missing or wrong units cost points. She also gave three quick checks for any formula (lecture 1):

  • both sides of the equation have the same dimension;
  • every term of a sum has the same dimension;
  • whatever stands inside $e^{(\dots)}$, $\sin(\dots)$, $\ln(\dots)$ has no dimension.

The same idea, used backwards, finds a formula up to a constant (her “Koeffizientenvergleich”): write $T=c\,\ell^{\alpha}g^{\beta}$ and match the powers of m and s on both sides. Older exams ask exactly this (FS04, FS10, FS16, HS07).

And keep the precision of your data (lecture 10): $\Delta x=100\,\text m$ in $\Delta t=(3\pm1)\,\text s$ gives $v\approx3\cdot10^1\,\text{m/s}$, not $33.333\,\text{m/s}$. In our lessons: lesson 1, “Units and dimensions: the free check”.

2. Energy with a system boundary

Her way into energy (lectures 9–12): first draw the system, then decide whether energy crosses its boundary.

  • Closed system (only internal forces): the total energy stays the same, $\sum_i E_i(t_A)=\sum_i E_i(t_E)$; energy only changes its form.
  • Open system (external forces act across the boundary): $\sum_i\big(E_i(t_E)-E_i(t_A)\big)=W$, the work done by the external forces. $W>0$: the system gains energy.
  • With friction: $W_{NK}=\Delta T+\Delta U$, where $W_{NK}$ is the work of the non-conservative forces. With only conservative forces, $W_{NK}=0$ and $T+U$ stays constant.
Trap

Counting gravity twice (her “Achtung” in lecture 12). Either gravity is inside the system and appears as $\Delta U=mg\,\Delta h$, or it is outside and appears as work $\int\vec F_G\cdot d\vec r$. Never both.

In our lessons: lesson 5 and lesson 6.

3. Three kinds of collision, and 2-D collisions

She names three cases (lectures 11–13). The script sorts the same cases by the $Q$-value (§6.8.1, p. 148).

elasticbodies separate, no lossconservedconserved$Q=0$
fully inelasticbodies stick: $v_{1,E}=v_{2,E}$conservednot conserved (heat, deformation)$Q<0$
partially elastic (teilelastisch)bodies separate, some lossconservednot conserved$Q<0$
  • In every case the centre of mass keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed.
  • In 2-D and 3-D, momentum is conserved component by component. This is exactly her practice exam's task 2(d)–(e).
  • Equal masses, one at rest, elastic and not head-on: the two bodies leave at $90^\circ$ (square the momentum equation and compare with the energy equation).
  • Ballistic pendulum: fully inelastic hit (momentum), then a swing up (energy). Two steps, two different laws.

In our lessons: lesson 8.

4. The centre of mass does not care where a force acts

$M\ddot{\vec r}_s=\sum\vec F_{\text{ext}}$, whatever the points where the external forces act. Where the force acts only decides whether the body also starts to rotate (lecture 16: “if $\vec r_s$ is not on the line of action, the body rotates”). Her practice exam 1(c) tests exactly this rule with a push on a dumbbell (try it before reading on in lesson 7). In our lessons: lesson 7.

5. Escape velocity

To leave the Earth for good, the kinetic energy must pay for the whole potential difference to infinity (lecture 12): $\tfrac12mv^2=G\frac{mM_E}{r_E}$, so $v_{\text{escape}}=\sqrt{2GM_E/r_E}\approx11\,\text{km/s}$ (“2nd cosmic velocity”). For a circular orbit just above the ground, $v=\sqrt{g\,r_E}\approx8\,\text{km/s}$ (“1st cosmic velocity”). Old exams ask this (FS11, FS20). In our lessons: lesson 9.

6. Statics: a strategy, and sliding vs tipping

Her recipe from lecture 16:

  1. Equilibrium needs both $\sum\vec F_i=0$ and $\sum\vec M_i=0$.
  2. Choose the pivot so that as many torques as possible vanish: a mounted body about its mount, a free body about its centre of mass, otherwise where many lines of action cross.
  3. Two equal and opposite forces on parallel lines (a force couple) give a torque that is the same about every point.
  4. Always check both ways equilibrium can fail: sliding (friction reaches $\mu_H F_N$) and tipping (the resultant's line of action leaves the support area).
  5. Kinds of equilibrium: stable (a small push brings it back), labile (it moves away), indifferent (it rests in the new place).

The script has torque and equilibrium (§8.3–8.4, pp. 186–196) but not the word “tipping”; tipping appears in six old exams (FS12, FS17, FS18, FS25, HS16, HS25). In our lessons: lesson 10.

7. Constraint forces and buoyancy

Constraint forces (Zwangskräfte, lecture 17) such as the normal force, a rope force or the track force on a roller coaster have no formula of their own: they are exactly as large as needed so the body obeys the constraint (stays on the surface, the rope stays taut, the car stays on the track). You find them from Newton's second law, never the other way round.

Buoyancy (lectures 6 and 17): the fluid pushes up with the weight of the fluid the body displaces, $F_A=\rho_{\text{fluid}}V_{\text{submerged}}\,g$. A fully submerged body sinks, rises or floats depending only on $\rho_{\text{body}}$ vs $\rho_{\text{fluid}}$; a floating body sinks in until $\rho_{\text{fluid}}V_{\text{submerged}}=\rho_{\text{body}}V_{\text{body}}$. The script only has buoyancy as a Newton's-third-law example (§3.6.2, p. 47). In our lessons: lesson 3.

Read once, low exam weight: five more board extras
  • Mean velocity vs mean speed (lecture 2): $\langle\vec v\rangle=\Delta\vec r/\Delta t$ is a vector, $\langle v\rangle=\Delta s/\Delta t$ uses the path length, and $|\langle\vec v\rangle|\le\langle v\rangle$. After one full lap, $\langle\vec v\rangle=0$ but $\langle v\rangle>0$.
  • Tidal force (lectures 9–10): gravity is stronger on the near side of a body than on the far side. A first-order Taylor expansion gives a difference $\Delta F\approx2G\frac{m_1m_2}{r^3}\Delta r$, falling off like $1/r^3$, not $1/r^2$. Not in the script; old exams only tell you to neglect it.
  • Noether's theorem (lecture 9), in words: laws that do not change with time give energy conservation, laws that do not change from place to place give momentum conservation, and laws that do not change with direction give angular-momentum conservation.
  • Swing-by and Hohmann orbits (lectures 12–13): in Jupiter's frame the probe bounces off elastically like off a moving wall, so in the Sun's frame it can gain up to $2v_{\text{Jupiter}}$. A Hohmann orbit uses the Earth's own orbital speed to reach Mars cheaply.
  • Kepler's ellipse via $u=1/r$ (lectures 14–15): Newton's second law in polar coordinates plus $L=mr^2\dot\varphi=$ const turns into $\frac{d^2u}{d\varphi^2}+u=\frac{GMm^2}{L^2}$, an oscillator equation, so $\frac1r=\frac1p+\frac{\varepsilon}{p}\cos\varphi$ with $p=\frac{L^2}{GMm^2}$. The energy then decides the shape: $E=\frac{GmM}{2p}(\varepsilon^2-1)$, so $E<0$ ellipse, $E=0$ parabola, $E>0$ hyperbola. The script reaches the same results another way (§7.2–7.3). For the exam the results count, not the derivation.

2 · Script parts you still need, although she left them out

These were not on the board (or only on her slides), but old exams ask them, so keep them:

  • The Coriolis force on the rotating Earth (script §3.11.4.2–3.11.4.3, pp. 86–90): which way a falling or moving body is deflected, and why the swing plane of a pendulum turns (Foucault). Five old exams ask it (FS13, FS26, HS12, HS14, HS19). She derived the general Coriolis force but showed the Earth only on slides. Lesson 4.
  • The ball in the loop (script §4.5.2, p. 124): energy conservation plus the condition for staying on the track. The classic problem-2 type (loops, half-pipes). Lesson 6.
  • The curl test for conservative forces, $\nabla\times\vec F=0$ (script §4.1.7, pp. 105–107): she only said that the curl criterion is equivalent to $\oint\vec F\cdot d\vec r=0$ and $\vec F=-\nabla U$, but her practice exam 1(b) and HS23 both offer it as an answer option. Lesson 5.
  • The field of a homogeneous sphere (script §7.4.1–7.4.2, pp. 173–178): outside, a sphere pulls like a point mass at its centre; inside a homogeneous sphere, $g$ grows linearly with $r$. Know the results; the derivation is optional. Lesson 9.

3 · Safe to skip

In the script, not on the board, and not needed in any of the 36 old exams on the site (we searched them all):

  • Surface and volume elements, flux, divergence, Gauss's and Stokes's theorems (§4.1.2–4.1.3 and §4.1.5–4.1.8, pp. 93–108). Keep only the gradient and the curl test above.
  • The NaCl crystal potential (§4.4.2.3, p. 117).
  • The general three-particle system (§6.7, p. 148) and the energy and angle distributions of scattering (§6.8.4.4, pp. 157–160). A chain of collisions on a line (FS20) needs only the two-body rules, one collision at a time.
  • Kepler's equation and the eccentric anomaly (§7.2 from p. 167): replaced by the $u=1/r$ route; only the results count.
  • Galaxy rotation curves (§7.4.3, pp. 178–180).
  • For now also Euler angles and the inertia tensor (§8.5.1–8.5.4, pp. 197–203): she has not reached them; this page will say so if she does.

The same topics are on the site's skip list, so you can always find them again.

Two traps

$\Phi$ is a field, not a potential. She writes the gravitational field (a vector, in $\text{m/s}^2$) as $\vec\Phi_G$. In many books $\Phi$ means the potential (a scalar). In her notes, the potential energy is $U$.

Her $\nabla\cdot U$ means the gradient. She writes $\nabla\cdot U=-\vec F$ with a multiplication dot. $U$ is a scalar, so this can only be the gradient $\nabla U$. The divergence $\nabla\cdot\vec v$ only acts on vectors.

6 · Lecture by lecture

Open a lecture to see what matched the script, what she added or did differently, and what to take for the exam. Page numbers are the script's printed numbers.

Lecture 1 · 15 Sep · Units, dimensional analysis, position and path
Same as the script
Physical quantities and units (§1.2, p. 6); position vector, path, displacement (§2.1–2.3, pp. 11–13).
Added
The three dimension checks (extra 1) with the drag formula $F=\frac12c_w\rho Av^2$ as the example: $c_w$ comes out dimensionless. The length of a path as an integral, $\Delta s=\int ds$ with $ds=|d\vec r|$.
For the exam
Units in every answer.
Lecture 2 · 17 Sep · Velocity, acceleration, throws
Same as the script
Velocity and acceleration as vectors, constant velocity, constant acceleration, horizontal and oblique throw (§2.4–2.5.2, pp. 13–20).
Added
Mean velocity vs mean speed. The motion laws derived by integrating, with the integration variable written $\tilde t$. A long-jump example: oblique throw from a height of 70 cm.
For the exam
Her recipe for throws: a coordinate system with a sensible zero, split the motion into phases, write down the initial conditions. Her practice exam 2(a)–(c) is a throw.
Lecture 3 · 22 Sep · Circular motion and polar unit vectors
Same as the script
Uniform circular motion, angular velocity, angular acceleration (§2.5.3–2.5.5, pp. 20–25).
Done differently
She builds everything from the polar unit vectors $\hat r=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$, $\hat\varphi=(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi)$ and their derivatives $\dot{\hat r}=\dot\varphi\,\hat\varphi$, $\dot{\hat\varphi}=-\dot\varphi\,\hat r$. She reuses them for Kepler's laws in lecture 14, so they are worth knowing by heart.
For the exam
$\vec a=-R\dot\varphi^2\,\hat r+R\ddot\varphi\,\hat\varphi$: the central part is always there, the tangential part only when the speed changes.
Lecture 4 · 24 Sep · Newton's first and second law, inertial frames
Same as the script
Inertial frames, $\vec F=m\vec a$ and $\vec F=\dot{\vec p}$, adding forces (§3.1–3.5, pp. 31–46); the osculating circle for general curves (§2.5.6, p. 25).
Done differently
Order: the script defines mass and momentum first and states the second law before the first; she goes first, second, third law. She names the Galilei transformation $\vec r\,'=\vec r-\vec R(t)$ and the rule “primed = moving frame, capital letters = motion of the frames relative to each other”.
For the exam
Her free-body routine: sketch, draw the system boundary, draw only the forces that cross the boundary.
Lecture 5 · 29 Sep · Weight, springs, force components, friction
Same as the script
Inertial and gravitational mass (§3.1.2, p. 34); spring force, normal force, friction, fluid drag (§3.8–3.9, pp. 52–65).
Added
A box lifted in four phases (speed up, constant speed, slow down, rest), to show when the net force is zero. A list of force components she said to learn: normal force, downhill force $F_{\text{Hang}}=F_G\sin\alpha$, centripetal force $m\omega^2R$. The centripetal force is not a new force: it is the component pointing to the centre, supplied by a real force (gravity, a rope, the normal force).
Letters
$D$ for the spring constant, $\mu_H$ and $\mu_G$ for friction.
Lecture 6 · 1 Oct · Newton's third law, buoyancy, accelerated frames
Same as the script
Action and reaction (§3.6, p. 46); internal and external forces; the linearly accelerated frame and the inertial force (§3.11).
Added
Buoyancy with densities (extra 7). A sharp distinction: action and reaction act on different bodies, an equilibrium is about the forces on one body. Only external forces can accelerate the centre of mass.
For the exam
The take-off example: argue once in the plane's frame (an inertial force pushes you into the seat) and once in the inertial frame (the seat pushes you forward). Both must give the same physics.
Lecture 7 · 6 Oct · Weightlessness, rotating frames qualitatively
Same as the script
Apparent weight and weightlessness (§3.10.3.2, p. 71); angular velocity as a vector (§2.5.4, p. 22); the idea of centrifugal and Coriolis forces (§3.11).
Added
The parabolic flight: the plane flies exactly the parabola of a thrown body, so everyone inside floats. Rule: you are weightless when gravity is the only force on you (or no force at all).
Lecture 8 · 8 Oct · The rotating-frame law, gravitation
Same as the script
The full derivation $\vec a\,'=\vec a-\vec A_0-2\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'-\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r\,')$ with the centrifugal force $-m\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r\,')$ and the Coriolis force $-2m\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'$ (§3.11.1–3.11.3, pp. 76–81). The law of gravitation (§3.10.3, p. 69).
Added
The 1st cosmic velocity, 8 km/s. The field idea: force = property of the test body × field ($\vec F=q\vec E$, $\vec F=m\vec\Phi_G$).
Left out
Coriolis on the Earth's surface and the Foucault pendulum (§3.11.4.2–3): slides only. Keep the Earth case anyway (section 2).
For the exam
Her practice exam 1(d) (Coriolis vs centrifugal force) and 1(e) (how the force changes with distance).
Lecture 9 · 13 Oct · Tidal force, conservation laws, energy and work
Same as the script
Work as a line integral, power, the work of a spring, no work by a force perpendicular to the path (§4.2, pp. 109–112).
Added
The tidal force by Taylor expansion and Noether's theorem (both under “Read once”). Energy as a property of a system, closed vs open systems (extra 2).
Done differently
The script opens chapter 4 with 15 pages of vector analysis; she starts from forms of energy (pole vault) and brings in the maths only when needed.
Lecture 10 · 15 Oct · Kinetic energy, conservative forces, potential
Same as the script
$T=\frac12mv^2$ from the work integral, conservative forces, potential energy and $\vec F=-\nabla U$, the gravitational potential $-Gm_1m_2/r$ (§4.3–4.4, pp. 112–122).
Added
Significant figures. A short “toolbox”: partial derivatives, $\nabla$, gradient (points uphill), divergence (“sources?”), curl (“whirls?”), one line each.
Left out
Flux, Gauss's and Stokes's theorems (safe to skip).
Watch out
She writes $\nabla\cdot U$ for the gradient (trap).
Lecture 11 · 20 Oct · Mechanical energy, momentum, centre of mass, first collisions
Same as the script
$\oint\vec F\cdot d\vec r=0\Leftrightarrow\vec F=-\nabla U$, mechanical energy (§4.4–4.5); impulse $\Delta\vec p=\int\vec F\,dt$ (§3.4.2, p. 41); momentum conservation, centre of mass (§6.2, p. 135); the central elastic collision (§6.8.2, p. 149).
Added
$W_{NK}=\Delta T+\Delta U$ as the general energy law with friction. For a 1-D elastic collision she shows that the relative velocity just reverses, $v_{1,A}-v_{2,A}=-(v_{1,E}-v_{2,E})$, whatever the masses: a quick shortcut.
Lecture 12 · 22 Oct · Escape velocity, collision types, the centre-of-mass frame
Same as the script
Lab and centre-of-mass frame, $T=\frac12Mv_s^2+T'$ (§6.3, p. 138); collisions in the centre-of-mass frame (§6.8.3–6.8.4, pp. 150–156).
Added
“The formula sheet may be annotated by hand” (confirmed). Escape velocities (extra 5), Hohmann orbits. Partially elastic collisions as a named case, the ballistic pendulum, the 90° billiard rule (extra 3). In the centre-of-mass frame an elastic collision just reverses both velocities.
For the exam
Her practice exam 1(a) and 2(d)–(f).
Lecture 13 · 27 Oct · Swing-by, rocket, angular momentum
Same as the script
The rocket equation $\Delta v=v_{\text{rel}}\ln\frac{m_0}{m_1}$ (§3.7.3, p. 50; in the script it comes much earlier); torque $\vec M=\vec r\times\vec F$, angular momentum $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$, $\dot{\vec L}=\vec M$, conservation in central fields and the area law (ch. 5, pp. 129–133).
Added
The swing-by. The moment of inertia already here ($L_z=I\dot\varphi$, $T_{\text{rot}}=\frac12I\omega^2$) with a table that matches each linear quantity to its rotational partner.
For the exam
The rocket is in nine old exams. A single-stage rocket reaches at most about 9 km/s, less than the escape velocity: that is why rockets have stages.
Lecture 14 · 29 Oct · Angular momentum of systems, the two-body problem
Same as the script
Angular momentum of a system = that of the centre of mass + that about the centre of mass (§6.4.4, p. 141); internal torques cancel; reduced mass $\mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}$ (§6.6, pp. 144–147).
Done differently
The start of the orbit derivation with $u=1/r$ (see “Read once”). The script takes another route (§7.2, pp. 165–170).
Lecture 15 · 3 Nov · Kepler's laws, effective potential, start of rigid bodies
Same as the script
Kepler's three laws, the effective potential (§7.2–7.3, pp. 163–172); centre of mass as an integral (§8.2, p. 183); static equilibrium (§8.4, p. 194).
Added
The orbit type read off from the energy, $E=\frac{GmM}{2p}(\varepsilon^2-1)$. The leaning ladder as an example (exercise sheet 9). The announcement of the practice exam.
Left out
The field of extended bodies (§7.4); keep the result for a sphere (section 2).
Lecture 16 · 5 Nov · Statics, sliding vs tipping, motion of rigid bodies
Same as the script
The centre of mass of a cone, $z_s=h/4$ above the base, same calculation (§8.2.2, p. 184); lines of action, torques, equilibrium (§8.3–8.4, pp. 186–196); rotational energy (§8.5.5.2, p. 205, just started).
Added
Her statics strategy, sliding vs tipping, the support-area rule, kinds of equilibrium (extra 6). Motion of a rigid body = motion of the centre of mass + rotation about it (extra 4). Tidal locking as a side remark.
Lecture 17 · 7 Nov · Review week: overview and worked examples
Content
No new chapter. A map of the whole course (description → force laws → conservation laws), the difference between coordinate systems (Cartesian, polar, cylindrical, spherical) and reference frames (inertial, lab, accelerated, centre-of-mass), and her plan for the rest of the term (section 8).
Added
Constraint forces (extra 7); action and reaction when friction is involved. Two worked examples: a mass on a spring hanging from a rotating arm (the “spring carousel”: find the angle and $\omega$) and buoyancy puzzles (does the water level rise when a stone goes from a boat to the bottom? how deep does a floating cylinder sink?).
For the exam
Both examples are exam-sized; try them after lesson 4 and lesson 3.

What it tells us about her exam: no calculator; only the 12-page official formula sheet, on which your own handwritten notes are allowed; a translation dictionary is allowed. Single-choice questions have exactly one right box and need no reasoning. Everywhere else: short explanation, the way you got there, the formula before the numbers, and units. It is in German. It stops at collisions because that is where the class was; the real exam will cover the whole term.

8 · Still to come

Her plan from lecture 17 for the rest of the term, with where it sits in the script:

  • Rigid bodies, continued: translation and rotation, torque, angular momentum, moment of inertia (§8.5, pp. 197–220; lesson 11), and the gyroscope (Kreisel). In the script the gyroscope is only an extra appendix chapter (ch. 13), so it may matter more for her exam than for Ensslin's. We will add it once we see how far she goes.
