1. Warm-up (5 min)

Three quick questions. Answer each in your head, then open it.

  1. In the definition of continuity at $x_0$, who chooses first: $\varepsilon$ or $\delta$? And what may $\delta$ depend on?

    Answer

    $\varepsilon$ comes first (“for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$”). So $\delta$ may depend on $\varepsilon$ and on the point $x_0$. If one $\delta$ works for all points at once, the function is uniformly continuous.

  2. A continuous $f:(0,1)\to\mathbb R$ takes the values $-2$ and $3$. Must it take the value $0$?

    Answer

    Yes. $(0,1)$ is an interval, and the intermediate value theorem only needs an interval, not a closed one. $0$ lies between $-2$ and $3$.

  3. $f(x)=\frac1x$ on $(0,1]$. Which of the three promises (bounded, maximum attained, uniformly continuous) does it break, and which hypothesis is missing?

    Answer

    It is unbounded ($f(\frac1n)=n$), has no maximum, and is not uniformly continuous. The missing hypothesis: $(0,1]$ is not closed. The theorems need a compact interval $[a,b]$.

2. Recap card (10 min)

Read this once, slowly. Everything in bold must come out exactly in the exam.

Lesson 7: continuity and the intermediate value theorem

  • Continuity at $x_0$: $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x\in D:$ $|x-x_0|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$. Continuous means continuous at every point of $D$.
  • Not continuous at $x_0$: $\exists\varepsilon>0\ \forall\delta>0\ \exists x\in D:$ $|x-x_0|<\delta$ and $|f(x)-f(x_0)|\ge\varepsilon$. Recipe: name one $\varepsilon$ (half the jump), let $\delta>0$ be arbitrary, build an explicit $x$.
  • ε-δ by hand: compute $|f(x)-f(x_0)|$ first, factor, bound the extra factor by first promising $|x-x_0|<1$, then $\delta=\min(1,\dots)$.
  • The rules: sums, products, multiples, compositions and quotients (denominator $\neq0$) of continuous functions are continuous; polynomials are continuous. At a seam of a case-defined function, the two formulas must agree.
  • Intermediate value theorem: $I$ an interval, $f:I\to\mathbb R$ continuous, $a,b\in I$. Then for every $c$ between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ there is $x$ between $a$ and $b$ with $f(x)=c$. Existence only, not uniqueness; needs an interval and continuity on the whole stretch.
  • Pattern “an equation has a solution”: move everything to one side, check continuity, find two points with opposite signs, apply the theorem.

Lesson 8: what a closed bounded interval promises

  • Inverse function theorem: $I$ an interval, $f:I\to\mathbb R$ continuous and strictly monotone. Then $f(I)$ is an interval and $f^{-1}:f(I)\to I$ exists, is continuous and strictly monotone in the same direction. This gives the $n$-th roots.
  • Boundedness theorem: $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ continuous $\Rightarrow$ $f$ is bounded.
  • Extreme value theorem: $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ continuous $\Rightarrow$ $f$ attains a maximum and a minimum. In a “compute the maximum” question, always write this sentence first.
  • Uniformly continuous: $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x,y\in D:$ $|x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$. One $\delta$ for all points. Lipschitz ($|f(x)-f(y)|\le L|x-y|$) gives it with $\delta=\frac\varepsilon L$.
  • Heine's theorem: $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ continuous $\Rightarrow$ $f$ uniformly continuous.
  • The counterexamples the exam asks for: $\frac1x$ on $(0,1]$ or $(0,1)$ (not closed), $x$ or $x^2$ on $[0,\infty)$ or $\mathbb R$ (not bounded), a jump on $[0,1]$ (not continuous).

4. Write it on paper: three exam proofs (35 min)

For each proof: read the claim and the frame, close your eyes for ten seconds, then write the whole proof on paper without looking at the lesson. Only then open the fold and mark yourself with the grader's list.

All three proofs have the same skeleton, the supremum trick: define a set, show it is non-empty and bounded, take its supremum $s_0$, and use continuity at $s_0$ to get a contradiction. Once you see that, the three proofs are one idea used three times.

Proof 1: the intermediate value theorem (7–9 points)

Claim. Let $I$ be an interval, $f:I\to\mathbb R$ continuous and $a,b\in I$. Then for every $c$ between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ there is an $x$ between $a$ and $b$ with $f(x)=c$. (Lesson 7, Proof A.)

What the definitions force

An existence claim with no formula: the tool is the supremum. Start with “without loss of generality $a<b$ and $f(a)<c<f(b)$”, define $X=\{x\in[a,b]: f(x)\le c\}$ and $x_0=\sup X$. To show $f(x_0)=c$, rule out $f(x_0)<c$ and $f(x_0)>c$. In both cases use continuity with $\varepsilon$ = the gap $|f(x_0)-c|$.

The proof and the grader's list

Setup. Without loss of generality $a<b$ and $f(a)\le f(b)$ (otherwise swap the names of $a$ and $b$, or apply the result to $-f$ and $-c$; if $a=b$, then $c=f(a)$ and $x=a$ works). If $c=f(a)$ or $c=f(b)$, take $x=a$ or $x=b$. So assume $f(a)<c<f(b)$.

The set. Let $X=\{x\in[a,b]: f(x)\le c\}$. Then $a\in X$, so $X\neq\emptyset$, and $X$ is bounded above by $b$. By completeness $x_0=\sup X$ exists, and $a\le x_0\le b$.

Case $f(x_0)<c$. Then $x_0\neq b$ (as $f(b)>c$), so $x_0<b$. Continuity at $x_0$ with $\varepsilon=c-f(x_0)>0$ gives $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x\in[a,b]$ with $|x-x_0|<\delta$. Let $x=\min\left(b,x_0+\frac\delta2\right)$. Then $x>x_0$, $|x-x_0|<\delta$, and $f(x)<f(x_0)+\varepsilon=c$, so $x\in X$. This contradicts that $x_0$ is an upper bound of $X$.

Case $f(x_0)>c$. Then $x_0\neq a$, so $x_0>a$. Continuity at $x_0$ with $\varepsilon=f(x_0)-c>0$ gives $\delta>0$ as above. For every $x\in[a,b]$ with $x_0-\delta<x\le x_0$ we get $f(x)>f(x_0)-\varepsilon=c$, so $x\notin X$. Since every element of $X$ is $\le x_0$, every element of $X$ is $\le x_0-\delta$. So $x_0-\delta$ is an upper bound of $X$ smaller than $x_0$, contradicting that $x_0$ is the least upper bound.

By trichotomy $f(x_0)=c$, and $x_0\in[a,b]$. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • the “without loss of generality” sentence with its reason, and the cases $c=f(a)$, $c=f(b)$;
  • the set $X$, with “non-empty” and “bounded above” checked before taking the supremum;
  • in each case: $\varepsilon$ named as the gap, an explicit point, and which supremum property is contradicted (“upper bound” in the first case, “least” in the second);
  • the conclusion by trichotomy.

Proof 2: the extreme value theorem

Claim. Let $a<b$ and $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ continuous. Then $f$ attains a maximum and a minimum. You may use the boundedness theorem. (Lesson 8, Proof C.)

What the definitions force

The only candidate for the maximum is $S=\sup f([a,b])$, which exists because $f$ is bounded. So the goal is “some $x$ has $f(x)=S$”. With no formula for $x$, argue by contradiction: “Suppose $f(x)<S$ for all $x$.” The last line must be “…an upper bound smaller than $S$, contradiction.” The gap: a helper function, $\frac{1}{S-f}$.

The proof and the grader's list

By the boundedness theorem $f$ is bounded, so $S=\sup f([a,b])$ exists by completeness. Suppose, for contradiction, that $f(x)<S$ for all $x\in[a,b]$. Then $F(x)=\frac{1}{S-f(x)}$ is well-defined and positive on $[a,b]$, and continuous as a quotient of continuous functions whose denominator has no zero. By the boundedness theorem applied to $F$, there is $M>0$ with $\frac{1}{S-f(x)}\le M$ for all $x\in[a,b]$. Multiplying by the positive number $\frac{S-f(x)}{M}$ gives $\frac1M\le S-f(x)$, that is, $f(x)\le S-\frac1M$ for all $x$. So $S-\frac1M$ is an upper bound of $f([a,b])$ smaller than $S$, contradicting that $S$ is the least upper bound. Hence there is $x_{\max}\in[a,b]$ with $f(x_{\max})=S$, and $f(x)\le f(x_{\max})$ for all $x$. Applying this to $-f$ gives a minimum. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • why $S$ exists (boundedness theorem and completeness);
  • why $F$ is well-defined and continuous (the denominator is never $0$ by the assumption);
  • using the boundedness theorem a second time, on $F$;
  • the smaller upper bound $S-\frac1M$, the contradiction, and one sentence for the minimum.

Proof 3: Heine's theorem (7 points, Figalli FS26 B1)

Claim. Let $a<b$ and $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ continuous. Then $f$ is uniformly continuous. (Lesson 8, Proof E.)

What the definitions force

First line “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” No formula for $\delta$, so the supremum trick: let $X$ be the set of $t\in[a,b]$ for which one $\delta$ works on $[a,t]$; the goal becomes $b\in X$. Then the six beats: (1) $X$ and $s_0=\sup X$; (2) $[a,s_0)\subseteq X$; (3) continuity at $s_0$ with $\frac\varepsilon2$ gives $\delta_1$; (4) $t_0=\max(a,s_0-\frac{\delta_1}2)\in X$ gives $\delta_0$; (5) glue with $t_1=\min(b,s_0+\frac{\delta_1}2)$ and $\delta=\min(\delta_0,\frac{\delta_1}2)$, two cases; (6) $t_1\le s_0$ forces $t_1=b$.

The proof and the grader's list

Let $\varepsilon>0$ and let $X$ be the set of $t\in[a,b]$ for which there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x_1,x_2\in[a,t]$ with $|x_1-x_2|<\delta$.

$a\in X$ (for $x_1,x_2\in[a,a]$ the difference is $0$), and $X\subseteq[a,b]$, so $s_0=\sup X\in[a,b]$ exists by completeness. If $t\in X$ and $a\le t'\le t$, the same $\delta$ works for $t'$, so $t'\in X$. As every $t'<s_0$ lies below some element of $X$, we get $[a,s_0)\subseteq X$. (1)

By continuity of $f$ at $s_0$ there is $\delta_1>0$ with $|f(x)-f(s_0)|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $x\in[a,b]$ with $|x-s_0|<\delta_1$. For two such points the triangle inequality gives $$\begin{aligned}|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|&\le|f(x_1)-f(s_0)|\\&\quad+|f(s_0)-f(x_2)|\\&<\tfrac\varepsilon2+\tfrac\varepsilon2=\varepsilon.\qquad(2)\end{aligned}$$

By (1), $t_0=\max\left(a,s_0-\frac{\delta_1}2\right)\in X$ (either $t_0=a$, or $a\le t_0<s_0$), so there is $\delta_0>0$ that works on $[a,t_0]$. (3)

Let $t_1=\min\left(b,s_0+\frac{\delta_1}2\right)$ and $\delta=\min\left(\delta_0,\frac{\delta_1}2\right)$, and let $x_1,x_2\in[a,t_1]$ with $|x_1-x_2|<\delta$.

  • Case A: one of them, without loss of generality $x_1$, has $|x_1-s_0|\le\frac{\delta_1}2$. Then $|x_2-s_0|\le|x_2-x_1|+|x_1-s_0|$, which is $<\delta+\frac{\delta_1}2\le\delta_1$. Both points are within $\delta_1$ of $s_0$, so (2) gives $|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\varepsilon$.
  • Case B: $|x_1-s_0|>\frac{\delta_1}2$ and $|x_2-s_0|>\frac{\delta_1}2$. As $x_j\le t_1\le s_0+\frac{\delta_1}2$, this means $x_j<s_0-\frac{\delta_1}2\le t_0$. So $x_1,x_2\in[a,t_0]$ with $|x_1-x_2|<\delta\le\delta_0$, and (3) gives $|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\varepsilon$.

So $t_1\in X$, hence $t_1\le s_0$. Since $s_0+\frac{\delta_1}2>s_0$, the minimum must be $b$: $t_1=b\in X$. So there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x_1,x_2\in[a,b]$ with $|x_1-x_2|<\delta$. As $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $f$ is uniformly continuous. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • $\varepsilon$ fixed first, and the set $X$ written precisely (with “there is $\delta$” inside);
  • $s_0$ exists, and $[a,s_0)\subseteq X$ with its reason (a $\delta$ for a long stretch works for a shorter one);
  • continuity at $s_0$ with $\frac\varepsilon2$, and why two points in the window differ by less than $\varepsilon$;
  • the glued $\delta=\min(\delta_0,\frac{\delta_1}2)$ with both cases checked;
  • the ending: $t_1\le s_0$ forces $t_1=b$.

5. Spot the mistake (5 min)

A student hands in this “proof”. The claim is false (lesson 8 proves that $x^2$ is not uniformly continuous on $\mathbb R$), so the proof must break somewhere. Where exactly?

“Claim.” $f(x)=x^2$ is uniformly continuous on $\mathbb R$.

“Proof.” Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $x,y\in\mathbb R$. Choose $\delta=\min\left(1,\frac{\varepsilon}{2|y|+1}\right)$. If $|x-y|<\delta$, then $|x|<|y|+1$, so $|x+y|<2|y|+1$ and $$\begin{aligned}|x^2-y^2|&=|x-y|\,|x+y|\\&<\frac{\varepsilon}{2|y|+1}\cdot(2|y|+1)=\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$ So $f$ is uniformly continuous. ∎

The mistake

The order of the quantifiers. Uniform continuity says “$\forall\varepsilon\ \exists\delta\ \forall x,y$”: the $\delta$ must be chosen before the points. Here $\delta$ depends on $y$, because the points were named first. What the computation actually proves is plain continuity at every point $y$ (exactly lesson 7's proof for $x^2$). As $|y|$ grows, this $\delta$ shrinks to $0$, and no single $\delta$ works.

Exam lesson: in a uniform continuity proof, write “Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $\delta=\dots$” and only then “Let $x,y\in D$ with $|x-y|<\delta$”. If your $\delta$ contains $x$ or $y$, it isn't uniform.
