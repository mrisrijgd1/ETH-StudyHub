Flashcards

Definitions, theorems and proof moves, lesson by lesson. Say the answer out loud or write it down before you flip the card: definitions and theorem statements must come out exactly.

Choose the lessons and the kinds of card, then press Start. The cards come in random order. “Again” puts a card back a few places later; “Got it” removes it from this round.

Recipe cards are added with the calculation units of the refresher. New definition and theorem cards arrive with each refresher unit.

Negate $\forall x\in X:\ A(x)$ and $\exists x\in X:\ A(x)$. What stays the same?

$\neg(\forall x\in X:\ A(x))\iff\exists x\in X:\ \neg A(x)$ and $\neg(\exists x\in X:\ A(x))\iff\forall x\in X:\ \neg A(x)$.

The order of the quantifiers and their ranges stay the same ($\forall\varepsilon>0$ becomes $\exists\varepsilon>0$). Only the type of each quantifier and the final condition change.

Pointwise versus uniform existence: which of $\forall x\,\exists y$ and $\exists y\,\forall x$ is stronger, and why?

$\exists y\,\forall x:\ A(x,y)$ (uniform: one $y$ for all $x$) implies $\forall x\,\exists y:\ A(x,y)$ (pointwise: $y$ may depend on $x$), not conversely. Example on $\mathbb N$: $\forall x\,\exists y:\ x<y$ is true, $\exists y\,\forall x:\ x<y$ is false.

Is “$\forall x\in\emptyset:\ A(x)$” true or false? And “$\exists x\in\emptyset:\ A(x)$”?

“$\forall x\in\emptyset:\ A(x)$” is always true (vacuously true), whatever $A$ says. “$\exists x\in\emptyset:\ A(x)$” is always false.

Definition: $f:X\to Y$ is injective.

$\forall x_1,x_2\in X:\ f(x_1)=f(x_2)\Rightarrow x_1=x_2$.

Definition: $f:X\to Y$ is surjective. And bijective?

Surjective: $\forall y\in Y\ \exists x\in X:\ f(x)=y$. Bijective: injective and surjective. Then the inverse map $f^{-1}:Y\to X$ exists, with $f^{-1}(f(x))=x$ and $f(f^{-1}(y))=y$.

Definitions: the image $f(A)$ and the preimage $f^{-1}(B)$, for $f:X\to Y$, $A\subseteq X$, $B\subseteq Y$.

$f(A)=\{f(x)\mid x\in A\}\subseteq Y$ and $f^{-1}(B)=\{x\in X\mid f(x)\in B\}\subseteq X$. The preimage exists for every $f$: the symbol does not mean that $f$ is invertible.

Which set operations do the preimage and the image respect?

Preimage: all of them. $f^{-1}(B_1\cup B_2)=f^{-1}(B_1)\cup f^{-1}(B_2)$, the same with $\cap$, and $f^{-1}(Y\setminus B)=X\setminus f^{-1}(B)$.

Image: only $\cup$. In general only $f(A_1\cap A_2)\subseteq f(A_1)\cap f(A_2)$; equality holds if $f$ is injective.

Composition of $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to Z$: what follows forwards, and what follows backwards from $g\circ f$?

Forwards: $f,g$ injective (surjective, bijective) $\Rightarrow$ $g\circ f$ injective (surjective, bijective).

Backwards: $g\circ f$ injective $\Rightarrow$ $f$ injective (the first map). $g\circ f$ surjective $\Rightarrow$ $g$ surjective (the last map). Nothing more: $g$ resp. $f$ need not be.

Definition: a relation $\sim$ on $X$ is an equivalence relation.

Reflexive: $\forall x\in X:\ x\sim x$. Symmetric: $\forall x,y\in X:\ x\sim y\Rightarrow y\sim x$. Transitive: $\forall x,y,z\in X:\ (x\sim y\wedge y\sim z)\Rightarrow x\sim z$.

Definitions: the equivalence class $[x]$, the quotient $X/\!\sim$, and a partition of $X$.

$[x]=\{y\in X\mid y\sim x\}$. $X/\!\sim\ =\{[x]\mid x\in X\}$, the set of all classes. A partition of $X$ is a set of non-empty, pairwise disjoint subsets of $X$ whose union is $X$.

Let $\sim$ be an equivalence relation and $x,y\in X$. Which three statements are equivalent, and what follows for the classes?

$x\sim y\iff[x]=[y]\iff[x]\cap[y]\neq\emptyset$. So two classes are equal or disjoint, and the classes form a partition of $X$.

Definitions: $X$ has $n$ elements; $X$ is countable (countably infinite); $X$ is uncountable.

$X$ has $n$ elements if there is a bijection $\{1,\dots,n\}\to X$. $X$ is countable if there is a bijection $\mathbb N\to X$. $X$ is uncountable if it is infinite and not countable.

The pigeonhole principle.

If $X$ and $Y$ are finite with $|X|>|Y|$, there is no injective map $X\to Y$: two elements of $X$ land on the same element of $Y$.

Which of $\mathbb Z$, $\mathbb N\times\mathbb N$, $\mathbb Q$, $\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ are countable? What about subsets of a countable set?

$\mathbb Z$, $\mathbb N\times\mathbb N$ and $\mathbb Q$ are countable. $\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ (all infinite A/B-sequences) is uncountable, by the diagonal argument. A subset of a countable set is finite or countable.

Move: The negation recipe. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must show something is not continuous or not uniformly continuous. Sentences: “Let $\varepsilon=\dots$ (you choose). Let $\delta>0$ be arbitrary. Take $x=\dots$, $y=\dots$ (depending on $\delta$). Then $|x-y|<\delta$ but $|f(x)-f(y)|\ge\varepsilon$.”

Move: The injectivity template. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must show a map $f$ is injective. Sentences: “Let $x_1,x_2$ with $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$. … Hence $x_1=x_2$.” Start from equal outputs, end at equal inputs.

Move: The surjectivity template. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must show a map $f:X\to Y$ is surjective. Sentences: “Let $y\in Y$. Put $x=\dots$ (found on scrap paper by solving $f(x)=y$). Then $x\in X$ and $f(x)=\dots=y$.”

Move: A small counterexample. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: a statement “for all …” looks false. One concrete example kills it. Sentences: “The statement is false: take $X=\{1,2\}$, $f=\dots$. Then … but not ….” Use the smallest sets and simplest maps that work.

Move: The three-bullet check. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must show a relation $\sim$ is an equivalence relation. Sentences: “Reflexive: for every $x$, $x\sim x$ because …. Symmetric: let $x\sim y$; then … so $y\sim x$. Transitive: let $x\sim y$ and $y\sim z$; then … so $x\sim z$.”

Move: Prove a circle of implications. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: several statements (i), (ii), (iii) are claimed to be equivalent. Sentences: “We show (i) ⇒ (ii) ⇒ (iii) ⇒ (i).” Three arrows instead of six.

Move: Set equality by two inclusions. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must show $A=B$ for sets. Sentences: “⊆: let $x\in A$. … so $x\in B$. ⊇: let $x\in B$. … so $x\in A$.”

Move: Assume the opposite. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: the claim is a “no such thing exists” or “cannot be” statement, so there is nothing to build directly. Sentences: “Suppose, for contradiction, that …. Then …, which contradicts …. Hence ….” Negate carefully (the negation recipe).

The card in the moves library

Move: The diagonal argument. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must show that no list (no map $\mathbb N\to X$) reaches everything. Sentences: “Let $x_1,x_2,\dots$ be any list. Build $y$ that differs from $x_n$ in the $n$-th place, for every $n$. Then $y\ne x_n$ for all $n$, so $y$ is not on the list.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Go through the back gate (contrapositive). When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: “$A$ ⇒ $B$” is hard but “not $B$ ⇒ not $A$” is easy, often because “not $B$” gives you a formula to work with. Sentences: “We prove the contrapositive. Suppose $n$ is odd, so $n=2k+1$. Then … so $n^2$ is odd.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Induction with labelled parts. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: a statement $P(n)$ for every $n\ge n_0$. Sentences: “Base case $n=n_0$: …. Induction step: assume $P(n)$ for some $n\ge n_0$ (induction hypothesis). Then … (by the induction hypothesis) … which is $P(n+1)$.” Point to the exact line where the hypothesis is used.

The card in the moves library

Move: Drop a non-negative term. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you need a lower bound and the expression has an extra term that is $\ge0$. Sentences: “$1+(n+1)x+nx^2\ge1+(n+1)x$, since $nx^2\ge0$.” A cousin of reduce to “≥ 0”.

The card in the moves library

Move: Minimum of two δs. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: two different conditions each give you a $\delta$, and you need both at once. Sentences: “Let $\delta=\min(\delta_1,\delta_2)>0$. Then both … hold.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Reduce to one case (oBdA). When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: two cases are mirror images. Sentences: “Without loss of generality $f$ is increasing; otherwise replace $f$ by $-f$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Choose n by Archimedes. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you have a bound like $\frac Cn$ and need it below $\varepsilon$. Sentences: “By the Archimedean principle choose $n\in\mathbb N$ with $n>\frac C\varepsilon$. Then $\frac Cn<\varepsilon$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Insert a middle point. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you need to bound a distance $d(x,z)$ but only know things about $d(x,y)$ and $d(y,z)$. Sentences: “By the triangle inequality, $d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)<\dots$.” For numbers: $|a-c|\le|a-b|+|b-c|$; the reverse form is $\bigl||a|-|b|\bigr|\le|a-b|$.

The card in the moves library

Move: Unfold an absolute value. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: a statement about $|x|$ you can't see directly. Sentences: “$|x|\le c$ ⇔ $-c\le x\le c$.” Or split into cases: “If $x\ge0$, $|x|=x$; if $x<0$, $|x|=-x$.” Then add the two-sided inequalities.

The card in the moves library

Move: The supremum trick. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you need something to hold all the way to $b$, but there's no formula. Sentences: “Let $X=\{t\in[a,b]: \text{it holds on }[a,t]\}$. Then $a\in X$ and $X$ is bounded by $b$, so $s_0=\sup X$ exists. By continuity at $s_0$ … so some $t_1>s_0$ lies in $X$ unless $s_0=b$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Continuity with a chosen ε. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you know $f$ is continuous at a point and need a neighbourhood where $f$ is under control. Choose $\varepsilon$ as the room you have. Sentences: “By continuity at $x_0$ with $\varepsilon=\dots$, there is $\delta>0$ such that $|x-x_0|<\delta$ implies $|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: ε/2 twice. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you need two quantities within $\varepsilon$ of each other, and each is close to a third. Sentences: “$|u-w|\le|u-v|+|v-w|<\frac\varepsilon2+\frac\varepsilon2=\varepsilon$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: sup ≤ an upper bound. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you want $\sup A\le c$ (or $c\le\inf B$). Sentences: “Every element of $A$ is $\le c$, so $c$ is an upper bound of $A$, hence $\sup A\le c$.” And the other way round: “$x\in A$, so $x\le\sup A$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Approach a supremum. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you need an element of $A$ almost as big as $\sup A$. Sentences: “Since $\sup A-\eta<\sup A$, it is not an upper bound of $A$, so there is $x\in A$ with $x>\sup A-\eta$.” The twin of “contradict least”.

The card in the moves library

Move: Two armies for completeness. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you need a real number to exist (a supremum, a common point) and only have the completeness axiom. Sentences: “Let $A$ be the set …, $B$ the set of its upper bounds. Both are non-empty and $a\le b$ for all $a\in A$, $b\in B$. By completeness there is $c$ with $a\le c\le b$ for all of them.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Contradict “least”. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you assumed something about a supremum $S$ and want a contradiction. Sentences: “So $S-\eta$ is an upper bound smaller than $S$, contradicting that $S$ is the least upper bound.”

The card in the moves library

Move: A helper function. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: “$f$ gets arbitrarily close to $S$” needs to become something a theorem can catch. Build a new function, like $\frac1{S-f}$, that turns “close” into “large”. Sentences: “Then $F(x)=\dots$ is well-defined and continuous, so by … it is bounded.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Trap between two values. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: a function is monotone and you need to control it. Sentences: “Since $y_-<y<y_+$ and $g$ is increasing, $g(y_-)<g(y)<g(y_+)$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: The ε-criterion (squeeze). When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must show a function is Riemann integrable. Sentences: “Let $\varepsilon>0$. Let $u,o$ be the step functions … Then $u\le f\le o$ and $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx=\dots<\varepsilon$. By the ε-criterion, $f$ is Riemann integrable.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Smaller than every ε. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you want two numbers to be equal. Sentences: “$0\le d<\varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon>0$, so $d=0$.” (If $d>0$, take $\varepsilon=d$.)

The card in the moves library

Move: Telescoping sum. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: a sum of differences of consecutive terms. Sentences: “$\sum_{k=1}^n(a_k-a_{k-1})=a_n-a_0$, since the middle terms cancel.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Density in every piece. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: a function behaves differently on $\mathbb Q$ and on the irrationals. Sentences: “Every interval $(x_{k-1},x_k)$ contains a rational and an irrational number, so …”

The card in the moves library

Move: Reduce to “≥ 0”. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you need $A\le B$. Sentences: “$B-A=\dots\ge0$.” For integrals: “by linearity, $\int g-\int f=\int(g-f)$, and this is $\ge0$ since $g-f\ge0$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Take the larger N. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: two (or finitely many) “from some index on” statements must hold at the same time. Sentences: “There are $N_1,N_2$ with … for $n\ge N_1$ and … for $n\ge N_2$. Let $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$. Then for $n\ge N$ both hold.” It is the sequence version of the minimum of two δs.

The card in the moves library

Move: δ = 1/n builds a sequence. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you have “for every δ > 0 there is a point with …” and want a sequence. Sentences: “For each $n\in\mathbb N$ apply this with $\delta=\frac1n$ to get $x_n$ with $|x_n-x_0|<\frac1n$ and …. Then $x_n\to x_0$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Sandwich. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you can't compute $b_n$ directly but can trap it. Sentences: “For all $n$: $a_n\le b_n\le c_n$, and $a_n\to A$, $c_n\to A$. By the sandwich lemma, $b_n\to A$.” Common form: “$|b_n|\le c_n\to0$, so $b_n\to0$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Bolzano–Weierstrass for a candidate. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must show something converges, have no candidate limit, but know the sequence is bounded. Sentences: “$(a_n)_n$ is bounded, so by Bolzano–Weierstrass it has a convergent subsequence $a_{n_k}\to L$. We show that $a_n\to L$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Estimate piece by piece. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you compare an integral over $[a,b]$ with something built from a partition. Sentences: “On each piece the error is at most $\eta\,(x_k-x_{k-1})$. Summing over $k$, the total error is at most $\eta\sum_k(x_k-x_{k-1})=\eta\,(b-a)$.” Choose $\eta=\frac\varepsilon{b-a}$ (or half of it) in advance.

The card in the moves library

Move: Go to partial sums. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: any question about a series: its value, its convergence, its terms. A series is the sequence of its partial sums. Sentences: “Let $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^na_k$. Then … so $(s_n)_n$ converges, i.e. the series converges.” Terms: $a_n=s_n-s_{n-1}$.

The card in the moves library

Move: Group into blocks. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: terms decrease, but you need a lower or upper bound for a long sum. Cut it into blocks of length $1,2,4,8,\dots$ and bound each block by (number of terms) × (smallest or largest term). Sentences: “The block $\sum_{k=2^{j-1}+1}^{2^j}a_k$ has $2^{j-1}$ terms, each $\ge a_{2^j}$, so it is $\ge2^{j-1}a_{2^j}$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Monotone and bounded. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must show convergence, and the sequence only goes up (or only down). Then boundedness is all you need, and no candidate limit. Sentences: “$(s_n)_n$ is increasing (since $a_{n+1}\ge0$) and bounded above by $B$, so it converges by monotone convergence.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Even and odd separately. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: a sequence alternates, so it isn't monotone, but every second term is. Sentences: “$(s_{2n})_n$ is increasing and $(s_{2n-1})_n$ is decreasing, both converge, and their difference tends to $0$. With $N=2\max\{N_1,N_2\}$, the whole sequence converges.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Find a majorant (or minorant). When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must decide whether a series converges. Bound its terms by a series you know: geometric or $\sum\frac1{n^p}$. Sentences: “For $n\ge N$: $|a_n|\le b_n$, and $\sum b_n$ converges, so $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely.” Or: “$a_n\ge b_n\ge0$ and $\sum b_n$ diverges, so $\sum a_n$ diverges.” Dominant powers tell you which $b_n$ to try.

The card in the moves library

Move: Cauchy instead of a candidate. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must show convergence but cannot guess the limit, and nothing is monotone. Sentences: “Let $\varepsilon>0$. There is $N$ with $\left|\sum_{k=m}^na_k\right|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge m\ge N$, so by the Cauchy criterion the series converges.”

The card in the moves library

Move: ε/3 three times. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must compare $f(x)$ and $f(x_0)$, but only know a nicer $g$ that is close to $f$ everywhere. Sentences:

$$|f(x)-f(x_0)|\le|f(x)-g(x)|+|g(x)-g(x_0)|+|g(x_0)-f(x_0)|<\tfrac\varepsilon3+\tfrac\varepsilon3+\tfrac\varepsilon3=\varepsilon.$$

Fix $g$ first (one $N$), then find $\delta$ from $g$.

The card in the moves library

Move: Bound independently of x. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must show uniform convergence. Find a bound $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le r_n$ in which $x$ doesn't appear, with $r_n\to0$. Sentences: “For all $x\in X$: $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le r_n$, so $\sup_x|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le r_n\to0$.” To disprove it, find a bad point $x_n$ with $|f_n(x_n)-f(x_n)|\ge c>0$.

The card in the moves library

Move: Finitely many points, then fill the gaps. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you know something at every single point (pointwise), want it everywhere at once (uniformly), and have a Lipschitz or continuity bound. Sentences: “Choose grid points $x_0,\dots,x_M$ with spacing $<\delta$. For these finitely many points take $N=\max_iN_i$. Every $x$ is within $\delta$ of some $x_i$, and the bound bridges the gap.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Multiply with the Cauchy product. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you need the product of two series, or of two power series. Check absolute convergence first, then collect the products by total index. Sentences: “Both series converge absolutely, so by the Cauchy product $\left(\sum a_n\right)\left(\sum b_n\right)=\sum_n\sum_{k=0}^na_kb_{n-k}$.” Then simplify the inner sum (often with the binomial theorem).

The card in the moves library

Move: Add and subtract two versions. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you have one equation for $X+iY$ (or $A+B$) and can get a second one for $X-iY$ (by replacing $z$ with $-z$, or by conjugating). Sentences: “Adding the two equations and dividing by $2$ gives $X$; subtracting and dividing by $2i$ gives $Y$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Multiply and divide by x − a. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you know something about a difference quotient $\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$ and need something about $f(x)-f(a)$ itself (or the other way round). Sentences: “For $x\neq a$, $f(x)-f(a)=\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}\cdot(x-a)$, and as $x\to a$ the first factor tends to $f'(a)$ and the second to $0$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Write f as line plus error. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: a proof about derivatives would divide by something that might be $0$. Replace the fraction by an equation with no fractions. Sentences: “Define $\varepsilon(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}-f'(a)$ for $x\neq a$ and $\varepsilon(a)=0$. Then $\varepsilon$ is continuous at $a$ and $f(x)=f(a)+(f'(a)+\varepsilon(x))(x-a)$ for all $x$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Flip the difference quotient. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you need the derivative of an inverse function. With $y=f(x)$: “$\frac{f^{-1}(y)-f^{-1}(y_0)}{y-y_0}=\left(\frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0}\right)^{-1}$.” Check first that neither side divides by $0$ (injectivity).

The card in the moves library

Move: Read a sign from each side. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you know where $f(x)$ lies compared with $f(x_0)$ near $x_0$ (a maximum, a minimum) and want information about $f'(x_0)$, or the other way round. Sentences: “For $x>x_0$ close to $x_0$ the quotient $\frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0}$ is $\le0$, so $f'(x_0)\le0$; for $x<x_0$ it is $\ge0$, so $f'(x_0)\ge0$.” Limits keep $\le$, not $<$.

The card in the moves library

Move: Split into cases. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: the objects you get from a theorem can sit in different places, and each place needs a different argument. Sentences: “Case 1: …. Then …. Case 2: …. Then ….” Make sure the cases cover everything (here: inside the interval, or both at the endpoints).

The card in the moves library

Move: Subtract the secant. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: a theorem needs $f(a)=f(b)$ (like Rolle) but your $f$ doesn't have it. Subtract the straight line through $(a,f(a))$ and $(b,f(b))$. Sentences: “Let $F(x)=f(x)-\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a)$. Then $F(a)=F(b)=f(a)$ and $F'=f'-\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$.”

The card in the moves library

Move: Mean value theorem between two points. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you know something about $f'$ (a sign, a bound) and want something about $f(x)-f(y)$. Sentences: “Let $y<x$ in $I$. Since $I$ is an interval, $[y,x]\subseteq I$, and $f$ is continuous on $[y,x]$ and differentiable on $(y,x)$. By the mean value theorem, $f(x)-f(y)=f'(\xi)(x-y)$ for some $\xi\in(y,x)$.” Then use the sign or the bound of $f'(\xi)$.

The card in the moves library

Move: Compare three slopes. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: a proof about a convex function, with or without derivatives. Take three points $x_0<x<x_1$. Sentences: “Since $f$ is convex, $\frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0}\le\frac{f(x_1)-f(x_0)}{x_1-x_0}\le\frac{f(x_1)-f(x)}{x_1-x}$.” Then let one point move (to a tangent, or to $\pm\infty$).

The card in the moves library

Move: Merge two weights into one. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: an induction over weighted averages (Jensen). Sentences: “Put $s=t_n+t_{n+1}$ and $y=\frac{t_n}sx_n+\frac{t_{n+1}}sx_{n+1}$. Then $\sum_{k\le n+1}t_kx_k=\sum_{k\le n-1}t_kx_k+sy$ is an average of $n$ points.” Handle $s=0$ separately.

Move: Write a number as an average. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: you must compare an integral (or an average) with one fixed value $c$. Sentences: “Since $c=\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^xc\,dt$, we get $\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^xf(t)\,dt-c=\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^x\bigl(f(t)-c\bigr)\,dt$.” Now both sit under one integral and the triangle inequality applies.

Move: Name the constant, then plug in a point. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: two functions have the same derivative on an interval, and you want an exact formula. Sentences: “Since $(F-G)'=0$ on the interval, $F=G+C$ for a constant $C$. Setting $x=a$ gives $C=F(a)-G(a)$.”

Move: Check by differentiating. When do you use it, and what do you write?

When: any antiderivative you have just computed, and when proving an integration rule. Sentences: “Indeed, $\frac d{dx}\bigl(\dots\bigr)=\dots=f(x)$.” Twenty seconds, and it catches almost every sign error and missing factor.
