Serie 2, explained

Calculus warm-up, the frog's best jumping angle, the shot at the falling plate, and motion on a spiral. For every task: the idea in plain words, a sketch, up to three hints that each give away a little more, then the solution with every step explained next to what you write, and the trap people fall into.

How to use this page.

  1. Read the task and try it on paper for 10 minutes. Draw a sketch first: in physics it is half the work.
  2. Stuck on where to start? Read The idea and open You need.
  3. Still stuck? Open Hint 1, try again, then Hint 2, then Hint 3. Hint 3 is almost the whole solution.
  4. Only then open the solution. The left column says why each step is there, the right column is what you write.

The solutions follow the official Lösung (Serie 2, Physik I HS26). The task texts are short English summaries: keep the official sheet next to you for the exact wording. With the StudyHub connector, your own AI can check your attempt before you look.

Aufgabe 1 · Differentiation and integration

Let $n\neq-1$ be an integer and $a$ a constant.

  1. Differentiate: $ax^n$, $\ \frac1x$, $\ x^x$ ($x>0$), $\ \sin(ax)$, $\ \ln|x|$.
  2. Find antiderivatives: $\int ax^n\,dx$, $\ \int\frac1x\,dx$, $\ \int e^{-x/\tau}\,dx$, $\ \int\cos(ax)\,dx$, $\ \int\ln x\,dx$.
  3. For $s(t)=s_0+v_0t+\frac12a_0t^2$ (constants $s_0,v_0,a_0$), find $\dot s$ and $\ddot s$. What do you get when you integrate them again?
The idea

This is the toolkit for the whole course: velocity is the derivative of position, and position is the integral of velocity. Most items are standard rules. Two need a trick: $x^x$ (write it as $e^{x\ln x}$, because you can only differentiate a power with a fixed exponent or a fixed base) and $\int\ln x\,dx$ (write $\ln x=1\cdot\ln x$ and integrate by parts). Part c) shows the physics behind the “$+C$”: the integration constants are the starting position and starting velocity.

You need

Power rule $(x^n)'=nx^{n-1}$; chain rule $(f(g(x)))'=f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$; $(e^x)'=e^x$; $(\ln|x|)'=\frac1x$; partial integration $\int u'v=uv-\int uv'$. Every antiderivative gets a constant $+C$.

Hint 1

a) Write $\frac1x=x^{-1}$ and use the power rule. For $\sin(ax)$, use the chain rule (inner function $ax$). b) Each antiderivative is a derivative from a) read backwards: check your answer by differentiating it.

Hint 2

$x^x=e^{\ln(x^x)}=e^{x\ln x}$. Now it is $e^{(\dots)}$ with an inner function $x\ln x$: chain rule, then product rule for the inner derivative. For $\int e^{-x/\tau}dx$: what do you have to multiply $e^{-x/\tau}$ by so that its derivative is $e^{-x/\tau}$?

Hint 3

$\int\ln x\,dx=\int 1\cdot\ln x\,dx$: take $u'=1$ (so $u=x$) and $v=\ln x$ (so $v'=\frac1x$). Then $uv'=1$, which is easy to integrate. c) $\dot s=v_0+a_0t$, $\ddot s=a_0$. Integrate each and ask which constant the “$+C$” has to be.

Solution

a) Derivatives.

Why this stepWhat you write

Power rule; $\frac1x$ is the power $n=-1$.

$\frac{d}{dx}ax^n=nax^{n-1}$, $\quad\frac{d}{dx}\frac1x=-\frac1{x^2}$.

Chain rule: outer $\sin$, inner $ax$ with derivative $a$. And $\ln|x|$ has derivative $\frac1x$ on both sides of $0$.

$\frac{d}{dx}\sin(ax)=a\cos(ax)$, $\quad\frac{d}{dx}\ln|x|=\frac1x$.

Neither base nor exponent is fixed, so rewrite with $e$ first. Then chain rule, then product rule on $x\ln x$.

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}x^x&=\frac{d}{dx}e^{x\ln x}\\&=e^{x\ln x}\Big(\ln x+x\cdot\frac1x\Big)\\&=x^x\,(1+\ln x).\end{aligned}$$

b) Antiderivatives ($C$ is a constant).

Power rule backwards: raise the power by one, divide by the new power. That is why $n\neq-1$.

$\int ax^n\,dx=\frac{a\,x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C$, $\quad\int\frac1x\,dx=\ln|x|+C$.

Differentiating $e^{-x/\tau}$ brings out $-\frac1\tau$; to undo that, multiply by $-\tau$. Same for $\cos(ax)$ with $\frac1a$.

$\int e^{-x/\tau}dx=-\tau e^{-x/\tau}+C$, $\quad\int\cos(ax)\,dx=\frac1a\sin(ax)+C$.

Partial integration with $u'=1$, $v=\ln x$: the new integral is just $\int1\,dx$.

$$\begin{aligned}\int\ln x\,dx&=\int1\cdot\ln x\,dx\\&=x\ln x-\int x\cdot\frac1x\,dx\\&=x\ln x-x+C.\end{aligned}$$

c) Back and forth.

Differentiate term by term: the constant $s_0$ disappears, $v_0t$ gives $v_0$, $\frac12a_0t^2$ gives $a_0t$.

$\dot s(t)=v_0+a_0t=v(t)$, $\quad\ddot s(t)=a_0$.

Integrating gives back the same formulas, but with unknown constants in place of $s_0$ and $v_0$.

$\int\dot s\,dt=v_0t+\frac12a_0t^2+C_1$, $\quad\int\ddot s\,dt=a_0t+C_2$.

The physics fixes the constants: they are the initial conditions. Differentiating loses that information; the problem has to give it back to you.

The initial conditions fix the constants: $C_1=s_0$ (starting position) and $C_2=v_0$ (starting velocity).

Trap

Writing $\frac{d}{dx}x^x=x\cdot x^{x-1}$ (power rule) or $x^x\ln x$ (exponential rule). Each rule needs one part to be fixed, and here neither is. Always rewrite as $e^{x\ln x}$. In physics, also never drop the $+C$: it is where the initial conditions go.

Aufgabe 2 · The frog's long jump

A frog jumps with take-off speed $v_0$ (fixed) at angle $\theta$ to the ground. No air resistance.

  1. It jumps from level ground and lands at the same height ($h=0$) in the water. Show that the best angle (longest jump) is $\theta=45^\circ$.
  2. Now it jumps from a bank of height $h$ above the water. Find the best angle as a function of $h$. Guide on the sheet: eliminate the time to get the range $x(\theta)$, introduce the dimensionless $a=\frac{2gh}{v_0^2}$, and you should get $$\begin{aligned}x(\theta)=\frac{v_0^2}{g}\cos\theta\Big[&\sin\theta\\&+\sqrt{\sin^2\theta+a}\Big].\end{aligned}$$ At the best angle $\frac{dx}{d\theta}=0$; simplify that condition, square it, use $\cos^2\theta=1-\sin^2\theta$ and solve for $\sin^2\theta$. Check your result for $h=0$.
The idea

A throw without air resistance is two independent motions at once (superposition): steady motion sideways, free fall up and down. The vertical motion alone decides how long the frog is in the air; the horizontal motion turns that time into a distance. So: find the landing time from $y(t)=0$, put it into $x(t)$, and you have the range as a function of the angle. The best angle is where this function has its maximum. From a raised bank the frog gets extra fall time for free, so it pays to aim flatter than $45^\circ$.

Range against angle (units of $v_0^2/g$). On level ground the best angle is $45^\circ$. From a bank (here $a=1$, i.e. $h=v_0^2/2g$) the jump is longer and the best angle is flatter, about $35^\circ$.

You need

Throw equations (superposition): $x(t)=v_0\cos\theta\,t$, $y(t)=y_0+v_0\sin\theta\,t-\frac12gt^2$ (Physics lesson 1). Quadratic formula. $2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\sin2\theta$. A maximum of a smooth function has derivative $0$.

Hint 1

a) Solve $y(t)=v_0\sin\theta\,t-\frac12gt^2=0$ for the landing time ($t\neq0$). Put it into $x(t)$ and use $2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\sin2\theta$. When is $\sin2\theta$ largest?

Hint 2

b) Now $y(t)=h+v_0\sin\theta\,t-\frac12gt^2=0$ is a quadratic in $t$. Take the positive root, put it into $x(t)$ and pull out $\frac{v_0^2}{g}$: that gives the formula on the sheet. To keep the derivative readable, call the root $s=\sqrt{\sin^2\theta+a}$, so $s^2=\sin^2\theta+a$ and $s'=\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{s}$.

Hint 3

Setting $\frac{dx}{d\theta}=0$ and multiplying by $s$ simplifies (use $a=s^2-\sin^2\theta=(s+\sin\theta)(s-\sin\theta)$) to $s\sin\theta=\cos^2\theta$. Square it, put $u=\sin^2\theta$: you get $(u+a)u=(1-u)^2$, and the $u^2$ cancels.

Solution

a) Level ground.

Why this stepWhat you write

Split the motion: no force sideways (constant velocity), gravity downwards (free fall).

$x(t)=v_0\cos\theta\cdot t$, $\quad y(t)=v_0\sin\theta\cdot t-\frac12gt^2$.

The frog lands when it is back at height $0$. The other root, $t=0$, is the take-off.

$y(t)=0$, $t\neq0$: $\ t_{\text{land}}=\dfrac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g}$.

Time in the air → distance, then the double-angle identity.

$$\begin{aligned}x(\theta)&=v_0\cos\theta\cdot\frac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g}\\&=\frac{v_0^2}{g}\,\sin(2\theta).\end{aligned}$$

The prefactor is fixed, so maximise the sine. Its largest value is $1$.

$\sin2\theta=1\Rightarrow2\theta=90^\circ\Rightarrow\theta_{\text{opt}}=45^\circ$.

b) From a bank of height $h$.

Same split, but the frog starts at height $h$ above the water (water at $y=0$).

$x(t)=v_0\cos\theta\cdot t$, $\quad y(t)=h+v_0\sin\theta\cdot t-\frac12gt^2$.

Landing: $y=0$ is a quadratic in $t$. The root with “$-$” is negative (before the jump), so it is unphysical.

$$\begin{aligned}t_{\text{land}}=\frac1g\Big(&v_0\sin\theta\\&+\sqrt{v_0^2\sin^2\theta+2gh}\Big).\end{aligned}$$

Put it into $x(t)$, pull $v_0$ out of the root, and name the pure number $a=\frac{2gh}{v_0^2}$ and the root $s$.

$$x(\theta)=\frac{v_0^2}{g}\cos\theta\,\big(\sin\theta+s\big),$$ with $a=\frac{2gh}{v_0^2}$ and $s=\sqrt{\sin^2\theta+a}$.

Best angle: derivative zero. Product rule, and $s'=\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{s}$ by the chain rule. The prefactor $\frac{v_0^2}{g}\neq0$ drops out.

$$\begin{aligned}0=&-\sin\theta\,(\sin\theta+s)\\&+\cos\theta\Big(\cos\theta+\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{s}\Big).\end{aligned}$$

Multiply by $s$ to clear the fraction, then sort.

$$\begin{aligned}0&=s(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta)\\&\quad-s^2\sin\theta+\sin\theta\cos^2\theta.\end{aligned}$$

Replace $s^2$ by $\sin^2\theta+a$ and collect: the $a$-term stands alone, everything else has the factor $(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta)$.

$$\begin{aligned}a\sin\theta&=(s+\sin\theta)\\&\quad\cdot(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta).\end{aligned}$$

Trick: $a=s^2-\sin^2\theta$ is a difference of squares. Then the factor $(s+\sin\theta)>0$ appears on both sides and can be cancelled.

$$\begin{aligned}(s-\sin\theta)\sin\theta&=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\\ \Rightarrow\quad s\sin\theta&=\cos^2\theta.\end{aligned}$$

Square to get rid of the root, write everything with $u=\sin^2\theta$. The $u^2$ cancels, so it is a linear equation.

$$\begin{aligned}(u+a)\,u&=(1-u)^2\\u^2+au&=1-2u+u^2\\u&=\frac1{2+a}.\end{aligned}$$

Back to the angle, and put in $a$.

$$\begin{aligned}\sin^2\theta_{\text{opt}}&=\frac1{2+2gh/v_0^2},\\ \theta_{\text{opt}}&=\arcsin\frac{v_0}{\sqrt{2(v_0^2+gh)}}.\end{aligned}$$

Check that asked for on the sheet.

$h=0$: $\theta_{\text{opt}}=\arcsin\frac1{\sqrt2}=45^\circ$. ✓ For $h>0$ the angle is smaller than $45^\circ$.

Another way to write it (from the official solution)

From $\sin^2\theta=\frac1{2+a}$ you get $\cos^2\theta=\frac{1+a}{2+a}$, so $$\begin{aligned}\tan\theta_{\text{opt}}&=\frac1{\sqrt{1+a}}\\&=\frac{v_0}{\sqrt{v_0^2+2gh}}.\end{aligned}$$ This form is the easiest to remember: the numerator is the take-off speed, the denominator is the speed when hitting the water (energy conservation: $v^2=v_0^2+2gh$).

Trap

Taking the “$-$” root of the quadratic for the landing time: it is negative and belongs to a frog that would have started below the bank. Second trap: after squaring $s\sin\theta=\cos^2\theta$ you might also pick up solutions of $s\sin\theta=-\cos^2\theta$. Both sides are positive for $0<\theta<90^\circ$, so here nothing goes wrong, but say so.

Aufgabe 3 · Shooting at a falling plate (lecture experiment)

A spring cannon at the origin aims straight at a plate at horizontal distance $s$ and height $h$. At $t=0$ the ball leaves the cannon with speed $v_0$ at angle $\alpha$; at the same moment the plate is released and falls freely from rest. Neglect air resistance and the sizes of ball and plate.

  1. Write down the position vectors $\vec r_K(t)$ of the ball and $\vec r_P(t)$ of the plate.
  2. Find the time $t_1$ at which the ball reaches the plate's $x$-position, as a function of $v_0$.
  3. How large must $v_0$ be for the ball to hit the plate? Explain the result.
The idea

Without gravity, the ball would fly along the straight aiming line and hit the plate where it hangs. Gravity pulls both down by exactly the same amount, $\frac12gt^2$, in the same time. So the ball's drop below the aiming line always equals the plate's drop below its starting point: they meet, whatever the speed. The speed only decides where they meet. If it is too slow, the meeting point is below the floor.

Both ball and plate fall $\frac12gt_1^2$ below where they “would have been” without gravity. So they meet at $x=s$ for every $v_0$, just lower for a slower ball.

You need

Throw equations (superposition) from Physics lesson 1; free fall from rest $y=y_0-\frac12gt^2$. The aiming condition gives the angle: $\tan\alpha=\frac hs$.

Hint 1

a) Ball: $x_K=v_0\cos\alpha\,t$, $y_K=v_0\sin\alpha\,t-\frac12gt^2$. Plate: its $x$ never changes; its $y$ starts at $h$ with zero speed. From the right triangle with legs $s$ and $h$, write $\sin\alpha$ and $\cos\alpha$ with $s$ and $h$.

Hint 2

b) Solve $x_K(t_1)=s$ for $t_1$ and simplify with $\cos\alpha=\frac{s}{\sqrt{s^2+h^2}}$. c) Compute $y_K(t_1)$ and $y_P(t_1)$. Something surprising happens: what is the difference?

Hint 3

Both heights come out as $h-\frac12gt_1^2$, for every $v_0$. So the only real condition is that the meeting happens above the floor: $h-\frac12gt_1^2\ge0$ (taking the floor at the cannon's height). Solve that for $v_0$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

“Aims directly at the plate” is a geometric condition: the aiming line goes through $(s,h)$.

$\tan\alpha=\frac hs$, so $\sin\alpha=\frac h{\sqrt{s^2+h^2}}$, $\cos\alpha=\frac s{\sqrt{s^2+h^2}}$.

a) Ball: uniform motion in $x$ plus free fall in $y$ (superposition).

$$\vec r_K(t)=\begin{pmatrix}v_0t\cos\alpha\\ v_0t\sin\alpha-\frac12gt^2\end{pmatrix}.$$

Plate: stays at $x=s$, falls from height $h$ starting at rest.

$$\vec r_P(t)=\begin{pmatrix}s\\ h-\frac12gt^2\end{pmatrix}.$$

b) The ball reaches the plate's $x$-position when the $x$-coordinates agree.

$$\begin{aligned}v_0t_1\cos\alpha&=s\\ \Rightarrow\ t_1&=\frac{s}{v_0\cos\alpha}\\&=\frac{\sqrt{s^2+h^2}}{v_0}.\end{aligned}$$

c) Compare heights at $t_1$. In the ball's height, $v_0t_1\sin\alpha$ is exactly $h$: the height of the aiming line at $x=s$.

Since $v_0t_1=\sqrt{s^2+h^2}$, we get $v_0t_1\sin\alpha=h$, so $$\begin{aligned}y_K(t_1)&=v_0t_1\sin\alpha-\tfrac12gt_1^2\\&=h-\tfrac12gt_1^2\\&=y_P(t_1).\end{aligned}$$

So the paths always cross, for every $v_0>0$. The real condition is that this happens before the plate hits the floor (floor at $y=0$).

Hit requires $y_P(t_1)\ge0$: $$h\ge\frac12g\,\frac{s^2+h^2}{v_0^2}.$$

Solve for $v_0$.

$$v_0\ge\sqrt{\frac{g\,(s^2+h^2)}{2h}}.$$

Explain, as the task asks: same gravity, same time, same drop.

Ball and plate are pulled down by the same $\frac12gt_1^2$ in the same time (superposition). $v_0$ only decides how fast the ball crosses the distance, and so how high the hit is. If $v_0$ is too small, the meeting point would be below the floor.

Trap

Thinking you must aim below the plate because it falls. Aiming straight at it is exactly right. Also: “they always meet” is not the end of c). The sheet asks how large $v_0$ must be, and the floor gives the answer.

Aufgabe 4 · Motion on a spiral

A point moves with constant speed on a spiral (helix) on a cylinder of radius $R$ around the $z$-axis. Its velocity components are $$\begin{aligned}\dot x&=-R\omega\sin(\omega t),\\ \dot y&=R\omega\cos(\omega t),\\ \dot z&=v_z,\end{aligned}$$ with constant $\omega$ and $v_z$.

  1. Find $x(t)$, $y(t)$, $z(t)$ and sketch the path. Interpret the integration constants. Some of them are fixed because the motion lies on the cylinder.
  2. Find the length $\Delta s$ of the path along the spiral.
The idea

Seen from above, the point goes round a circle; seen from the side, it rises steadily. Integrate each component separately. The constants are starting values, and “stays on the cylinder” forces the $x$- and $y$-constants to be $0$. For the length, add up tiny steps $|d\vec r|=|\dot{\vec r}|\,dt$. The speed $|\dot{\vec r}|$ turns out to be constant, so the length is simply speed × time. Picture it: cut the cylinder open and roll it flat, and the spiral becomes a straight line.

Unrolled, the spiral is a straight line: $\Delta s=\sqrt{(R\omega t)^2+(v_zt)^2}$ by Pythagoras. That is the answer to b) in one picture.

You need

$\int\sin(\omega t)\,dt=-\frac1\omega\cos(\omega t)+C$, $\int\cos(\omega t)\,dt=\frac1\omega\sin(\omega t)+C$ (substitution $f=\omega t$). Arc length: $\Delta s=\int|\dot{\vec r}|\,dt$ with $|\dot{\vec r}|=\sqrt{\dot x^2+\dot y^2+\dot z^2}$. Circular motion: Physics lesson 1.

Hint 1

a) Integrate each component on its own; each gets its own constant $c_x$, $c_y$, $c_z$. Check: what are $x(t)^2+y(t)^2$? It must equal $R^2$ at all times, because the point stays on the cylinder.

Hint 2

With $x=R\cos\omega t+c_x$ and $y=R\sin\omega t+c_y$, the point goes round a circle of radius $R$ centred at $(c_x,c_y)$. The cylinder's axis is the $z$-axis, so the centre must be $(0,0)$. $c_z$ is free: it is the starting height.

Hint 3

b) $\dot x^2+\dot y^2=R^2\omega^2(\sin^2+\cos^2)=R^2\omega^2$. So $|\dot{\vec r}|=\sqrt{R^2\omega^2+v_z^2}$ is constant, and the integral is just this times the elapsed time.

Solution

a) Position.

Why this stepWhat you write

$z$ first: constant velocity, so position grows linearly. The constant is the starting height.

$z(t)=v_zt+c_z$, with $c_z=z(0)$.

$x$ and $y$: integrate with the substitution $f=\omega t$ (the $\omega$ from the inner derivative cancels the $\omega$ in front).

$x(t)=R\cos(\omega t)+c_x$, $\quad y(t)=R\sin(\omega t)+c_y$.

The point must stay on the cylinder around the $z$-axis at all times. That only works if the circle is centred on the axis.

$x(t)^2+y(t)^2=R^2$ for all $t$ forces $c_x=c_y=0$.

Collect. Any $c_z$ is allowed (the official sketch uses $c_z=0$).

$$\vec r(t)=\begin{pmatrix}R\cos(\omega t)\\R\sin(\omega t)\\v_zt+c_z\end{pmatrix}.$$

b) Length.

Length = sum of tiny steps $|d\vec r|$, and each step is speed × $d\tau$.

$$\begin{aligned}\Delta s&=\int|d\vec r|\\&=\int_{t_0}^{t}\sqrt{\dot x^2+\dot y^2+\dot z^2}\;d\tau.\end{aligned}$$

Insert the components; $\sin^2+\cos^2=1$ removes all the time dependence.

$$\begin{aligned}\Delta s&=\int_{t_0}^{t}\sqrt{R^2\omega^2+v_z^2}\;d\tau\\&=\sqrt{R^2\omega^2+v_z^2}\;(t-t_0).\end{aligned}$$

Starting the clock at $t_0=0$.

$\Delta s=\sqrt{R^2\omega^2+v_z^2}\;t$.

Trap

Leaving $c_x$ and $c_y$ free and calling them “starting positions”. The task says the motion lies on the cylinder around the $z$-axis, and that fixes them to $0$. Only $c_z$ is a free starting value. In b), don't forget $v_z$: the length is not just $R\omega t$ (that would be the length of the shadow on the floor, the circle).
