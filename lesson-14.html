1. The story: the tower over the lake

The lake kingdom wants a diving tower that reaches far out over the water, built only from loose stone blocks, without mortar. The master builder stacks them from the top down: the top block sticks out by half its length, the next by a quarter, then a sixth, an eighth, … Each new block adds a smaller overhang. The apprentices argue. “The additions shrink to nothing, so the tower must stop growing at some point,” says one. “Shrinking isn't enough,” says the master. “What matters is whether the running total settles down.” And indeed: with enough blocks, this tower reaches as far over the lake as you like. The additions $\frac12,\frac14,\frac16,\dots$ are half of the harmonic series, and its running totals never settle.

The royal messenger is different. On day one she covers half the road to the far city, on day two half of what remains, and so on. Her daily distances $\frac12,\frac14,\frac18,\dots$ shrink much faster, and her running total settles at exactly the whole road: $1$.

And then there's the ferryman, who rows one league out, half a league back, a third out, a quarter back… His position wobbles, but the wobbles shrink and always turn around, so he settles at a point on the lake.

In maths: the additions are the terms $a_k$, the running total after $n$ steps is the partial sum $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^na_k$, and “settles down” means the sequence $(s_n)_n$ converges. The tower is the harmonic series (divergent), the messenger the geometric series (convergent), and the ferryman the alternating harmonic series (convergent thanks to the Leibniz criterion).

2. Series and partial sums

Definition 7.1 (p. 293). Let $(a_k)_k$ be a real or complex sequence. The series $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k$ has the partial sums $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^na_k$. The series converges if the limit $\lim_{n\to\infty}s_n$ exists, and then this limit is its value:

$$\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^na_k.$$

Otherwise the series diverges. The index may start elsewhere (for example at $k=0$).

The idea in one sentence: a series is nothing new. It is the sequence of its partial sums, and everything from lessons 11 and 12 applies to that sequence. Careful with the notation: the symbol $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k$ means both “the series” (before we know anything) and “its value” (if it converges).

Worked example: the geometric series (Beispiel 7.3, p. 294)

Claim: for $|q|<1$, $\sum_{k=0}^\infty q^k=\frac1{1-q}$. For $|q|\ge1$ the series diverges.

  1. Write down the partial sum $s_n=\sum_{k=0}^nq^k$ in closed form (for $q\neq1$).

    Answer

    The geometric sum formula: $s_n=\frac{1-q^{n+1}}{1-q}$. (Check: $(1-q)s_n=1-q^{n+1}$, since the middle terms cancel.)

  2. What happens to $q^{n+1}$ when $|q|<1$? What is the limit of $s_n$?

    Answer

    $q^{n+1}\to0$ for $|q|<1$ (lesson 10), so $s_n\to\frac1{1-q}$.

  3. Why does it diverge for $|q|\ge1$? (Use Proof A below.)

    Answer

    Then $|q^k|=|q|^k\ge1$, so the terms do not tend to $0$, and by Proof A the series cannot converge.

Useful variant: starting at $k=1$, $\sum_{k=1}^\infty q^k=\frac1{1-q}-1=\frac q{1-q}$. Example: $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{2^k}=1$, the messenger's road.

Proof A: the terms of a convergent series tend to 0 (Proposition 7.2, p. 294)

Proposition 7.2. If $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k$ converges, then $a_n\to0$.

Used backwards (the term test): if $a_n\not\to0$, the series diverges. The converse is false: $\frac1n\to0$, but $\sum\frac1n$ diverges (Proof B).

The idea. Each term is the difference of two neighbouring partial sums, $a_n=s_n-s_{n-1}$. If the partial sums settle at $S$, both $s_n$ and $s_{n-1}$ are close to $S$, so their difference is close to $0$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^na_k\to S$.” Last line: “so $a_n\to0$.”
  • The gap: express $a_n$ through the partial sums, then use the limit rules.
The storyWhat you write

Suppose the tower's reach settles at some point $S$.

Let $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k$ converge, so $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^na_k\to S$ for some $S$.

The overhang of block $n$ is the reach after $n$ blocks minus the reach after $n-1$.

For $n\ge2$: $a_n=s_n-s_{n-1}$.

Both reaches settle at $S$, so the overhang shrinks to nothing.

Also $s_{n-1}\to S$ (the same sequence, shifted by one). By the difference rule, $a_n=s_n-s_{n-1}\to S-S=0$. ∎

Moves used: go to partial sums

The proof in one piece

Let $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^na_k\to S$. Then also $s_{n-1}\to S$, and $a_n=s_n-s_{n-1}\to S-S=0$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why is $\lim s_{n-1}=S$ allowed?

    Answer

    $(s_{n-1})_{n\ge2}$ is the sequence $(s_n)_n$ shifted by one index. Shifting doesn't change the limit: if $|s_m-S|<\varepsilon$ for $m\ge N$, then $|s_{n-1}-S|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N+1$.

  2. Rung 2 (1 min). Use the term test on $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac n{n+1}$.

    Answer

    $\frac n{n+1}\to1\neq0$, so the series diverges.

  3. Rung 3 (2 min). Write the proof from the frame alone.

Three rules that follow straight from the definition (p. 296–297):

  • Linearity (Lemma 7.6): if $\sum a_k$ and $\sum b_k$ converge, then $\sum(a_k+b_k)=\sum a_k+\sum b_k$ and $\sum\alpha a_k=\alpha\sum a_k$. (Partial sums add up, then use the limit rules.)
  • Finitely many terms don't matter (Lemma 7.8): $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k$ converges ⇔ $\sum_{k=N}^\infty a_k$ converges, and then $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}a_k+\sum_{k=N}^\infty a_k$. So for convergence you may ignore, change or add finitely many terms; only the value changes.
  • Brackets (Lemma 7.9): in a convergent series you may group neighbouring terms into brackets without changing the value (the partial sums of the bracketed series are a subsequence). The converse fails: $(1-1)+(1-1)+\dots=0$, but $\sum(-1)^n$ diverges.

Worked example: a telescoping series

Compute $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k(k+1)}$.

  1. Split $\frac1{k(k+1)}$ into a difference of two simpler fractions.

    Answer

    $\frac1{k(k+1)}=\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}$ (check: $\frac{(k+1)-k}{k(k+1)}$).

  2. Compute the partial sum $s_n$.

    Answer

    The middle terms cancel:

    $$s_n=\left(1-\tfrac12\right)+\left(\tfrac12-\tfrac13\right)+\dots+\left(\tfrac1n-\tfrac1{n+1}\right)=1-\tfrac1{n+1}.$$

  3. What is the value?

    Answer

    $s_n=1-\frac1{n+1}\to1$, so $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k(k+1)}=1$.

The recipe for “compute the value”: partial fractions, write out $s_n$, cancel, take the limit. Never cancel inside the infinite sum directly; always go through $s_n$.

Proof B: the harmonic series diverges (Beispiel 7.4, p. 294)

Claim. $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1k$ diverges; in fact its partial sums are unbounded, so $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1k=\infty$.

The idea. Group the terms into blocks that end at powers of $2$: $\frac12$, then $\frac13+\frac14$, then $\frac15+\dots+\frac18$, and so on. Each block contains $2^{j-1}$ terms, each at least as big as the block's last term $\frac1{2^j}$, so each block is at least $2^{j-1}\cdot\frac1{2^j}=\frac12$. Infinitely many halves add up to more than any bound.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Goal: the partial sums $s_n$ are unbounded (then they can't converge, since convergent sequences are bounded).
  • Last line: “$s_{2^\ell}\ge1+\frac\ell2$ for every $\ell$, so $(s_n)_n$ is unbounded.”
  • The gap: a lower bound for the block sums.
The storyWhat you write

Count the tower's overhangs in batches: the first block, then the next one, the next two, the next four, …

Let $\ell\in\mathbb N$. Group the partial sum $s_{2^\ell}$ into blocks:

$$s_{2^\ell}=1+\sum_{j=1}^\ell\ \sum_{k=2^{j-1}+1}^{2^j}\frac1k.$$

In each batch, every overhang is at least as big as the batch's smallest one.

Block $j$ has $2^j-2^{j-1}=2^{j-1}$ terms, and each satisfies $\frac1k\ge\frac1{2^j}$ (since $k\le2^j$). So

$$\sum_{k=2^{j-1}+1}^{2^j}\frac1k\ge2^{j-1}\cdot\frac1{2^j}=\frac12.$$

So every batch adds at least half a block length, and there are as many batches as you like.

Hence $s_{2^\ell}\ge1+\frac\ell2$ for every $\ell$. Given $K>0$, choose $\ell>2K$ (Archimedes); then $s_{2^\ell}>K$. So $(s_n)_n$ is unbounded, hence divergent. As $(s_n)_n$ is increasing, $s_n\to\infty$. ∎

Moves used: group into blocks choose n by Archimedes go to partial sums

The proof in one piece

For $j\ge1$ the block $\sum_{k=2^{j-1}+1}^{2^j}\frac1k$ has $2^{j-1}$ terms, each $\ge\frac1{2^j}$, so it is $\ge\frac12$. Hence $s_{2^\ell}=1+\sum_{j=1}^\ell\sum_{k=2^{j-1}+1}^{2^j}\frac1k\ge1+\frac\ell2$, which exceeds any $K$ for $\ell>2K$. So the partial sums are unbounded and the series diverges (to $\infty$, since the partial sums increase). ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Blocks from $2^{j-1}+1$ to $2^j$: there are $2^{j-1}$ terms.
  2. Each term $\ge$ the last one, $\frac1{2^j}$.
  3. Each block $\ge\frac12$.
  4. So $s_{2^\ell}\ge1+\frac\ell2\to\infty$.

In short: “blocks of $2^{j-1}$ terms, each block at least a half”.

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Write out the block for $j=3$ and check that it is $\ge\frac12$.

    Answer

    $\frac15+\frac16+\frac17+\frac18\ge4\cdot\frac18=\frac12$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Why does “unbounded” already imply “divergent”?

    Answer

    Every convergent sequence is bounded (lesson 10). The partial sums are unbounded, so they don't converge.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the exam box alone.

A second proof, with the Cauchy criterion (Beispiel 7.27, p. 305): for every $N$, $\sum_{k=N+1}^{2N}\frac1k\ge N\cdot\frac1{2N}=\frac12$. So the “tail pieces” never get small, and the Cauchy criterion for series (section 5) fails with $\varepsilon=\frac12$.

3. Series with non-negative terms

The idea. If all terms are $\ge0$, the partial sums can only go up. A sequence that only goes up has just two options (lesson 11): it is bounded and converges, or it is unbounded and tends to $\infty$. So for such series, “converges” is the same as “the partial sums are bounded”, and you never need to guess the value.

Proposition 7.11 (p. 297). If $a_k\ge0$ for all $k$, the partial sums are increasing. The series converges if they are bounded; otherwise $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k=\infty$.

Proof C: the comparison test (Korollar 7.12, p. 297)

Comparison test. Let $0\le a_k\le b_k$ for all $k$. Then $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k\le\sum_{k=1}^\infty b_k$, and

  • $\sum b_k$ converges ⇒ $\sum a_k$ converges (a convergent majorant);
  • $\sum a_k$ diverges ⇒ $\sum b_k$ diverges (a divergent minorant).

Both also hold if $0\le a_k\le b_k$ only for all $k$ from some $N$ on (finitely many terms don't matter).

You'll need this (lesson 11)
  • Monotone convergence: an increasing sequence that is bounded above converges (to its supremum).

The idea. The small series' partial sums are below the big series' partial sums, which are below the big series' value. So the small partial sums are increasing and bounded: they converge. The second bullet is the first one read backwards.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $A_n=\sum_{k=1}^na_k$ and $B_n=\sum_{k=1}^nb_k$, and let $\sum b_k=B$ converge.” Last line: “so $(A_n)_n$ converges, and $\sum a_k\le B$.”
  • The gap: show $(A_n)_n$ is increasing and bounded above.
The storyWhat you write

Two towers, block for block: each overhang of the first is at most the matching overhang of the second.

Let $A_n=\sum_{k=1}^na_k$ and $B_n=\sum_{k=1}^nb_k$. Since $0\le a_k\le b_k$, we get $A_n\le B_n$ for all $n$, and both sequences are increasing.

If the second tower's reach settles at $B$, it never goes past $B$, and the first tower stays behind it.

Let $\sum b_k$ converge with value $B$. As $(B_n)_n$ is increasing with limit $B$, $B_n\le B$ for all $n$. Hence $A_n\le B_n\le B$.

A reach that only grows and never passes a mark must settle.

So $(A_n)_n$ is increasing and bounded above by $B$, hence convergent by monotone convergence, and its limit is $\le B$. So $\sum a_k$ converges and $\sum a_k\le\sum b_k$.

And if the first tower reaches arbitrarily far, the second one, always ahead, must too.

The second statement is the contrapositive of the first: if $\sum b_k$ converged, so would $\sum a_k$. ∎

Moves used: go to partial sums monotone and bounded prove the contrapositive

The proof in one piece

Let $A_n=\sum_{k=1}^na_k$, $B_n=\sum_{k=1}^nb_k$. From $0\le a_k\le b_k$: both are increasing and $A_n\le B_n$. If $\sum b_k=B$ converges, then $B_n\le B$ (an increasing sequence stays below its limit), so $A_n\le B$. Thus $(A_n)_n$ is increasing and bounded, hence convergent, with limit $\le B$. The divergence statement is the contrapositive. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Where do we use $a_k\ge0$?

    Answer

    To know that $(A_n)_n$ is increasing, so that monotone convergence applies. Without it the test fails: $a_k=-1\le b_k=\frac1{k^2}$, and $\sum b_k$ converges but $\sum a_k$ doesn't.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Explain the “from some $N$ on” version in one sentence.

    Answer

    Apply the test to $\sum_{k=N}^\infty a_k$ and $\sum_{k=N}^\infty b_k$; dropping the first $N-1$ terms doesn't change convergence.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Worked example: $\sum\frac1{k^2}$ converges (Beispiel 7.14, p. 298)

  1. Find a telescoping majorant: compare $\frac1{k^2}$ with $\frac1{k(k-1)}$ for $k\ge2$.

    Answer

    $k(k-1)\le k^2$, so $\frac1{k^2}\le\frac1{k(k-1)}=\frac1{k-1}-\frac1k$.

  2. Does $\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{k(k-1)}$ converge?

    Answer

    Its partial sums telescope: $\sum_{k=2}^n\left(\frac1{k-1}-\frac1k\right)=1-\frac1n\to1$. Yes, with value $1$.

  3. Conclude.

    Answer

    By the comparison test, $\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{k^2}$ converges (with value $\le1$), and so does $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k^2}$ (one more term). Its value is $\le2$. (The exact value is $\frac{\pi^2}6$; see For wonder.)

The limit comparison test, the everyday tool (the idea of Beispiel 7.15, p. 298). Finding an exact inequality is often fiddly. Instead, compare ratios, like Landau's test in lesson 13:

Limit comparison. Let $a_n,b_n>0$ and $\frac{a_n}{b_n}\to c$ with $0<c<\infty$. Then $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ either both converge or both diverge.

Why: from some $N$ on, $\frac c2\le\frac{a_n}{b_n}\le2c$ (take $\varepsilon=\frac c2$ in the limit definition). So $a_n\le2c\,b_n$ and $b_n\le\frac2c\,a_n$ for $n\ge N$, and the comparison test works in both directions.

In practice: keep only the dominant powers in the numerator and denominator, and compare with $\sum\frac1{n^p}$ from the $p$-test below. Example: $\frac{2n-10}{n^3-10n+100}$ behaves like $\frac{2n}{n^3}=\frac2{n^2}$; indeed $\frac{a_n}{1/n^2}\to2$, so the series converges (Beispiel 7.15). (Its first terms are negative, but finitely many terms don't matter.)

Proof D: condensation and the p-test (Proposition 7.16 and Beispiel 7.17, p. 299)

Condensation (Proposition 7.16). Let $a_1\ge a_2\ge\dots\ge0$. Then $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k$ converges ⇔ $\sum_{k=0}^\infty2^ka_{2^k}$ converges.

The $p$-test (Beispiel 7.17). $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^p}$ converges ⇔ $p>1$.

The idea. It's Proof B's trick, made into a tool. For a decreasing sequence, the block from $2^k+1$ to $2^{k+1}$ has $2^k$ terms, all between $a_{2^{k+1}}$ and $a_{2^k}$. So the block sum is trapped between $2^ka_{2^{k+1}}$ and $2^ka_{2^k}$, and the whole series is trapped between two versions of the “condensed” series $\sum2^ka_{2^k}$. For $a_n=\frac1{n^p}$ the condensed series is geometric, and we know exactly when that converges.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Both series have non-negative terms, so “converges” ⇔ “partial sums bounded” (see the start of section 3). Two directions: bound each series' partial sums by the other's.
  • The gap: the block inequality $2^ka_{2^{k+1}}\le\sum_{\ell=2^k+1}^{2^{k+1}}a_\ell\le2^ka_{2^k}$.
The storyWhat you write

Count the overhangs in batches of 1, 2, 4, 8, … blocks. In a batch, every overhang lies between the batch's last and the previous batch's last.

For $k\ge0$ the block $\ell=2^k+1,\dots,2^{k+1}$ has $2^k$ terms, and $a_{2^{k+1}}\le a_\ell\le a_{2^k}$ for these $\ell$ (monotonicity). So

$$2^ka_{2^{k+1}}\le\sum_{\ell=2^k+1}^{2^{k+1}}a_\ell\le2^ka_{2^k}.$$

Add up the first $m+1$ batches.

Summing over $k=0,\dots,m$ (the blocks fill $\ell=2,\dots,2^{m+1}$):

$$\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}2^ka_{2^k}\le\sum_{\ell=2}^{2^{m+1}}a_\ell\le\sum_{k=0}^m2^ka_{2^k}.$$

(Left: $2^ka_{2^{k+1}}=\frac12\,2^{k+1}a_{2^{k+1}}$, then rename $k+1$ as $k$.)

If the condensed tower is bounded, so is the real one.

“⇐”: if $\sum2^ka_{2^k}=C$ converges, the right inequality gives $s_{2^{m+1}}\le a_1+C$ for all $m$. Since $(s_n)_n$ is increasing and $n\le2^{m+1}$ for suitable $m$, all $s_n\le a_1+C$. So $\sum a_k$ converges.

And if the real tower is bounded, so is the condensed one.

“⇒”: if $\sum a_k=S$ converges, the left inequality gives $\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}2^ka_{2^k}\le2S$ for all $m$. So the partial sums of $\sum2^ka_{2^k}$ are bounded, and it converges. ∎

Now the tower with overhangs $\frac1{n^p}$.

$p$-test. If $p\le0$, then $\frac1{n^p}\ge1$, the terms don't tend to $0$, and the series diverges (Proof A). If $p>0$, then $\left(\frac1{n^p}\right)_n$ is decreasing and positive, and the condensed series is

$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty2^k\cdot\frac1{(2^k)^p}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(2^{1-p}\right)^k,$$

a geometric series with $q=2^{1-p}>0$. It converges ⇔ $2^{1-p}<1$ ⇔ $1-p<0$ ⇔ $p>1$. ∎

Moves used: group into blocks monotone and bounded find a majorant

The proof in one piece

For $k\ge0$, the $2^k$ terms $a_\ell$ with $2^k<\ell\le2^{k+1}$ satisfy $a_{2^{k+1}}\le a_\ell\le a_{2^k}$, so $2^ka_{2^{k+1}}\le\sum_{\ell=2^k+1}^{2^{k+1}}a_\ell\le2^ka_{2^k}$. Summing over $k=0,\dots,m$: $\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}2^ka_{2^k}\le\sum_{\ell=2}^{2^{m+1}}a_\ell\le\sum_{k=0}^m2^ka_{2^k}$. Both series have non-negative terms, so each converges iff its partial sums are bounded. If the condensed series converges, the right inequality bounds $s_{2^{m+1}}$, hence (monotonicity) all $s_n$. If $\sum a_\ell$ converges, the left inequality bounds the condensed partial sums. For $a_n=n^{-p}$, $p>0$, the condensed series is $\sum(2^{1-p})^k$, geometric, convergent iff $p>1$; for $p\le0$ the terms don't tend to $0$. ∎

4. Alternating series and the Leibniz criterion

Definitions (p. 300). $\sum a_n$ (real or complex terms) is absolutely convergent if $\sum|a_n|$ converges. It is conditionally convergent if it converges but not absolutely. For a sequence of positive numbers $a_n$, the series $\sum_{n=1}^\infty(-1)^{n+1}a_n=a_1-a_2+a_3-\dots$ is called alternating.

The idea. The ferryman rows out $a_1$, back $a_2$, out $a_3$, … If the strokes get shorter and shorter (decreasing) and shrink to nothing, his position can't escape: after every stroke out he's at a new “farthest point” that is closer in than the last one, and after every stroke back at a new “nearest point” that is farther out than the last one. The farthest points come down, the nearest points go up, and they squeeze together.

Proof E: the Leibniz criterion (Proposition 7.25, p. 303)

Leibniz criterion. Let $(a_n)_n$ be a decreasing sequence of positive numbers with $a_n\to0$. Then the alternating series $\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^{k+1}a_k$ converges. Moreover, for its value $S$ and every $\ell$:

$$\left|\sum_{k=1}^\ell(-1)^{k+1}a_k-S\right|\le a_{\ell+1}.$$

(The error after $\ell$ terms is at most the first omitted term.)

You'll need these (lessons 10 and 11)
  • Monotone convergence: increasing and bounded above ⇒ convergent; decreasing and bounded below ⇒ convergent.
  • Limit rules for differences, and “take the larger N” to combine two sequences.
What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{k+1}a_k$.” Last line: “so $s_n\to S$, i.e. the series converges.”
  • The given: $a_n>0$, $a_{n+1}\le a_n$, $a_n\to0$. All three must be used.
  • The gap: there's no candidate $S$. Find one by splitting into odd and even partial sums, which are monotone.
The storyWhat you write

Look at the ferryman only after strokes out (odd steps) or only after strokes back (even steps).

Let $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{k+1}a_k$. For all $n$:

$$\begin{aligned}&s_{2n+1}=s_{2n-1}-a_{2n}+a_{2n+1}\le s_{2n-1}\\&s_{2n+2}=s_{2n}+a_{2n+1}-a_{2n+2}\ge s_{2n}\end{aligned},$$

since $a_{2n+1}\le a_{2n}$ and $a_{2n+2}\le a_{2n+1}$. So $(s_{2n-1})_n$ is decreasing and $(s_{2n})_n$ is increasing.

He is always nearer after rowing back than after rowing out.

Also $s_{2n}=s_{2n-1}-a_{2n}\le s_{2n-1}$, since $a_{2n}>0$. Together with the monotonicity:

$$s_2\le s_{2n}\le s_{2n-1}\le s_1\quad\text{for all }n.$$

The farthest points come down but never below $s_2$; the nearest points go up but never above $s_1$. Both settle.

$(s_{2n})_n$ is increasing and bounded above by $s_1$, so it converges, say to $S$. $(s_{2n-1})_n$ is decreasing and bounded below by $s_2$, so it converges, say to $S'$.

The strokes shrink to nothing, so the two settling points are the same.

$$S'-S=\lim_{n}(s_{2n-1}-s_{2n})=\lim_{n}a_{2n}=0,$$so $S'=S$.

Whether you look after a stroke out or a stroke back, he's near $S$ late enough.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N_1$ with $|s_{2n}-S|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N_1$ and $N_2$ with $|s_{2n-1}-S|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N_2$, and let $N=2\max\{N_1,N_2\}$. Every $m\ge N$ is $2n$ or $2n-1$ with $n\ge\max\{N_1,N_2\}$, so $|s_m-S|<\varepsilon$. Hence $s_m\to S$, and the series converges. ∎

Moves used: even and odd separately monotone and bounded take the larger N go to partial sums

The proof in one piece

Let $s_n=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{k+1}a_k$. Since $(a_n)_n$ is decreasing, $s_{2n+1}=s_{2n-1}-(a_{2n}-a_{2n+1})\le s_{2n-1}$ and $s_{2n+2}=s_{2n}+(a_{2n+1}-a_{2n+2})\ge s_{2n}$. Since $a_{2n}>0$, $s_{2n}=s_{2n-1}-a_{2n}\le s_{2n-1}$. Hence $s_2\le s_{2n}\le s_{2n-1}\le s_1$ for all $n$. By monotone convergence, $s_{2n}\to S$ and $s_{2n-1}\to S'$, and $S'-S=\lim a_{2n}=0$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, take $N_1,N_2$ for the even and odd partial sums and $N=2\max\{N_1,N_2\}$; then $|s_m-S|<\varepsilon$ for all $m\ge N$. So the series converges to $S$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Odd partial sums decrease, even ones increase (one line each, using “decreasing”).
  2. Sandwich: $s_2\le s_{2n}\le s_{2n-1}\le s_1$ (using $a_n>0$).
  3. Monotone convergence: $s_{2n}\to S$, $s_{2n-1}\to S'$.
  4. Same limit: $S'-S=\lim a_{2n}=0$ (using $a_n\to0$).
  5. Glue: $N=2\max\{N_1,N_2\}$, so the whole sequence $s_m\to S$.

In short: “odd down, even up, squeezed between $s_2$ and $s_1$, the gap is $a_{2n}\to0$, glue”. Each of the three assumptions is used exactly once.

5. The Cauchy criterion and absolute convergence

Cauchy criterion for series (Satz 7.26, p. 304). $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k$ converges ⇔ for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ with

$$\left|\sum_{k=m}^na_k\right|<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }n\ge m\ge N.$$

Why: it is the Cauchy criterion from lesson 12 applied to the partial sums, since $s_n-s_{m-1}=\sum_{k=m}^na_k$. (For complex terms, lesson 12's Bonus gives the criterion in $\mathbb C$.)

In words: a series converges exactly when its tail pieces $a_m+\dots+a_n$ become small, however long they are. Its strength: it proves convergence without knowing the value.

Proof F: absolute convergence implies convergence (Proposition 7.28, p. 306)

Proposition 7.28. If $\sum|a_n|$ converges, then $\sum a_n$ converges, and $\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\right|\le\sum_{n=1}^\infty|a_n|$.

The idea. A tail piece of $\sum a_n$ is at most the matching tail piece of $\sum|a_n|$, by the triangle inequality. The latter become small (Cauchy for $\sum|a_n|$), so the former do too (Cauchy for $\sum a_n$).

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • No candidate for the value, so prove convergence with the Cauchy criterion: “Let $\varepsilon>0$ … so $\left|\sum_{k=m}^na_k\right|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge m\ge N$.”
  • The given: the Cauchy criterion for $\sum|a_k|$, with the same $\varepsilon$.
The storyWhat you write

Suppose the strokes, all counted as forward, add up to a finite distance.

Let $\sum|a_k|$ converge, and let $\varepsilon>0$. By the Cauchy criterion there is $N$ with $\sum_{k=m}^n|a_k|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge m\ge N$.

Then any stretch of the real journey, with its turnarounds, covers even less net distance.

By the triangle inequality, for $n\ge m\ge N$:

$$\left|\sum_{k=m}^na_k\right|\le\sum_{k=m}^n|a_k|<\varepsilon.$$

By the Cauchy criterion, $\sum a_k$ converges.

And the net distance is at most the total distance rowed.

For every $n$: $\left|\sum_{k=1}^na_k\right|\le\sum_{k=1}^n|a_k|\le\sum_{k=1}^\infty|a_k|$. Letting $n\to\infty$ (the absolute value is continuous, and limits keep “$\le$”) gives $\left|\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k\right|\le\sum_{k=1}^\infty|a_k|$. ∎

Moves used: Cauchy instead of a candidate insert a middle point

The proof in one piece

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $\sum|a_k|$ converges, the Cauchy criterion gives $N$ with $\sum_{k=m}^n|a_k|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge m\ge N$. Then $\left|\sum_{k=m}^na_k\right|\le\sum_{k=m}^n|a_k|<\varepsilon$, so $\sum a_k$ converges by the Cauchy criterion. Finally $\left|\sum_{k=1}^na_k\right|\le\sum_{k=1}^n|a_k|$ for all $n$, and $n\to\infty$ gives the inequality. ∎

(Proof C applied to $|a_n|\le b_n$, then Proof F.) This is how you handle series with signs or complex terms: check absolute convergence first.

6. The root test and the ratio test

The idea. Both tests ask: does the series eventually behave like a geometric series $\sum q^n$ with $q<1$? The root test looks at $\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}$ (“which $q$ is $|a_n|$ like $q^n$ for?”), the ratio test at $\frac{|a_{n+1}|}{|a_n|}$ (“by which factor do the terms shrink?”). If the answer is a number below $1$, a geometric series is a majorant. If it is above $1$, the terms don't even tend to $0$.

Proof G: the root test (Korollar 7.30, p. 307)

Root test. Let $\alpha=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}\in[0,\infty]$. Then

  • $\alpha<1$ ⇒ $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely;
  • $\alpha>1$ ⇒ $\sum a_n$ diverges (and $a_n\not\to0$).

If $\alpha=1$, the test says nothing (Beispiel 7.31): $\sqrt[n]{1/n}\to1$ and $\sqrt[n]{1/n^2}\to1$, but $\sum\frac1n$ diverges and $\sum\frac1{n^2}$ converges.

You'll need this (lesson 11)
  • limsup: $\limsup_{n\to\infty}x_n=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{k\ge n}x_k$, and $\sup_{k\ge n}x_k$ decreases in $n$.
What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • $\alpha<1$: find $q<1$ and $n_0$ with $|a_k|<q^k$ for all $k\ge n_0$, then use the geometric series as majorant.
  • $\alpha>1$: show $|a_k|>1$ for infinitely many $k$, so $a_k\not\to0$ (Proof A).
  • The gap: a number strictly between $\alpha$ and $1$, here $q=\frac{1+\alpha}2$.
The storyWhat you write

If, late enough, every overhang is smaller than the messenger's $q^k$ for some $q<1$, the tower is bounded by the messenger's road.

Let $\alpha<1$ and $q=\frac{1+\alpha}2$, so $\alpha<q<1$. Since $\sup_{k\ge n}\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}$ decreases to $\alpha<q$, there is $n_0$ with $\sup_{k\ge n_0}\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}<q$.

Then the overhangs are beaten by a geometric series.

So $\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}<q$, i.e. $|a_k|<q^k$, for all $k\ge n_0$. As $\sum q^k$ converges ($0<q<1$), the majorant test gives absolute convergence of $\sum a_k$.

If instead the overhangs keep exceeding a whole block length, the tower can't settle.

Let $\alpha>1$. For every $n$, $\sup_{k\ge n}\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}\ge\alpha>1$ (it decreases to $\alpha$), so there is $k\ge n$ with $\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}>1$, i.e. $|a_k|>1$. So $|a_k|>1$ for infinitely many $k$: $a_k\not\to0$, and the series diverges by Proof A. ∎

Moves used: find a majorant approach a supremum

The proof in one piece

Let $\alpha<1$ and $q=\frac{1+\alpha}2\in(\alpha,1)$. As $\sup_{k\ge n}\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}$ decreases to $\alpha$, there is $n_0$ with $\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}<q$, i.e. $|a_k|<q^k$, for $k\ge n_0$. Since $\sum q^k$ converges, $\sum a_k$ converges absolutely by the majorant test. Let $\alpha>1$. Then $\sup_{k\ge n}\sqrt[k]{|a_k|}\ge\alpha>1$ for every $n$, so for every $n$ some $k\ge n$ has $|a_k|>1$. Thus $a_k\not\to0$ and the series diverges. ∎

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why not take $q=\alpha$ itself?

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the $\alpha>1$ part.

    Answer

  3. Rung 3 (4 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Ratio test (Korollar 7.32, p. 307). Let $a_n\neq0$ and suppose $\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|a_{n+1}|}{|a_n|}$ exists. Then $\alpha<1$ ⇒ $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely, and $\alpha>1$ ⇒ $\sum a_n$ diverges. For $\alpha=1$ it says nothing (same examples). The proof is Exercise 6 (Wichtige Übung 7.33).

Which test when? A decision list.

  1. Do the terms tend to $0$? If not: divergent (Proof A). Done.
  2. Can you compute $s_n$? Geometric or telescoping: compute the value.
  3. Positive terms built from powers of $n$ (fractions, roots): limit comparison with $\sum\frac1{n^p}$.
  4. $n$-th powers like $\left(\frac n{2n+1}\right)^n$: root test. Factorials or $c^n$ like $\frac{n^2}{2^n}$, $\frac{3^n}{n!}$: ratio test.
  5. Signs that alternate: first try absolute convergence (steps 3–4 on $|a_n|$). If that fails, try Leibniz.

7. Rearranging series

Can you add the terms of a series in a different order? For finite sums, always. For series, it depends:

  • Absolutely convergent: yes (Satz 7.35, p. 308). Every rearrangement $\sum a_{\varphi(n)}$ ($\varphi:\mathbb N\to\mathbb N$ a bijection) converges absolutely, with the same value.
  • Conditionally convergent: no (Riemann's rearrangement theorem, p. 301). For a conditionally convergent real series and any $A\in\mathbb R$, some rearrangement converges to $A$, and another one diverges.

No contradiction: the value was defined as the limit of the partial sums, and these depend on the order. That's another reason to prefer absolute convergence: it behaves like a finite sum.

Worked example 1: $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{2^n+3^n}{6^n}$

  1. Split the term into two geometric terms.

    Answer

    $\frac{2^n+3^n}{6^n}=\left(\frac13\right)^n+\left(\frac12\right)^n$.

  2. Compute the two values (the index starts at $n=1$).

    Answer

    $\sum_{n=1}^\infty q^n=\frac q{1-q}$: $\frac{1/3}{2/3}=\frac12$ and $\frac{1/2}{1/2}=1$.

  3. Why may you add them?

    Both series converge, so linearity applies: the value is $\frac12+1=\frac32$.

Worked example 2: for which $\alpha>0$ does $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{n^\alpha}{n^3+1}$ converge?

  1. Which power of $n$ does the term behave like?

    Answer

  2. Check this with limit comparison.

    Answer

    $\frac{n^\alpha/(n^3+1)}{1/n^{3-\alpha}}=\frac{n^3}{n^3+1}\to1\in(0,\infty)$. So the series converges ⇔ $\sum\frac1{n^{3-\alpha}}$ converges.

  3. Use the $p$-test.

    Answer

Worked example 3: $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{n^2}{2^n}$

  1. Which test fits, and why?

    Answer

    A power $2^n$ in the denominator: the ratio test.

  2. Compute the ratio and its limit.

    Answer

    $$\begin{aligned}\frac{(n+1)^2/2^{n+1}}{n^2/2^n}&=\frac12\left(1+\frac1n\right)^2\to\frac12\\&<1\end{aligned}.$$The series converges (absolutely).

Worked example 4: $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt n}$

  1. Absolute convergence?

    Answer

    $\sum\frac1{\sqrt n}=\sum\frac1{n^{1/2}}$ diverges ($p=\frac12\le1$). Not absolutely convergent.

  2. Convergence?

    Answer

    $\frac1{\sqrt n}$ is positive, decreasing, $\to0$. By Leibniz, $\sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{\sqrt n}$ converges, and our series is $-1$ times it, so it converges (linearity). It is conditionally convergent.

Why it's cool. Adding infinitely many numbers can give a finite answer ($\sum\frac1{2^k}=1$), an infinite one (the harmonic series), or, for a conditionally convergent series, any answer you like, just by changing the order of the terms (section 7).

Exercise 1 (Einsiedler HS21, question 4, 4 points)

Open the original: HS21 exam · solutions

Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{4k^2-1}=\frac12$.

Solution

Frame: a value is asked, so: partial fractions, the partial sum $s_n$, cancel, limit. Moves: telescoping sum go to partial sums.

Partial fractions (1 point). $4k^2-1=(2k-1)(2k+1)$, and

$$\frac1{4k^2-1}=\frac12\left(\frac1{2k-1}-\frac1{2k+1}\right).$$

(Check: $$\begin{aligned}\frac1{2k-1}-\frac1{2k+1}&=\frac{(2k+1)-(2k-1)}{(2k-1)(2k+1)}\\&=\frac2{4k^2-1}\end{aligned}.$$)

Partial sums (1 point). The sum telescopes:

$$\begin{aligned}s_n&=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1{4k^2-1}\\&=\frac12\left[\left(1-\frac13\right)+\left(\frac13-\frac15\right)+\dots+\left(\frac1{2n-1}-\frac1{2n+1}\right)\right]\\&=\frac12\left(1-\frac1{2n+1}\right)\end{aligned}.$$

Limit. $\frac1{2n+1}\to0$, so $s_n\to\frac12$. By definition of the value of a series, $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{4k^2-1}=\frac12$. ∎

Exercise 2 (Figalli FS24, MC2)

Open the original: FS24 exam with solutions

For which $\alpha\in\mathbb R$ does $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\sqrt{\frac{n\log n}{n^3+n^{2\alpha}}}$ converge? (A) $\alpha>\frac32$. (B) $\alpha\in(1,2)$. (C) $\alpha<3$. (D) $\alpha\in(0,2)$.

Solution

Answer (A). Frame: positive terms, so compare with $\sum\frac1{n^p}$. The denominator is dominated by $n^3$ if $2\alpha\le3$, and by $n^{2\alpha}$ if $2\alpha>3$.

  • $2\alpha\le3$: $n^3+n^{2\alpha}\le2n^3$, so for $n\ge3$ (where $\log n\ge1$) the term is $\ge\sqrt{\frac{n}{2n^3}}=\frac1{\sqrt2\,n}$. A divergent minorant (harmonic): the series diverges.
  • $2\alpha>3$: $n^3+n^{2\alpha}\ge n^{2\alpha}$, so the term is $\le\sqrt{\log n}\;n^{\frac{1-2\alpha}2}$. Write $\frac{2\alpha-1}2=1+2\eta$ with $\eta>0$. Since $\log n\le n^{2\eta}$ for large $n$ (logarithms grow slower than every positive power; lesson 16), the term is $\le\frac{n^\eta}{n^{1+2\eta}}=\frac1{n^{1+\eta}}$, a convergent majorant ($p=1+\eta>1$).

So it converges exactly for $\alpha>\frac32$.

Solution

Answer (C). $\sqrt{k(k+1)}\le\sqrt{(k+1)^2}=k+1$, so $\frac1{\sqrt{k(k+1)}}\ge\frac1{k+1}$, and $\sum\frac1{k+1}$ diverges (harmonic, shifted). The terms are positive, so the partial sums increase to $\infty$.

  • (A) Converges by Leibniz: $\frac1{\log k}$ is positive, decreasing and $\to0$.
  • (B) Diverges, but not to $\infty$: the partial sums are $-1,0,-1,0,\dots$
  • (D) Converges: it is Worked example 3 (ratio test, ratio $\to\frac12$).

Exercise 4 (Felder HS22, 3.MC4)

Open the original: HS22 exam

For each $n\in\mathbb N$ let $s_n=\sum_{j=1}^n\frac1j$. Which statement is correct? (A) $(s_n)_n$ is monotone and bounded, so convergent. (B) $\left(\frac1{s_n}\right)_n$ converges to $0$. (C) $\left(\frac1j\right)_j$ is a null sequence, so the series $\sum_{j=1}^\infty\frac1j=\lim s_n$ converges. (D) $(s_n)_n$ is a Cauchy sequence.

Solution

Answer (B). By Proof B, $s_n\to\infty$, so $\frac1{s_n}\to0$: given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $s_n>\frac1\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$; then $0<\frac1{s_n}<\varepsilon$.

  • (A) False: monotone, but not bounded (Proof B).
  • (C) False: the classic mistake. Terms tending to $0$ is necessary, not sufficient (Proof A).
  • (D) False: a Cauchy sequence converges (lesson 12), and $(s_n)_n$ doesn't.

Exercise 5 (Felder FS23, 7 points)

Open the original: FS23 exam with solutions

(a) Let $(a_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}$ be a decreasing sequence of real numbers converging to $0$. Prove that $\sum_{n=1}^\infty(-1)^na_n$ converges. (b) Show that the statement is false without “decreasing”.

Solution

(a) Frame: this is Proof E with the sign flipped. First note $a_n\ge0$: a decreasing sequence with limit $0$ satisfies $a_n\ge\lim a_m=0$. If some $a_n=0$, all later terms are $0$ and the series is a finite sum. Otherwise $a_n>0$ for all $n$, and Proof E gives the convergence of $\sum_{n=1}^\infty(-1)^{n+1}a_n$. Since $(-1)^na_n=-(-1)^{n+1}a_n$, linearity gives the convergence of $\sum(-1)^na_n$. In the exam, write out Proof E's five steps (from the exam box) for full points. ∎

(b) Frame: a counterexample: positive terms tending to $0$, not decreasing, with a divergent alternating series. Take $a_{2k}=\frac1k$ and $a_{2k-1}=\frac1{k^2}$. Then $a_n>0$ and $a_n\to0$, but the partial sums of $\sum(-1)^na_n$ are

$$\begin{aligned}s_{2m}&=\sum_{k=1}^m\left(a_{2k}-a_{2k-1}\right)\\&=\sum_{k=1}^m\left(\frac1k-\frac1{k^2}\right)\end{aligned}.$$

Since $\sum\frac1{k^2}$ converges and $\sum\frac1k$ diverges to $\infty$, $s_{2m}\to\infty$, so the series diverges. (The official solution uses the even simpler $a_n=\frac{(-1)^n}n$, which tends to $0$ but is not decreasing: then $\sum(-1)^na_n=\sum\frac1n$ diverges.) Moves: a small counterexample.

Exercise 6 (the ratio test, Wichtige Übung 7.33)

Prove the ratio test: if $a_n\neq0$ and $\frac{|a_{n+1}|}{|a_n|}\to\alpha<1$, then $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely; if $\alpha>1$, it diverges.

Solution

Frame: like Proof G: room $q=\frac{1+\alpha}2$, then a geometric majorant. Moves: find a majorant.

$\alpha<1$. Let $q=\frac{1+\alpha}2\in(\alpha,1)$. With $\varepsilon=q-\alpha>0$ there is $N$ with $\frac{|a_{n+1}|}{|a_n|}<q$ for $n\ge N$. By induction, $|a_n|\le|a_N|\,q^{n-N}=C\,q^n$ for $n\ge N$, with $C=|a_N|q^{-N}$. Since $\sum Cq^n$ converges, the majorant test gives absolute convergence.

$\alpha>1$. There is $N$ with $\frac{|a_{n+1}|}{|a_n|}>1$ for $n\ge N$, so $|a_n|\ge|a_N|>0$ for all $n\ge N$. Hence $a_n\not\to0$, and the series diverges (Proof A). ∎

Exercise 7 (convergence drill)

Converge or diverge? (a) $\displaystyle\sum\left(\frac n{2n+1}\right)^n$. (b) $\displaystyle\sum\frac1{\sqrt{n^3+n}}$. (c) $\displaystyle\sum\frac{n+1}{n^2}$. (d) $\displaystyle\sum\frac{3^n}{n!}$. (e) $\displaystyle\sum(-1)^n\frac n{n+1}$.

Solution

(a) Converges. Root test: $\sqrt[n]{a_n}=\frac n{2n+1}\to\frac12<1$.

(b) Converges. Limit comparison with $\frac1{n^{3/2}}$: $\frac{1/\sqrt{n^3+n}}{1/n^{3/2}}=\sqrt{\frac{n^3}{n^3+n}}\to1$, and $p=\frac32>1$.

(c) Diverges. $\frac{n+1}{n^2}\ge\frac n{n^2}=\frac1n$: a divergent minorant.

(d) Converges. Ratio test: $\frac{3^{n+1}/(n+1)!}{3^n/n!}=\frac3{n+1}\to0<1$.

(e) Diverges. $\left|(-1)^n\frac n{n+1}\right|=\frac n{n+1}\to1$, so the terms don't tend to $0$ (Proof A). Leibniz doesn't apply: $\frac n{n+1}$ doesn't tend to $0$.

Bonus (Übung 7.18 (i))

Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^\infty\frac1{n(\log n)^p}$ converges if and only if $p>1$. (Use the logarithm from school: $\log(2^k)=k\log2$, and $\log$ is increasing. Lesson 16 makes it rigorous.)

Solution

Frame: decreasing positive terms, so condensation (Proof D). Moves: group into blocks.

For $p\le0$, $\frac1{n(\log n)^p}\ge\frac1n$ for $n\ge3$ (where $\log n\ge1$), so the series diverges by comparison. For $p>0$, $n(\log n)^p$ is increasing, so the terms are positive and decreasing (for $n\ge2$; condensation works the same with the index starting at $2$). The condensed series is

$$\sum_{k=1}^\infty2^k\cdot\frac1{2^k(\log2^k)^p}=\frac1{(\log2)^p}\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k^p},$$

which converges ⇔ $p>1$ by the $p$-test. ∎ (So $\sum\frac1{n\log n}$ diverges, even more slowly than the harmonic series.)

10. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

The tower is real. With $n$ identical blocks of length $2$ m, the stack in the story reaches $1+\frac12+\frac13+\dots+\frac1n$ metres over the water (Beispiel 7.5, p. 294–296). It goes arbitrarily far, but slowly: to reach $10$ m you need $12\,367$ blocks. The partial sums of the harmonic series grow like $\log n$, very slowly (and the Bonus series grows even more slowly).

The same terms, any total you like. Rearrange the alternating harmonic series as “one positive term, two negative terms”: $\left(1-\frac12\right)-\frac14+\left(\frac13-\frac16\right)-\frac18+\dots$ Each bracket is half of a term of the original series, and the total becomes exactly half: $\frac12\log2$ instead of $\log2$ (Beispiel 7.22). Riemann proved you can reach any number this way: take positive terms until you pass the target, then negative terms until you drop below it, and repeat. The overshoots shrink because the terms tend to $0$.

The Basel problem. What is $\sum\frac1{n^2}$? The best mathematicians of the 17th century failed. In 1734 the 28-year-old Euler found $\frac{\pi^2}6$. Why should $\pi$, a circle number, appear in a sum of squares? Answers to that question led to whole new fields of mathematics.
