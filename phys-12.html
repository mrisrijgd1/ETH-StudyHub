Why it matters for the exam

Every exam has a multiple-choice block “Schwingungen” (three to six points), and five of the last six exams have a long problem that ends in an oscillation: the string pendulum with a collision (FS24, 10 points), the rod pendulum (FS23, 22 points), the ring pendulum (HS23), the swing (FS26), the halfpipe (FS25). The long problems always ask the same thing: set up the equation of motion, use the small-angle approximation, read off $\omega$, solve with the initial conditions. That is the recipe in section 5.

Recap of lesson 11 (3 min)

  1. What is the moment of inertia of a thin rod of mass $m$ and length $L$ about one of its ends?

    Answer

    $\frac1{12}mL^2+m\left(\frac L2\right)^2=\frac13mL^2$, Steiner from the centre of mass.

  2. What is the equation of motion for rotation about a fixed axis?

    Answer

    $M=J_A\ddot\varphi$, with $M$ the torque component along the axis. Today it gives every pendulum.

  3. Newton's law for a mass on a spring (lesson 3; script, section 3.9.3) gives $m\ddot x=-kx$. What is its solution?

    Answer

    $x(t)=A\sin(\omega t+\delta)$ with $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$. Today we see why almost everything near a resting position moves like this.

1. The story: the clock tower

The castle clock has stopped, and the clockmaker climbs the tower with two apprentices. The clock's heart is a long brass rod swinging behind the face. “Its swing must take exactly two seconds,” she says, “whether it swings wide or narrow, warm day or cold.” One apprentice pushes the rod hard, the other barely touches it: both swings take the same time. Then she lowers the brass weight a little on the rod, and the clock runs slower.

In the cellar a cart rocks between two springs, and someone has left it standing in the oil barrel. It sways, slower than before, and each sway is smaller than the last. When the oil is thick enough, it doesn't sway at all: it just creeps back to the middle.

In physics: any system pulled back to a resting position by a force proportional to the displacement oscillates harmonically, with a period that doesn't depend on how far it swings. The period is set by the inertia (mass, moment of inertia) against the stiffness (spring constant, gravity times lever). Friction proportional to the speed (the oil) makes the swings die out and the oscillation a little slower; strong enough friction turns the oscillation into a creep.

2. Why everything oscillates harmonically

Take any system with a stable equilibrium at $r_0$: a potential energy $U(r)$ with a minimum there (section 9.2.2, pages 224–226; the script's example is the two atoms of a molecule). Expand $U$ in a Taylor series around $r_0$ and write $x=\Delta r$:

$$U(r_0+x)\approx U(r_0)+\underbrace{U'(r_0)}_{=0}\,x+\tfrac12\underbrace{U''(r_0)}_{=:k}\,x^2.$$

The first derivative vanishes at a minimum. So near the minimum every potential is a parabola, and the force is $F=-\frac{dU}{dx}=-kx$: Hooke's law, whatever the system. Newton gives $m\ddot x=-kx$, and with $\omega_0:=\sqrt{k/m}$:

$$\ddot x+\omega_0^2x=0.$$

This is the equation of the harmonic oscillator. It is a linear homogeneous ODE with constant coefficients, the kind you solved in Analysis: the characteristic equation $\lambda^2+\omega_0^2=0$ gives $\lambda=\pm i\omega_0$, so $x=C_1e^{i\omega_0t}+C_2e^{-i\omega_0t}$. For $x$ to be real, $C_2=\overline{C_1}$, and then (script, pages 226–227)

$$x(t)=A\sin(\omega_0t+\delta)\qquad\text{or equally}\qquad x(t)=B\sin\omega_0t+C\cos\omega_0t.$$

Two free constants, as a second-order equation needs: the amplitude $A$ and the phase constant $\delta$ (or $B=A\cos\delta$ and $C=A\sin\delta$). The script uses the sine form; a cosine is the same thing with $\delta$ shifted by $\frac\pi2$.

Why it's cool

Because every smooth minimum looks like a parabola up close, this one equation describes a guitar string, a swinging bridge, the atoms in a crystal, the electrons in an antenna and the air in a flute. Physicists joke that their whole subject is the harmonic oscillator, solved again and again in different clothes.

3. Period, velocity, acceleration

The sine repeats when its argument grows by $2\pi$, so (page 230)

$$T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega},\qquad f=\frac1T=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\quad[\mathrm{Hz}=\mathrm{s^{-1}}].$$

$\omega$ depends only on the system ($k$ and $m$), not on $A$: wide or narrow swings take the same time. That is why pendulum clocks work. Differentiate (pages 231–233):

$$x=A\sin(\omega t+\delta),\qquad v=A\omega\cos(\omega t+\delta),\qquad a=-A\omega^2\sin(\omega t+\delta)=-\omega^2x.$$
  • At the turning points ($x=\pm A$): $v=0$, and $|a|=A\omega^2$ is largest, pointing back to the middle.
  • At the middle ($x=0$): $|v|=A\omega$ is largest and $a=0$.
  • The velocity runs a quarter period ahead of the displacement, the acceleration half a period: it is always opposite to $x$.

Initial conditions (page 235). With $x(0)=x_0$ and $v(0)=v_0$: $x_0=A\sin\delta$ and $v_0=A\omega\cos\delta$, so

$$A=\sqrt{x_0^2+\frac{v_0^2}{\omega^2}},\qquad\tan\delta=\frac{x_0\,\omega}{v_0}.$$

The two most common cases need no formula: released from rest at $x_0$ means $x=x_0\cos\omega t$; pushed through the middle with $v_0$ means $x=\frac{v_0}\omega\sin\omega t$.

Try it: the spring oscillator

A block hangs between springs. Choose the spring constant and the mass and press Go. The curves show $x$, $v$ and $a$ over 6 seconds, each scaled to the same height; the bars show how the energy is shared.

Four times the mass halves $\omega$ and doubles $T$; four times the spring constant does the opposite. Watch the energy bars: they swap twice per period.

Worked example 1: fitting the initial conditions

A block of mass $m=0.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ on a spring with $k=50\,\mathrm{N/m}$ is pushed $x_0=3\,\mathrm{cm}$ to the right and given $v_0=-0.4\,\mathrm{m/s}$ (to the left). Find $x(t)$.

  1. What is $\omega$?

    Answer

    $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}=\sqrt{100}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}=10\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $T=\frac{2\pi}{10}\,\mathrm s\approx0.63\,\mathrm s$.

  2. What is the amplitude?

    Answer

    $A=\sqrt{x_0^2+v_0^2/\omega^2}$ $=\sqrt{0.03^2+0.04^2}\,\mathrm m=0.05\,\mathrm m$.

  3. Which $\delta$? Careful with the quadrant.

    Answer

    $\sin\delta=\frac{x_0}A=0.6>0$ and $\cos\delta=\frac{v_0}{A\omega}=-0.8<0$: $\delta$ lies in the second quadrant, $\delta=\pi-\arcsin0.6\approx2.50$. The formula $\tan\delta=\frac{x_0\omega}{v_0}=-0.75$ alone would give $-0.64$, the wrong quadrant: always check the signs of $\sin\delta$ and $\cos\delta$.

Result: $x(t)=0.05\,\mathrm m\cdot\sin(10\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\,t+2.50)$.

4. Energy of the oscillator

The script finds the energy straight from the equation (pages 235–236): multiply $\ddot x+\omega^2x=0$ by $m\dot x$ and notice that both terms are time derivatives:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12m\dot x^2+\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2\right)=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad E_{\text{tot}}=E_{\text{kin}}+E_{\text{pot}}=\tfrac12m\omega^2A^2=\tfrac12kA^2.$$

Insert the solution: $E_{\text{kin}}=E_{\text{tot}}\cos^2(\omega t+\delta)$ and $E_{\text{pot}}=E_{\text{tot}}\sin^2(\omega t+\delta)$. Both oscillate with twice the frequency of $x$ (since $\cos^2u=\frac12(1+\cos2u)$), and over a period each averages half the total (page 237):

$$\langle E_{\text{kin}}\rangle=\langle E_{\text{pot}}\rangle=\tfrac12E_{\text{tot}}.$$

At every instant they are not equal; only their averages are. The energy grows with the square of the amplitude: double $A$, four times $E$.

5. Pendulums and the recipe

The exams turn almost every oscillation problem into one of three systems. The script has no section on the string pendulum; the formula sheet gives its $\omega=\sqrt{g/l}$, and the exams ask you to derive it, so here it is.

Springs. A mass between two springs (one on each side) or on two springs side by side feels both forces: $k=k_1+k_2$. Two springs one after the other (in series) are softer: $\frac1k=\frac1{k_1}+\frac1{k_2}$. A mass hanging on a vertical spring oscillates with the same $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$ as a horizontal one: gravity only shifts the equilibrium down by $\frac{mg}k$.

The string pendulum (mass $m$ on a massless string of length $l$). Torque about the pivot, with $\varphi$ measured from the vertical: $ml^2\ddot\varphi=-mgl\sin\varphi$. For small angles $\sin\varphi\approx\varphi$:

$$\ddot\varphi+\frac gl\,\varphi=0,\qquad\omega=\sqrt{\frac gl}.$$

The mass cancels: a heavy and a light ball on equal strings swing together. On the Moon ($g$ six times smaller) the same pendulum is slower.

The physical pendulum: a rigid body hanging on an axis through P, its centre of mass S at distance $d$ below P. Gravity acts at S with lever arm $d\sin\varphi$, and $M=J_P\ddot\varphi$ from lesson 11 gives

$$J_P\ddot\varphi=-mgd\sin\varphi\approx-mgd\,\varphi,\qquad\omega=\sqrt{\frac{mgd}{J_P}},\qquad J_P=J_S+md^2.$$

It swings like a string pendulum of the equivalent length $l=\frac{J_P}{md}$. For a point mass $J_P=md^2$ and $l=d$, as it must be.

Recipe: set up and solve an oscillation

  1. Choose the coordinate ($x$ for a spring, $\varphi$ for anything that swings) and its zero at the equilibrium.
  2. Write Newton ($m\ddot x=\sum F$) or the torque equation about the fixed axis ($J_P\ddot\varphi=\sum M$). The restoring term gets a minus sign.
  3. Small angles: $\sin\varphi\approx\varphi$ (and $\cos\varphi\approx1$). Bring it into the form $\ddot\varphi+\omega^2\varphi=0$ and read off $\omega^2$ as “everything in front of $\varphi$”.
  4. Write $\varphi(t)=A\sin(\omega t+\delta)$, fit $A$ and $\delta$ to the initial conditions, and give $T=\frac{2\pi}\omega$.
  5. Check: units of $\omega^2$ are $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$; a heavier mass on a spring or a longer pendulum should get slower, a stiffer spring faster.

Try it: the rod pendulum

A rod of length $L=1\,\mathrm m$ hangs on an axis at distance $d$ above its centre of mass. Move the axis and press Go. The curve is the period $T(d)$.

As $d\to0$ the period grows without bound: with the axis through S, gravity has no lever. The fastest swing is at $d=\frac L{\sqrt{12}}\approx0.29\,\mathrm m$, where $d$ equals the rod's radius of gyration.

Worked example 2: a hoop on a nail

A thin hoop of mass $m$ and radius $R$ hangs on a nail through its rim. Find the period of small oscillations in its own plane.

  1. What is $J_P$ about the nail?

    Answer

    $J_S=mR^2$ for a hoop, the nail is at distance $d=R$ from the centre: $J_P=mR^2+mR^2=2mR^2$.

  2. Set up the torque equation for small angles.

    Answer

    $2mR^2\ddot\varphi=-mgR\sin\varphi\approx-mgR\varphi$, so $\ddot\varphi+\frac g{2R}\varphi=0$.

  3. What are $\omega$, $T$ and the equivalent length?

    Answer

    $\omega=\sqrt{\frac g{2R}}$, $T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2R}g}$, and $l=\frac{J_P}{md}=2R$: the hoop swings exactly like a string pendulum as long as its diameter, whatever its mass.

6. Damping

Add a friction force proportional to the velocity, $F_R=-\kappa\dot x$ (a body in oil or water; section 9.3.1, page 238). With $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$ and the damping constant $\rho:=\frac\kappa{2m}$:

$$\ddot x+2\rho\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0,\qquad\lambda^2+2\rho\lambda+\omega_0^2=0,\qquad\lambda_{1,2}=-\rho\pm\sqrt{\rho^2-\omega_0^2}.$$

The sign under the root gives three cases (pages 238–241):

CaseSolutionBehaviour
weak damping, $\rho<\omega_0$$x=Ae^{-\rho t}\sin(\omega t+\delta)$, $\omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\rho^2}$oscillates with a shrinking amplitude, and more slowly than without damping: $\omega<\omega_0$, $T>T_0$
critical damping, $\rho=\omega_0$$x=(C_1+C_2t)e^{-\rho t}$no oscillation (it crosses zero at most once); the fastest return to rest
strong damping, $\rho>\omega_0$$x=C_1e^{\lambda_1t}+C_2e^{\lambda_2t}$, both $\lambda<0$creeps back to rest (Kriechfall), slower than critical

In the critical case the characteristic equation has a double root $\lambda=-\rho$, so the second solution is $te^{-\rho t}$, exactly as in Analysis.

Energy (section 9.3.3, pages 244–245). Multiply by $m\dot x$ again: $\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12m\dot x^2+\frac12m\omega_0^2x^2\right)=-2\rho m\dot x^2=P_R$, the power lost to friction. For weak damping ($\rho\ll\frac1T$) average over a period: $\langle\dot x^2\rangle$ gives $P_R=-2\rho E$, so

$$E(t)=E_0e^{-t/\tau},\qquad\tau=\frac1{2\rho},\qquad Q=2\pi\frac{\tau}{T}.$$

The energy decays twice as fast as the amplitude ($E\propto A^2$). The quality factor $Q$ is $2\pi$ times the energy stored over the energy lost per period: a guitar string has $Q\sim10^3$, a quartz crystal in a watch $10^5$. Small damping, large $Q$.

Try it: three kinds of damping

The block is released from rest at $x_0=1$. Choose $\rho$ in units of $\omega_0$.

Slide to $1$: the curve stops oscillating and comes back fastest. Beyond $1$ it gets slower again. Near $0$, compare the zero crossings with the faint undamped curve: the damped ones come later.

Worked example 3: how long does the bell ring?

A bell oscillates at $f=500\,\mathrm{Hz}$ with damping constant $\rho=0.5\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. After what time has its energy dropped to $\frac1e$? What is its $Q$?

  1. Is the damping weak?

    Answer

    $\omega_0\approx2\pi\cdot500\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\approx3100\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\gg\rho$: yes, and $\omega\approx\omega_0$.

  2. Energy time and $Q$?

    Answer

    $\tau=\frac1{2\rho}=1\,\mathrm s$. $Q=2\pi\frac\tau T=2\pi f\tau\approx3100$. The amplitude needs $\frac1\rho=2\,\mathrm s$ to drop to $\frac1e$.

7. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed. Exercises 1–10 are multiple choice; 11–15 are parts of long problems.

Exercise 1 (FS23, Konzeptfrage 1.5(a), 2 points, original)

Masses A and B hang on two springs. A oscillates with twice the period of B. Which is correct? (A) If the springs are equal, B is twice as heavy as A. (B) If the springs are equal, B is half as heavy as A. (C) If the masses are equal, A's spring constant is four times smaller than B's. (D) If the masses are equal, A's spring constant is four times larger than B's.

Solution

C. $T=2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$, so $T_A=2T_B$ means $\frac{m_A}{k_A}=4\frac{m_B}{k_B}$. Equal springs: $m_A=4m_B$ (neither A nor B). Equal masses: $k_A=\frac14k_B$.

Exercise 2 (FS23, Konzeptfrage 1.5(b), 2 points, original)

Now springs and masses are equal, but A oscillates in water (friction proportional to the velocity), B in vacuum. (A) A's frequency is larger. (B) A's frequency is smaller. (C) Both are equal. (D) Not enough information.

Solution

B. Weak damping: $\omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\rho^2}<\omega_0$. (If the water damped critically or more, A would not oscillate at all, which still isn't a larger frequency.)

Exercise 3 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.4(a), 1 point, original)

A mass $m$ sits between two springs, $k_1$ on its left and $k_2$ on its right, and oscillates along $x$. What is $\omega_0$? (A) $\sqrt{\frac{k_1+k_2}m}$ (B) $\sqrt{\frac{|k_1-k_2|}m}$ (C) $\sqrt{\frac{k_1k_2}m}$ (D) $\sqrt{\frac{k_1k_2}{2m}}$

Solution

A. Displaced by $x$, one spring pushes back with $k_1x$ and the other pulls back with $k_2x$, both towards the middle: $m\ddot x=-(k_1+k_2)x$. (C) and (D) don't even have the right units.

Exercise 4 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.4(b), 1 point, original)

A mass $m$ on a spring $k$ oscillates with $\omega$. A second identical spring is added in parallel. What changes? (A) The restoring force and the frequency double. (B) $\omega_{\text{neu}}=2\sqrt{k/m}$. (C) At the same amplitude the total energy doubles. (D) The frequency gets smaller because the mass is held more strongly.

Solution

C. In parallel $k'=2k$, so $\omega'=\sqrt2\,\omega$ (not doubled: A and B are wrong) and $E=\frac12k'A^2$ doubles at the same $A$.

Exercise 5 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.4(d), 1 point, original)

A string pendulum (massless string, metal ball) swings with frequency $f$ in a vacuum chamber on Earth. How does $f$ change on the Moon? (A) $f$ gets smaller. (B) $f$ stays the same. (C) $f$ gets larger. (D) No statement possible, because the ball's mass is different on the Moon.

Solution

A. $f=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{g/l}$ and $g_{\text{Moon}}<g_{\text{Earth}}$. The mass doesn't enter (and it doesn't change on the Moon anyway; the weight does).

Exercise 6 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.4(a), 1 point, original)

Which statement about the energy of a harmonic oscillator is correct? (A) The kinetic energy equals the potential energy. (B) The kinetic energy oscillates with twice the frequency of the oscillator. (C) Doubling the amplitude doubles the mean potential energy. (D) The mean total energy depends on the phase of the oscillator.

Solution

B. $E_{\text{kin}}=E_{\text{tot}}\cos^2(\omega t+\delta)$ $=\frac12E_{\text{tot}}(1+\cos(2\omega t+2\delta))$. (A) holds only for the averages, (C) gives four times, (D) is wrong since $E_{\text{tot}}=\frac12m\omega^2A^2$.

Exercise 7 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.4(b), 1 point, original)

An oscillator has natural frequency $\omega_0$ and damping constant $\rho$. When is its quality factor largest? (A) $\rho=\omega_0/2$ (B) $\rho=\omega_0$ (C) $\rho=\sqrt2\,\omega_0$ (D) $\rho=2\omega_0$

Solution

A. $Q=2\pi\frac\tau T$ with $\tau=\frac1{2\rho}$ grows as $\rho$ shrinks; take the smallest $\rho$. The other three are critical or strong damping, where the system doesn't oscillate at all.

Exercise 8 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.4(c), 1 point, original)

An undamped pendulum has natural frequency $\omega_0$. What holds when it is damped? (A) With critical damping the initial amplitude is smaller than without damping. (B) The period of a weakly damped pendulum is longer than without damping. (C) … shorter than without damping. (D) Damping doesn't change the oscillation.

Solution

B. $\omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\rho^2}<\omega_0$, so $T>T_0$. (A) is wrong: the initial amplitude is set by the initial conditions, not by the damping.

Exercise 9 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.5(b), 1 point, original)

What is the exact solution of $\ddot x+2\rho\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0$ for critical damping ($\rho=\omega_0$) with $x(0)=0$ and $\dot x(0)=v_0$? (A) $x=v_0te^{-\rho t}$ (B) $x=v_0t\sin(\omega_0t)e^{-\rho t}$ (C) $x=v_0t\sin\left(\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\rho^2}\,t\right)e^{-\rho t}$ (D) $x=v_0t\,\cdot$ $\left(\frac12e^{(-\rho+\sqrt{\rho^2-\omega_0^2})t}\right.$ $\left.+\frac12e^{(-\rho-\sqrt{\rho^2-\omega_0^2})t}\right)$

Solution

A. $x=(C_1+C_2t)e^{-\rho t}$; $x(0)=C_1=0$; $\dot x(0)=C_2=v_0$. (B) does not solve the equation. (C) is identically zero at $\rho=\omega_0$. (D) happens to reduce to the same function at $\rho=\omega_0$, but it is written in the form of the strong-damping case; the answer meant is (A).

Exercise 10 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.4(c), 1 point, original)

An oscillator with natural frequency $\omega_0$ and damping $\rho$ starts at $x(0)=x_0>0$ with $v(0)=0$. Which $\rho$ makes it reach $x(t_1)=0$ at the smallest $t_1>0$? (A) $\rho=0$ (B) $0<\rho<\omega_0$ (C) $\rho=\omega_0$ (D) $\rho>\omega_0$

Solution

A. With critical or strong damping, starting from rest, $x$ never reaches $0$ (it only creeps towards it). With weak damping it crosses $0$, but later than the undamped $t_1=\frac T4=\frac\pi{2\omega_0}$: friction slows it down. The widget above shows it.

Exercise 11 (FS24, problem 4, “Pendel”, 10 points, original)

An ideal string pendulum of length $l$ and mass $m$ can be deflected by the angle $\varphi$. (a) Show that for small angles $\ddot\varphi+\omega^2\varphi=0$ with $\omega=\sqrt{g/l}$. (b) It is released at $t=0$ from $-\varphi_i$. Find $\varphi(t)$ and $\dot\varphi(t)$. (c) Sketch both for $0<\omega t<5\pi$. (d) What is the horizontal speed $|v_x|$ at $\varphi=0$? (e) What are the energy $E$ and the momentum $P$ at that moment, with the direction of $P$? A second identical pendulum is hung so that the two balls touch at rest; pendulum 1 is released from $-\varphi_i$ and hits pendulum 2 elastically at $\varphi=0$. (f) Use momentum and energy conservation to find both velocities right after the collision. (g) Sketch $\varphi_1(t)$ for $0<\omega t<5\pi$.

Solution

(a) Torque about the pivot: $ml^2\ddot\varphi=-mgl\sin\varphi\approx-mgl\varphi$, so $\ddot\varphi+\frac gl\varphi=0$.

(b) Released from rest: $\varphi(t)=-\varphi_i\cos\omega t$, $\dot\varphi(t)=\omega\varphi_i\sin\omega t$.

(c) $\varphi$ starts at $-\varphi_i$, crosses $0$ at $\omega t=\frac\pi2$, reaches $+\varphi_i$ at $\pi$, and so on: a cosine flipped upside down. $\dot\varphi$ is a sine of amplitude $\omega\varphi_i$, largest where $\varphi=0$.

(d) At $\varphi=0$ the string is vertical, so the velocity is horizontal: $|v_x|=l|\dot\varphi|_{\max}=l\omega\varphi_i=\varphi_i\sqrt{gl}$.

(e) Taking the lowest point as zero of potential energy: $E=\frac12mv^2=\frac12mgl\varphi_i^2$ (the small-angle form of $mgl(1-\cos\varphi_i)$). $P=m\varphi_i\sqrt{gl}$, pointing in the $+x$ direction (from the release side towards the other).

(f) $mv=mv_1'+mv_2'$ and $\frac12mv^2=\frac12mv_1'^2+\frac12mv_2'^2$. Equal masses, elastic (lesson 8): the solution other than “no collision” is $v_1'=0$, $v_2'=v=\varphi_i\sqrt{gl}$. Pendulum 1 stops, pendulum 2 takes over.

(g) $\varphi_1=-\varphi_i\cos\omega t$ until $\omega t=\frac\pi2$; then $\varphi_1=0$ while pendulum 2 swings out and back (half a period, until $\frac{3\pi}2$); then pendulum 2 stops and pendulum 1 swings out to $-\varphi_i$ (at $2\pi$) and back to $0$ (at $\frac{5\pi}2$); rest until $\frac{7\pi}2$; swing out to $-\varphi_i$ (at $4\pi$) and back (at $\frac{9\pi}2$); rest until $5\pi$. Pendulum 1 only ever moves on the negative side.

Exercise 12 (HS23, problem 4(a)–(e), “Ringpendel”, 7 points, original)

A flat ring of mass $M$ (inner radius $R-\frac d2$, outer radius $R+\frac d2$) hangs on a horizontal axis at distance $R$ from its centre; $J_D=\frac14M(d^2+8R^2)$ (you showed it in lesson 11). (a) At $t=0$ the centre is level with the axis. What angular velocity $\omega_0$ does the ring need at least to make a complete turn? (b) Now it is released from rest at $\varphi=\frac\pi2$ (measured from the vertical). Give its energy at $\varphi=\frac\pi2$ and at $\varphi=0$. (c) Set up the equation of motion $\varphi(t)$ (no need to solve). (d) For small angles, find the general solution. (e) What length $l$ must a string pendulum have to swing with the same period?

Solution

(a) To turn over, the centre must reach the point straight above the axis, a rise of $R$. Energy: $\frac12J_D\omega_0^2=MgR$, so $\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{2MgR}{J_D}}=\sqrt{\frac{8gR}{d^2+8R^2}}$.

(b) With the zero of potential energy at the lowest position of the centre: at $\frac\pi2$ the ring is at rest, $E=MgR$ (all potential). At $\varphi=0$: $E=\frac12J_D\dot\varphi^2$ (all kinetic), and by energy conservation it is also $MgR$.

(c) $J_D\ddot\varphi=-MgR\sin\varphi$.

(d) $\ddot\varphi+\frac{MgR}{J_D}\varphi=0$, so $\varphi(t)=A\sin(\omega t+\delta)$ with $\omega=\sqrt{\frac{4gR}{d^2+8R^2}}$, $A$ and $\delta$ from the initial conditions.

(e) Equal periods means $\frac gl=\omega^2$: $l=\frac{d^2+8R^2}{4R}=2R+\frac{d^2}{4R}$, the equivalent length $\frac{J_D}{MR}$.

Exercise 13 (FS23, problem 4(b)–(d), “Harmonische Drehschwingung”, 17 points, original)

A thin rod (length $L$, mass $m$, $J_S=\frac1{12}mL^2$) hangs on a horizontal axis through a point P at distance $d$ above its centre of mass S. (b) Show that for small angles $\ddot\varphi+c\varphi=0$ and find $c$. (c) Give the general solution and the period, and interpret the period for $d\to0$ and $d\gg L$. (d) A spring of constant $k$ is now attached at S; it is relaxed when the rod hangs vertically and always pushes perpendicular to the rod. Set up the equation of motion.

Solution

(b) Steiner: $J_P=\frac1{12}mL^2+md^2$. Gravity acts at S with lever arm $d\sin\varphi$: $J_P\ddot\varphi=-mgd\sin\varphi\approx-mgd\varphi$. So $c=\frac{mgd}{J_P}=\frac{12gd}{L^2+12d^2}$.

(c) $\varphi(t)=A\sin(\sqrt c\,t+\delta)$, with $A$, $\delta$ from the initial conditions (released from rest at $\varphi_0$: $\varphi=\varphi_0\cos\sqrt c\,t$). Period $T=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt c}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L^2+12d^2}{12gd}}$. For $d\to0$: $T\to\infty$; with the axis through S gravity has no torque, every position is an equilibrium and nothing oscillates. For $d\gg L$: $T\to2\pi\sqrt{d/g}$, a string pendulum of length $d$; the rod is small compared with its distance and acts like a point mass.

(d) S moves on a circle of radius $d$; at angle $\varphi$ it is displaced by the arc $d\varphi$, so the spring pushes back with $kd\varphi$, perpendicular to the rod, lever arm $d$: torque $-kd^2\varphi$. Together: $J_P\ddot\varphi=-mgd\sin\varphi-kd^2\varphi$, for small angles $\ddot\varphi+\frac{mgd+kd^2}{\frac1{12}mL^2+md^2}\varphi=0$. The spring makes it stiffer, so faster.

Exercise 14 (FS26, problem 4(e)–(f), “Die Schaukel”, 4 points, original)

The swing from lesson 11: a half-cylinder (mass $M$, radius $R$, centre of mass $y_S=\frac{4R}{3\pi}$ below its flat top) on a massless rope of length $L$, so that S is at distance $L+y_S$ from the suspension point O; $I_O=\frac12MR^2+M(L^2+2Ly_S)$. (e) Give the restoring torque about O as a function of the angle $\varphi$. (f) Set up the equation of motion with the angular momentum theorem, simplify it for small angles, and give the natural frequency $\omega_{\text{leer}}$.

Solution

(e) The weight $Mg$ acts at S, lever arm $(L+y_S)\sin\varphi$: $\vec\tau=-Mg(L+y_S)\sin\varphi\,\hat e_z$, with $\varphi$ counted positive about $+\hat e_z$. The sign says the torque always turns the swing back towards $\varphi=0$.

(f) $\dot L_z=I_O\ddot\varphi=-Mg(L+y_S)\sin\varphi$ $\approx-Mg(L+y_S)\varphi$, so $\omega_{\text{leer}}=\sqrt{\frac{Mg(L+y_S)}{I_O}}=\sqrt{\frac{g(L+y_S)}{\frac12R^2+L^2+2Ly_S}}$ with $y_S=\frac{4R}{3\pi}$.

Exercise 15 (FS25, problem 2(a)–(d), “Halfpipe”, 7 points, original)

A skateboarder (point mass $m$) glides without friction in a parabolic halfpipe of height $h$ and width $d$ (from rim to rim); the origin O is the lowest point. (a) Give the shape $y(x)$ and the potential energy $V(x)$ with $V(0)=0$. (b) Give the component $F_\parallel$ of the weight along the halfpipe as a function of the slope angle $\alpha(x)$, and $\alpha(x)$. (c) What are $F_\parallel$ and $\alpha$ for $x/d\ll1$? Compare with $F_x=-\frac{dV}{dx}$. (d) For $x/d\ll1$ the motion is $m\ddot x=-\frac{dV}{dx}$. Solve it with $x(0)=x_0$, $\dot x(0)=0$. What kind of motion is it?

Solution

(a) A parabola through O with $y(\pm\frac d2)=h$: $y=\frac{4h}{d^2}x^2$, $V(x)=mgy=\frac{4mgh}{d^2}x^2$.

(b) $F_\parallel=-mg\sin\alpha$ (along the slope, downhill), with $\tan\alpha=y'(x)$: $\alpha(x)=\arctan\frac{8hx}{d^2}$.

(c) For small $x/d$: $\alpha\approx\frac{8hx}{d^2}$ and $F_\parallel\approx-mg\alpha=-\frac{8mgh}{d^2}x$. That is exactly $-\frac{dV}{dx}$: near the bottom the halfpipe acts like a spring with $k=\frac{8mgh}{d^2}$.

(d) $\ddot x+\frac{8gh}{d^2}x=0$, so $x(t)=x_0\cos\omega t$ with $\omega=\frac{\sqrt{8gh}}d$: a harmonic oscillation (for small amplitudes).

8. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Galileo's chandelier. The story goes that the young Galileo, bored in Pisa cathedral, timed a swinging chandelier against his own pulse and noticed that wide and narrow swings took the same time. That is the amplitude independence of section 3, and it gave the world its first accurate clocks. It is only true for small angles: at $90^\circ$ a pendulum is about $18\,\%$ slower. Huygens later found a curve, the cycloid you saw on the rolling wheel in lesson 11, along which the period is exactly independent of the amplitude.

Why a car has shock absorbers. Car springs alone would make the car bounce after every bump. The shock absorbers add damping close to critical: the car returns to its level as quickly as possible without bouncing. Bathroom scales and the needles of old meters are damped the same way.
