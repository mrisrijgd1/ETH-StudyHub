Why it matters for the exam

Rotating frames come up in almost every recent exam. The multiple-choice part asks about the Coriolis force nearly every time (HS23, HS25, FS26: all three are exercises below), and the long problems use the centrifugal and Coriolis force inside bigger stories: a rotating crane (HS23), a satellite changing orbit (FS24), a space elevator (FS26). The formula sheet gives you all the formulas; what you need is to know which term to use, and its direction.

Recap of lesson 3 (3 min)

  1. A crate rests on a ramp of angle $\alpha$. When does it stick?

    Answer

    When $\tan\alpha\le\mu_H$: the friction needed, $mg\sin\alpha$, is at most $\mu_HF_N=\mu_Hmg\cos\alpha$.

  2. What does a scale in a lift accelerating upwards with $a$ show?

    Answer

    The normal force $F_N=m(g+a)$: more than $mg$.

  3. What is the terminal velocity with drag $\frac12c_W\rho Av^2$?

    Answer

    $v_\infty=\sqrt{\frac{2mg}{c_W\rho A}}$, where the drag equals the weight.

1. The story: the winter fair

When the moat has frozen, the fortress holds its winter fair. A sledge pulled by horses races across the ice, and a lantern hangs from its roof. When the horses start, the lantern swings backwards and stays tilted, as if a hand pushed it towards the back. Nobody touches it.

In the courtyard turns a great wooden carousel. Two children sit on it, one at the centre and one at the rim, and try to roll a ball to each other across the boards. It never arrives: every ball curves away, as if pushed sideways by an invisible hand. An old soldier watching from the courtyard wall laughs. From up there, he says, every ball rolls dead straight. It's the carousel that turns away under it.

In physics: the sledge and the carousel are accelerated frames of reference. Newton's laws hold only in inertial frames (lesson 2), like the soldier on the wall. If you insist on using them on the sledge or the carousel, you have to add fictitious forces: the inertial force $-m\vec A$ that tilts the lantern, and the centrifugal and Coriolis forces that bend the ball's path. They are not interactions between bodies; they are the price of describing the world from a frame that accelerates.

2. The linearly accelerated frame

Let a frame $K'$ move relative to an inertial frame $K$, with its origin at $\vec R(t)$, its velocity $\vec V=\dot{\vec R}$ and its acceleration $\vec A=\ddot{\vec R}$, without turning. Then $\vec r=\vec R+\vec r\,'$, and differentiating twice, $\vec a=\vec A+\vec a\,'$ (script, section 3.11, page 76).

Newton's second law holds in $K$: $\vec F=m\vec a=m\vec A+m\vec a\,'$. Bring $m\vec A$ to the other side:

$$m\vec a\,'=\vec F+\vec F_T,\qquad\vec F_T=-m\vec A.$$

In $K'$ the second law looks the same as always, if you add the inertial force $\vec F_T=-m\vec A$, pointing against the frame's acceleration. Rupp writes the frames as IS (inertial) and BS′ (accelerated) and the frame's acceleration as $\vec A_0$; the formula sheet uses IS, BS′ and $\vec A$.

Worked example 1: the lantern on the sledge

A lantern of mass $m$ hangs on a string from the roof of a sledge that accelerates horizontally with $A$. At what angle $\theta$ to the vertical does the string hang?

  1. From the ice (frame $K$): which forces act on the lantern, and what is its acceleration?

    Answer

    The weight $mg$ (down) and the string's tension $F_S$ (along the string). The lantern moves with the sledge, so its acceleration is $A$, horizontal. Horizontally $F_S\sin\theta=mA$, vertically $F_S\cos\theta=mg$.

  2. From the sledge (frame $K'$): the lantern is at rest. Which forces balance?

    Answer

    Weight, tension and the inertial force $mA$ pointing backwards. Horizontally $F_S\sin\theta=mA$, vertically $F_S\cos\theta=mg$. The same two equations, now read as a balance.

  3. Find $\theta$.

    Answer

    Divide: $\tan\theta=\frac Ag$. The lantern tilts backwards, away from the acceleration; the mass cancels. Hang a lantern in a train and it's an accelerometer.

Try it: the lantern as an accelerometer

Choose the sledge's acceleration (forwards positive, braking negative). The arrows are the weight (orange), the inertial force (dark) and the string's tension (blue), as seen on the sledge.

The free-falling lift (script, page 82). A lift whose rope breaks falls with $\vec A=\vec g$. For a person in it, $m\vec a\,'=m\vec g-m\vec g=0$: relative to the lift, the person floats. This is the weightlessness of lesson 3, seen from inside.

3. The rotating frame: velocity and acceleration

Now let $K'$ also rotate relative to $K$, with a constant angular velocity $\vec\omega$. The trouble is that the basis vectors $\hat e_{x'},\hat e_{y'},\hat e_{z'}$ of $K'$ now turn, seen from $K$. In lesson 1 we found how a rotating unit vector changes: $\dot{\hat e}=\vec\omega\times\hat e$ (script, equation (2.69)).

Velocity (section 3.11.1, page 76). Write $\vec r\,'=x'\hat e_{x'}+y'\hat e_{y'}+z'\hat e_{z'}$ and differentiate with the product rule:

$$\dot{\vec r}\,'=\underbrace{\dot x'\hat e_{x'}+\dot y'\hat e_{y'}+\dot z'\hat e_{z'}}_{=\vec v\,'}+\underbrace{x'\dot{\hat e}_{x'}+y'\dot{\hat e}_{y'}+z'\dot{\hat e}_{z'}}_{=\vec\omega\times\vec r\,'}.$$

So the velocity seen from $K$ is

$$\vec v=\vec V+\vec v\,'+\vec\omega\times\vec r\,':$$

the frame's motion, the motion relative to the frame, and the frame's rotation carrying the point along.

Acceleration (section 3.11.2, page 78). Differentiate once more, with the same rule for every rotating basis vector:

$$\vec a=\vec a\,'+\vec A+2\,\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'+\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r\,').$$
Where the factor 2 comes from (open if you want the steps)

Differentiate $\vec v=\vec V+\vec v\,'+\vec\omega\times\vec r\,'$. First, $\dot{\vec V}=\vec A$. Second, $\vec v\,'$ also has rotating basis vectors, so $\dot{\vec v}\,'=\vec a\,'+\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'$, with the same trick as for $\dot{\vec r}\,'$. Third, $\frac d{dt}(\vec\omega\times\vec r\,')=\vec\omega\times\dot{\vec r}\,'=\vec\omega\times(\vec v\,'+\vec\omega\times\vec r\,')$. So $\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'$ appears twice, once from each of the last two terms: that's the 2.

The four terms: $\vec a\,'$ the acceleration relative to $K'$; $\vec A$ the acceleration of the origin of $K'$; $2\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'$ the Coriolis term; $\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r\,')$ the centripetal term. For $\vec\omega$ along $z$ and $\vec r\,'$ in the $xy$-plane, $\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r\,')=-\omega^2\vec r\,'$ (script, equation (3.167)): the centripetal acceleration of lesson 1, pointing to the axis.

4. Fictitious forces

In $K$, $\vec F=m\vec a$. A real force doesn't depend on the frame: it comes from distances and relative velocities of bodies, which all observers agree on (section 3.11.3, page 79). Insert the acceleration formula and bring everything except $m\vec a\,'$ to the right:

$$m\vec a\,'=\vec F\;\underbrace{-\,m\vec A}_{\vec F_T}\;\underbrace{-\,2m\,\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'}_{\vec F_C}\;\underbrace{-\,m\,\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r\,')}_{\vec F_{ZF}}.$$
The three fictitious forces (formula sheet, page 2)
  • Inertial force $\vec F_T=-m\vec A$: against the frame's acceleration.
  • Centrifugal force $\vec F_{ZF}=-m\,\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r\,')$: away from the axis, size $m\omega^2r_\perp$, where $r_\perp$ is the distance from the axis. It acts on everything in the rotating frame, moving or not.
  • Coriolis force $\vec F_C=-2m\,\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'=2m\,\vec v\,'\times\vec\omega$: only on bodies that move relative to the rotating frame. It is perpendicular to both $\vec v\,'$ and $\vec\omega$, so it never does work, and it vanishes when $\vec v\,'\parallel\vec\omega$.

All three are proportional to $m$ and vanish in an inertial frame ($\vec A=0$, $\vec\omega=0$). They are not interactions: they have no third-law partner.

“Real” acceleration (section 3.11.4, page 81). The script resolves the puzzle like this: the true acceleration $\vec a_e$ is the left side of the acceleration formula, the same in every frame, and $\vec F_e=m\vec a_e$ holds everywhere. Only in an inertial frame is $\vec a_e$ equal to the second derivative of the coordinates. In $K'$, $\ddot{\vec r}\,'$ can be zero while a real force acts (the free-falling lift), or non-zero while none acts (the ball on the carousel). Rupp writes the same law solved for $\vec a\,'$: $\vec a\,'=\vec a-\vec A_0-2\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'-\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r\,')$.

Recipe: a problem in an accelerated frame

  1. Decide whether a frame change helps. Is the body at rest or simple in a rotating or accelerating frame (a mass on a turning crane, a lantern in a train)? Then use that frame.
  2. Draw all real forces (weight, normal force, tension, friction, gravity), as in lesson 3.
  3. Add the fictitious forces: $-m\vec A$ if the frame accelerates; $m\omega^2r_\perp$ outwards from the axis if it rotates; $-2m\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'$ if the body moves relative to the rotating frame.
  4. Write $m\vec a\,'=\sum\vec F$ in components. At rest in $K'$, $\vec a\,'=0$ and $\vec v\,'=0$: no Coriolis force, only a balance.
  5. Check: from the inertial frame, the same equations must come out with $m\vec a$ on the left.

Worked example 2: the ball on the carousel (script, section 3.11.4.1)

A frame $K'$ rotates with constant $\vec\omega=\omega\hat z$ about the common $z$-axis. A ball starts at the origin with $\vec v\,'(0)=v_0\hat y'$, and no real force acts in the plane. How fast does it move relative to $K'$ at distance $r'$ from the axis?

  1. Compute the Coriolis and the centrifugal term in components ($\vec v\,'=(\dot x',\dot y',0)$).

    Answer

    $-2m\,\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'=-2m\omega\,\hat z\times(\dot x'\hat x'+\dot y'\hat y')=2m\omega\,(\dot y',-\dot x',0)$, and $-m\,\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r\,')=m\omega^2(x',y',0)$.

  2. Write the equations of motion in $K'$.

    Answer

    $\ddot x'=2\omega\dot y'+\omega^2x'$ and $\ddot y'=-2\omega\dot x'+\omega^2y'$ (script, (3.200)–(3.201)).

  3. Multiply the first by $\dot x'$, the second by $\dot y'$, and add. What happens to the Coriolis terms? Integrate.

    Answer

    The Coriolis terms $2\omega\dot y'\dot x'-2\omega\dot x'\dot y'$ cancel: the Coriolis force does no work. What remains is $\dot x'\ddot x'+\dot y'\ddot y'=\omega^2(x'\dot x'+y'\dot y')$, that is $\frac d{dt}\frac12v'^2=\frac d{dt}\frac12\omega^2r'^2$. So $\frac12v'^2=\frac12\omega^2r'^2+c$, and the start ($r'=0$, $v'=v_0$) gives $c=\frac12v_0^2$:

    $$v'=\sqrt{v_0^2+\omega^2r'^2}.$$

    The ball gets faster relative to the carousel as it moves out, with no real force at all. (The script's equation (3.208) has a misprint: it writes $0$ for $v_0^2$.)

Try it: the carousel from two points of view

The ball is rolled from the centre with no friction. Left: seen from the courtyard wall (inertial). Right: seen by a child on the carousel. Change the carousel's speed and press Roll.

Same ball, same motion, two descriptions. The straight line on the left needs no force. The curve on the right needs the centrifugal force (pushing it outwards, faster and faster) and the Coriolis force (bending it against the rotation).

5. Coriolis on the earth and the Foucault pendulum

The earth turns once a day: $\omega=\frac{2\pi}{1\,\mathrm{day}}\approx7.3\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, along the axis from south to north. Split $\vec\omega$ at latitude $\varphi$ into a part perpendicular to the ground, $\omega_\perp=\omega\sin\varphi$, and a part along it. For horizontal motion, only $\omega_\perp$ gives a horizontal Coriolis acceleration (section 3.11.4.2, page 86):

$$|\vec a_C|_{\text{horizontal}}=2v'\omega\sin\varphi.$$
  • In the northern hemisphere, everything moving horizontally is deflected to the right, in the southern to the left.
  • Air flowing into a low-pressure area is deflected to the right and ends up circling it counter-clockwise (in the north): that's why storms spin.
  • At the equator, $\omega_\perp=0$: no horizontal deflection. Crossing it flips the direction.

The Foucault pendulum (section 3.11.4.3, pages 86–90). A long pendulum swings in a plane. The Coriolis force pushes it slightly sideways on every swing, and the script shows that its plane of swinging turns with the angular velocity $\dot\alpha=\omega_\perp=\omega\sin\varphi$. Seen from the inertial frame, the plane stays put and the earth turns underneath. A full turn of the plane takes

$$T_{\text{plane}}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega\sin\varphi}=\frac{24\,\mathrm h}{\sin\varphi}:$$

a day at the poles, about $33\,\mathrm h$ in Zurich ($\varphi\approx47^\circ$), never at the equator.

Worked example 3: how strong is the Coriolis force?

A car drives north at $v'=100\,\mathrm{km/h}\approx28\,\mathrm{m/s}$ in Zurich ($\sin47^\circ\approx0.73$). How large is the horizontal Coriolis acceleration, and which way does it push?

  1. Its size?

    Answer

    $a_C=2v'\omega\sin\varphi\approx2\cdot28\cdot7.3\cdot10^{-5}\cdot0.73\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\approx3\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$: about $0.03\,\%$ of $g$. You will never feel it in a car.

  2. Its direction? Use $\vec a_C=-2\vec\omega_\perp\times\vec v\,'$ with $\hat z$ up, $\hat y$ north, $\hat x$ east.

    Answer

    $-2\omega_\perp\hat z\times v'\hat y=-2\omega_\perp v'(\hat z\times\hat y)=+2\omega_\perp v'\hat x$: east, to the right of the direction of travel.

  3. So why does the Coriolis force matter for weather but not for cars?

    Answer

    It's tiny, but it acts for days over thousands of kilometres, while the pressure forces driving the wind are tiny too. For a car, friction forces thousands of times larger dominate.

Try it: the Foucault pendulum around the world

Choose the latitude. The pendulum is seen from above, and one day passes in 12 seconds.

Why it's cool. In 1851 Léon Foucault hung a 67-metre pendulum in the Panthéon in Paris and let the public watch its plane creep around. It was the first experiment that showed the earth's rotation without looking at the sky. The fictitious forces are not “fake”: they are how a rotating world looks from inside.

6. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed.

Exercise 1 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.1(d), 1 point, original)

A body of mass $m$ moves with velocity $\vec v$ relative to a frame rotating with angular velocity $\vec\omega$. Which statement about the Coriolis force is correct? (A) The Coriolis force is $\vec F_{\text{cor}}=2m(\vec v\times\vec\omega)$. (B) The Coriolis force acts only when the body moves radially away from the centre of rotation. (C) The Coriolis force can do work, since it acts perpendicular to the velocity. (D) The Coriolis force depends only on the speed of rotation.

Solution

A. $-2m\,\vec\omega\times\vec v=2m\,\vec v\times\vec\omega$, since the cross product changes sign when you swap the factors. (B) It acts for any $\vec v$ not parallel to $\vec\omega$, also tangential motion. (C) Perpendicular to the velocity means it does no work. (D) It depends on $m$ and $\vec v$ too.

Exercise 2 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.2(d), 1 point, original)

In a rotating frame: (A) the centrifugal force is always larger than the Coriolis force; (B) the Coriolis force is always larger than the centrifugal force; (C) the centrifugal and the Coriolis force always point the same way; (D) the Coriolis force points perpendicular to the axis of rotation.

Solution

D. $\vec F_C=-2m\,\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'$ is a cross product with $\vec\omega$, so it is perpendicular to $\vec\omega$, the axis. (A), (B): the ratio depends on $v'$ and $r_\perp$ (at rest, $F_C=0$; on the axis, $F_{ZF}=0$). (C): the Coriolis force depends on the direction of $\vec v\,'$, the centrifugal force doesn't.

Exercise 3 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.2(a), 1 point, original)

A pendulum swings parallel to the earth's surface; the earth rotates with $\vec\omega$. Which statement is correct? (A) Since the Coriolis force is only a fictitious force, the pendulum's direction of swing relative to the earth's surface never changes. (B) If the pendulum swings in Zurich (mid-latitude), a Coriolis force acts for every horizontal direction of motion. (C) The Coriolis force acts against the direction of motion and slows the pendulum down. (D) If the pendulum swings exactly at the equator, no Coriolis force ever acts.

Solution

B. The Coriolis force vanishes only for $\vec v\,'\parallel\vec\omega$. In Zurich $\vec\omega$ points into the sky at $47^\circ$, so no horizontal velocity is parallel to it. (A) The plane does turn: that's Foucault. (C) It's perpendicular to the motion. (D) At the equator $\vec\omega$ is horizontal (north): a north–south swing feels nothing, but an east–west swing feels a vertical Coriolis force.

Exercise 4 (HS23, problem 3 “Kran”, parts (d) and (e), 4 points, original)

A crane's horizontal arm of length $L$ turns about a vertical axis at $L/4$ from one end, with constant angular velocity $\omega$, counter-clockwise seen from above. At the long end (distance $\frac34L$ from the axis) a mass $m$ hangs on a massless rope of length $l$.

(d) Find the angle $\theta$ of the rope to the vertical in dynamic equilibrium. Use $\sin x\approx\tan x\approx x$. [3 points]

(e) Neglect the deflection now ($\theta=0$). The crane moves the rope's suspension point inwards with constant speed $v$, still rotating with $\omega$. Give the size of the Coriolis force on $m$. In which direction does it act, in cylindrical coordinates $(r,\varphi,z)$? [1 point]

Solution

(d) In the crane's frame the mass is at rest. Forces: weight $mg$ down, tension along the rope, centrifugal force $m\omega^2r$ outwards, where $r=\frac34L+l\sin\theta$ is the mass's distance from the axis. Balance: $F_S\cos\theta=mg$, $F_S\sin\theta=m\omega^2r$, so $\tan\theta=\frac{\omega^2r}g$. With small angles,

$$\theta=\frac{\omega^2}g\left(\tfrac34L+l\theta\right)\iff\theta=\frac{3\omega^2L}{4\,(g-\omega^2l)}.$$

(If you take $r\approx\frac34L$, you get $\theta\approx\frac{3\omega^2L}{4g}$, fine when $\omega^2l\ll g$.)

(e) $\vec\omega=\omega\hat z$ (counter-clockwise from above), $\vec v\,'=-v\hat r$. Then $\vec F_C=-2m\,\omega\hat z\times(-v\hat r)=2m\omega v\,(\hat z\times\hat r)=2m\omega v\,\hat\varphi$. Size $2m\omega v$, in the $+\varphi$ direction: the direction of rotation. (Moving inwards, the mass still has the larger speed of the outer circle, so it runs ahead.)

Exercise 5 (FS26, problem 3 “Weltraumlift”, parts (b) and (c), 2 points, original)

A planet of mass $M$ and radius $R$ rotates with $\omega$. A rigid rod sticks radially out from the equator, from $r=R$ to $r=r_{\text{end}}$, and turns with the planet. A lift carries a small test mass $m$ along the rod.

(b) Find the radius $r_S$ of the geostationary orbit in terms of $G$, $M$ and $\omega$. Hint: in a geostationary orbit, gravity and the centrifugal force cancel. [1 point]

(c) The test mass rests in the lift at radius $r$ (between $R$ and $r_{\text{end}}$). What effective force $F_{\text{eff}}(r)$ does it feel? Take $F_{\text{eff}}>0$ as pointing outwards. [1 point]

Solution

(b) In the rotating frame, at rest at $r_S$: $G\frac{Mm}{r_S^2}=m\omega^2r_S$, so $r_S=\left(\frac{GM}{\omega^2}\right)^{1/3}$.

(c) At rest in the rotating frame there is no Coriolis force, only gravity (inwards) and the centrifugal force (outwards): $F_{\text{eff}}(r)=m\omega^2r-G\frac{Mm}{r^2}$. It's negative below $r_S$ (the mass is pulled down) and positive above (it's flung out).

Exercise 6 (FS24, problem 3 “Satellit”, parts (f) and (g), 3 points, original)

A satellite is on a circular orbit with angular velocity $\omega$. For a short time it flies with speed $v'$ radially away from the earth, perpendicular to its orbit.

(f) Seen in the frame rotating with the orbit, which additional effective force acts on the satellite? Give the size of the resulting acceleration and its direction. Take $\omega$ constant. [2 points]

(g) Is there a direction of $\vec v\,'$ for which this effective force would not appear? If so, give it relative to $\vec\omega$. [1 point]

Solution

(f) The Coriolis force. Gravity and centrifugal force cancel on the orbit, and $\vec v\,'\perp\vec\omega$, so $|\vec a_C|=2\omega v'$. Direction: $\vec a_C=-2\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'$. With $\vec v\,'=v'\hat r$ and $\vec\omega$ along $\hat z$ (orbit counter-clockwise), $-2\omega v'(\hat z\times\hat r)=-2\omega v'\hat\varphi$: in the orbital plane, perpendicular to $\vec v\,'$, against the direction of orbital motion. The satellite falls behind.

(g) Yes: $\vec v\,'\parallel\vec\omega$ (or antiparallel), perpendicular to the orbital plane. Then $\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'=0$.

Exercise 7 (the lantern, a quick one)

A train brakes from $72\,\mathrm{km/h}$ to rest in $10\,\mathrm s$, uniformly. In which direction and by how much does a lantern hanging from the ceiling tilt? ($g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.)

Solution

$A=\frac{20\,\mathrm{m/s}}{10\,\mathrm s}=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ backwards. The inertial force $-m\vec A$ points forwards, so the lantern tilts forwards, with $\tan\theta=\frac Ag=0.2$, $\theta\approx11^\circ$.

Scoring yourself: Exercises 1–6 make 12 points. With 8 you're done. Exercise 7 is a warm-up.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

The bathtub myth. Does water drain clockwise in Australia and counter-clockwise in Switzerland? Work it out: in a bathtub, $v'$ is a few centimetres per second, so $a_C\approx10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, far below the swirl left over from filling the tub. In 1962 Ascher Shapiro at MIT let a large tank settle for a day, then drained it: it turned counter-clockwise, as Coriolis predicts in the north. In an ordinary sink, the shape of the basin wins.

Einstein's lift. Inside the free-falling lift, no experiment can tell you that gravity exists; inside a lift accelerated upwards in space, none can tell you it isn't gravity. Einstein called this “the happiest thought of my life” (1907) and built general relativity on it: gravity itself is a kind of inertial force.
