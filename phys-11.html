You're done when
you can (1) compute a moment of inertia as an integral $\int r_\perp^2\,dm$ and know the standard ones, (2) use Steiner's theorem, starting from the axis through the centre of mass, (3) write $L=J\omega$, $T_{\text{rot}}=\frac12J\omega^2$ and $M=J\ddot\varphi$ for a fixed axis, (4) use the rolling condition $v=R\omega$ and energy conservation to find the speed of a rolling body, and (5) say which body wins a rolling race and why.

Why it matters for the exam

Every exam has a multiple-choice block “Drehbewegung” (four to five points), and most have a long problem built on a moment of inertia: the ball rolling off a ramp (FS24, 10 points), the clay-pigeon thrower (HS25), the crane (HS23), the swing (FS26), the ring pendulum (HS23), the rod pendulum (FS23). Three tools carry almost all of it: $J=\int r_\perp^2\,dm$ with Steiner, energy with a rotational term, and the rolling condition. All the exam questions below come from those exams.

Recap of lesson 10 (3 min)

  1. How do you compute the centre of mass of a continuous body?

    Answer

    $\vec r_S=\frac1M\int\vec r\,dm=\frac1M\int\vec r\rho\,dV$; use symmetry first, then slice the body.

  2. What are the two conditions for static equilibrium?

    Answer

    $\sum\vec F_i=0$ and $\sum\vec M_i=0$; the second about any point, best one where an unknown force acts.

  3. For a point mass on a circle of radius $r$ (lesson 7), how are $L$ and $\omega$ related?

    Answer

    $L=mr^2\omega$. Today the factor $mr^2$ grows into the moment of inertia of a whole body.

1. The story: the mill wheel

Below the castle the miller is replacing her water wheel. The old one was a solid oak disc; the new one is a light rim with spokes, the same weight. “It will be harder to start and harder to stop,” she tells the children, “because all its wood sits far out.” To show them, she lets three things race down the ramp to the river: a cheese wheel, a wooden hoop of the same weight, and a block of butter on a greased board. The butter slides down first. The cheese beats the hoop every time, and it doesn't matter how big or heavy the cheese is.

Then she winds a rope round the axle of the millstone, hangs a sack on the end and lets go. The sack does not fall freely: it has to spin up the stone as it falls.

In physics: for rotation the role of the mass is taken by the moment of inertia $J$, which depends not only on the mass but on how far from the axis it sits. The rim has its mass at $R$, the disc spread between $0$ and $R$, so the rim's $J$ is twice as large. A rolling body must spend part of its energy on spinning, and the larger its $J$ (relative to $mR^2$), the slower it rolls. The butter doesn't spin at all and wins.

2. Rotation about a fixed axis

Let a rigid body turn with angular velocity $\omega$ about a fixed axis A (section 8.5.5, page 204). Each mass element $\Delta m_i$ moves on a circle of radius $r_{i\perp}$, its distance from the axis (not from a point!), with speed $v_i=\omega r_{i\perp}$. Its angular momentum along the axis is $\Delta m_i\,r_{i\perp}^2\,\omega$, as for the point mass in lesson 7. Summing:

$$L_\parallel=J_A\,\omega,\qquad J_A=\sum_i\Delta m_i\,r_{i\perp}^2=\int r_\perp^2\,dm=\int_V r_\perp^2\,\rho\,dV.$$

$J_A$ is the moment of inertia about the axis A, unit $\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$. It depends on where the axis is and on how it is oriented. The same sum gives the kinetic energy and, with $\dot{\vec L}=\vec M$ from lesson 7, the equation of motion (pages 205–206):

$$T_{\text{rot}}=\tfrac12J_A\omega^2,\qquad M_\parallel=J_A\,\dot\omega=J_A\,\ddot\varphi.$$

Only the torque component along the axis counts; the bearings take up everything else. With these, rotation about a fixed axis is a copy of motion along a line (script, table 8.1, page 207):

TranslationRotation about a fixed axis
position $x$angle $\varphi$
velocity $v=\dot x$angular velocity $\omega=\dot\varphi$
acceleration $a=\ddot x$angular acceleration $\alpha=\ddot\varphi$
mass $m$moment of inertia $J_A=\int r_\perp^2\,dm$
momentum $p=mv$angular momentum $L=J_A\omega$
force $F=\dot p=ma$torque $M=\dot L=J_A\alpha$
work $W=\int F\,dx$work $W=\int M\,d\varphi$
kinetic energy $\frac12mv^2$rotational energy $\frac12J_A\omega^2$
power $P=Fv$power $P=M\omega$

Notation: the script and the formula sheet write $J$ for the moment of inertia; Rupp and several exams (FS24, FS26) write $I$. Same thing.

If the body also moves as a whole, its kinetic energy splits like in lesson 7 (page 204): $T=\frac12mv_S^2+\frac12J_S\omega^2$, the motion of the centre of mass plus the rotation about it.

3. Computing moments of inertia

The habits from the centre-of-mass integral (lesson 10) carry over: slice the body into pieces whose distance from the axis is known. For a constant density, the script computes five bodies (section 8.5.7, pages 207–209), all about an axis through the centre of mass:

The standard moments of inertia (not on the formula sheet)
  • Thin hollow cylinder or hoop, radius $R$, about its axis: $J=MR^2$ (all the mass is at distance $R$).
  • Solid cylinder or disc, about its axis: $J=\frac12MR^2$.
  • Solid sphere: $J=\frac25MR^2$. (A thin hollow sphere, which the exams also use: $J=\frac23MR^2$.)
  • Cuboid with edges $a,b$ perpendicular to the axis: $J=\frac1{12}M(a^2+b^2)$.
  • Thin rod of length $\ell$, axis perpendicular through its middle: $J=\frac1{12}M\ell^2$.

The more mass sits far from the axis, the larger the number in front of $MR^2$: hoop 1, hollow sphere $\frac23$, disc $\frac12$, solid sphere $\frac25$.

Worked example 1: the solid cylinder and the rod (script, pages 208–209)

  1. A solid cylinder (radius $R$, height $h$, density $\rho$) turns about its axis. Set up $J_z$ in cylindrical coordinates, $dV=r_\perp\,dr_\perp\,d\varphi\,dz$.

    Answer

    $J_z=\rho\int_0^h dz\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_0^R r_\perp^2\,r_\perp\,dr_\perp$ $=\rho\cdot h\cdot2\pi\cdot\frac{R^4}4$.

  2. Express it with the mass.

    Answer

    $M=\rho\pi R^2h$, so $J_z=\frac12\rho\pi R^2h\cdot R^2=\frac12MR^2$. The height drops out: a coin and a log have the same formula.

  3. A thin rod (length $\ell$, mass $M$) turns about an axis through its middle, perpendicular to it. Use the linear density $\lambda=\frac M\ell$.

    Answer

    $J=\int_{-\ell/2}^{\ell/2}x^2\lambda\,dx=\lambda\frac{\ell^3}{12}=\frac1{12}M\ell^2$. This is FS23 problem 4 (a), worth five points.

Steiner's theorem (section 8.5.8, page 210). Often the axis does not go through the centre of mass. Take an axis $z'$ parallel to the axis $z$ through S, at distance $a$. With $\vec r\,'=\vec a+\vec r$ (perpendicular parts):

$$J_{z'}=\int(\vec a+\vec r)^2\,dm=a^2\!\int dm+2\,\vec a\cdot\!\int\vec r\,dm+\int r^2\,dm=J_z+Ma^2.$$

The middle term vanishes because S is the centre of mass ($\int\vec r\,dm=0$ relative to S). So:

Steiner's theorem (formula sheet, page 5 left)

$J_A=J_S+Md^2$, where $J_S$ is about the parallel axis through the centre of mass and $d$ the distance between the axes. Consequence: among all parallel axes, the one through S has the smallest $J$.

The classic trap: Steiner only connects an axis with the axis through S. To go from an axis A (not through S) to another axis O, go via S: $J_S=J_A-Md_A^2$, then $J_O=J_S+Md_O^2$ (FS26 problem 4 (d), Exercise 18).

Try it: Steiner's theorem

Move the axis away from the centre of mass, parallel to itself. The curve is $J(d)$ in units of $ML^2$ (rod of length $L$) or $MR^2$ (disc, hoop).

For the rod, put the axis at the end ($d=0.5$): $\frac1{12}+\frac14=\frac13$. For the hoop at its rim ($d=1$): $1+1=2$.

4. Rolling

A wheel of radius $R$ rolls without slipping when its contact point does not slide on the ground (section 8.5.9, pages 210–211). Then the distance the centre moves equals the arc that has touched the ground:

$$s=R\varphi,\qquad v_S=R\omega,\qquad a_S=R\alpha.$$

That is the rolling condition. The script shows rolling as a superposition of a translation with $v_S$ and a rotation with $\omega$ about the centre (figures 8.19–8.21): at the contact point the two velocities cancel ($v_S-\omega R=0$), at the top they add ($2v_S$). Equivalently, at each instant the wheel turns about the contact point with angular velocity $\omega$: every point moves perpendicular to the line to the contact point, with speed $\omega\times$(distance to the contact point).

Try it: the velocities on a rolling wheel

The arrows are the velocities of points on the rim, in the frame of the ground. Press Go to roll the wheel; the orange point traces its path (a cycloid).

Switch between the three views: “rolling” is the arrow-by-arrow sum of the other two. The contact point is at rest, the top moves at $2v_S$.

Energy of a rolling body. With $\omega=\frac{v}R$ (writing $v=v_S$):

$$T=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12J_S\omega^2=\tfrac12\left(m+\frac{J_S}{R^2}\right)v^2.$$

Write $J_S=k\,mR^2$ with the number $k$ from section 3 (hoop 1, disc $\frac12$, solid sphere $\frac25$). Rolling down a height $h$ from rest:

$$mgh=\tfrac12m(1+k)v^2\quad\Rightarrow\quad v=\sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+k}},\qquad a=\frac{g\sin\alpha}{1+k}\ \text{on a slope}.$$

Mass and radius cancel: only $k$, the shape, decides. Static friction makes the rolling possible but does no work, since the contact point doesn't move. (If the body slides without friction, $k$ effectively is $0$: no rotation, fastest of all.)

Recipe: a rolling or rotating body

  1. Find $J$ about the right axis: standard value or integral, then Steiner from the axis through S.
  2. Rolling? Write the rolling condition $v=R\omega$ (with $R$ the radius that touches the ground or the rope).
  3. Speeds: energy conservation with $T=\frac12mv^2+\frac12J_S\omega^2$ (or $\frac12J_P\omega^2$ about a fixed pivot P).
  4. Accelerations and forces: Newton for the centre of mass, $ma_S=\sum F$, plus $J\alpha=\sum M$ about the axis; link them with $a=R\alpha$.
  5. No external torque? Angular momentum is conserved: $J\omega=J'\omega'$ (the dancer of lesson 7, the crane of Exercise 15).

Try it: the rolling race

Five bodies start together on the same slope: a block sliding without friction, a solid sphere, a solid cylinder, a hollow sphere and a hoop. All roll without slipping. Choose the slope angle and press Go.

Change the angle: the order never changes, because every arrival time is proportional to $\sqrt{1+k}$.

Rolling friction (pages 212–213). A real wheel deforms a little, so the normal force acts slightly in front of the lowest point and brakes the rotation. The effect is described like sliding friction, $F_R=\mu_RF_N$, with a coefficient $\mu_R\approx0.001\ldots0.01$, much smaller than $\mu$. That's why wheels exist. The exams almost always say “without rolling friction”.

Worked example 2: the sack on the millstone (script, pages 214–216)

A pulley (a solid disc, mass $m_1$, radius $R$, $J=\frac12m_1R^2$) turns about a fixed axle. A rope wound round it carries a sack of mass $m_2$. Find the acceleration of the sack.

  1. Write Newton's law for the sack and the rotation law for the pulley. Call the rope force $S$.

    Answer

    Sack (down positive): $m_2a=m_2g-S$. Pulley: $J\ddot\varphi=SR$.

  2. Link them and solve.

    Answer

    The rope doesn't slip: $a=R\ddot\varphi$. So $S=\frac{J}{R^2}a=\frac12m_1a$, and $m_2a=m_2g-\frac12m_1a$:

    $$a=\frac{m_2}{m_2+\frac12m_1}\,g.$$

  3. Why is it not simply “the force $m_2g$ on the pulley”?

    Answer

    Because the sack is accelerated too: the rope force $S$ is smaller than $m_2g$ while it falls. The potential energy of the sack is shared between its own kinetic energy and the rotational energy of the pulley. Only for $m_2\ll m_1$ is $S\approx m_2g$ (script, page 216).

Why it's cool. A spinning neutron star is a collapsed star: its radius shrank from about a million kilometres to about ten, so its moment of inertia dropped by a factor of ten billion, and by $J\omega=\text{const}$ it now spins hundreds of times per second. Some of them sweep a radio beam across the earth with every turn, so regular that the first one found, in 1967, was jokingly labelled “LGM-1”, for “little green men”. Angular momentum conservation with a changing $J$, the same law as the pirouette.

5. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed. Exercises 1–14 are multiple choice; 15–20 are parts of long problems.

Exercise 1 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.2(a), 1 point, original)

A rod of length $l$ has the moment of inertia $\frac1{12}ml^2$ about an axis through its middle, perpendicular to the rod. What is its moment of inertia about a parallel axis through one end? (A) $\frac1{12}ml^2$ (B) $\frac14ml^2$ (C) $\frac13ml^2$ (D) $\frac12ml^2$

Solution

C. Steiner with $d=\frac l2$: $\frac1{12}ml^2+m\frac{l^2}4=\frac13ml^2$.

Exercise 2 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.4(a), 1 point, original)

A solid sphere and a hollow sphere have the same mass and radius. Both turn about the same axis, which need not go through the centre. Which is correct? (A) $J_{\text{Voll}}=J_{\text{Hohl}}$ (B) $J_{\text{Voll}}<J_{\text{Hohl}}$ (C) $J_{\text{Voll}}>J_{\text{Hohl}}$ (D) Without the axis no general statement is possible.

Solution

B. About the centre, $\frac25MR^2<\frac23MR^2$. Steiner adds the same $Md^2$ to both for any parallel axis, so the order stays.

Exercise 3 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.4(b), 1 point, original)

Two wheels of radius $R$ and mass $m$ each have four equal holes drilled into them. In wheel A the centres of all four holes are $\frac R2$ from the axis; in wheel B two are $\frac R4$ and two $\frac{3R}4$ from the axis. Each hole removes the mass $m_{\text{Loch}}$. Which is correct? (A) $J_A$ is larger than $J_B$ by $\frac1{16}m_{\text{Loch}}R^2$. (B) … by $\frac18m_{\text{Loch}}R^2$. (C) … by $\frac14m_{\text{Loch}}R^2$. (D) Without the radii of the holes no general statement is possible.

Solution

C. A hole removes $J_{\text{hole},S}+m_{\text{Loch}}d^2$ (Steiner). The first term is the same for all holes and cancels in the difference. Removed in A: $4\cdot\frac{R^2}4=R^2$; in B: $2\cdot\frac{R^2}{16}+2\cdot\frac{9R^2}{16}=\frac54R^2$ (times $m_{\text{Loch}}$). B lost more, so $J_A-J_B=\frac14m_{\text{Loch}}R^2$.

Exercise 4 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.2(d), 1 point, original)

Three flat bodies of the same mass $m$ and thickness turn about an axis perpendicular to the drawing through a marked point: A, a full disc of radius $1.5r$ about its centre; B, a quarter disc of radius $r$ about its corner (the centre of the full circle); C, a full disc of radius $r$ about its centre. Which relation holds? (A) $I_A>I_C=I_B$ (B) $I_A>I_C>I_B$ (C) $I_A=I_C>I_B$ (D) $I_A=I_C=I_B$

Solution

A. $I_A=\frac12m(1.5r)^2=1.125\,mr^2$, $I_C=\frac12mr^2$. For B, every piece of the quarter disc has the same distance distribution from the corner as the full disc from its centre; only the mass is squeezed into a quarter. So $I_B=\frac12mr^2=I_C$. ($J$ depends on how the mass is spread in distance from the axis, not on the angle.)

Exercise 5 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.2(a), 1 point, original)

Two rims of the same mass $m$ turn without friction about fixed axles; $J=mR^2$ (massless spokes). Rim 1 has $R_1=1\,\mathrm m$ and is driven by a tangential force $F_1=4\,\mathrm N$; rim 2 has $R_2=0.5\,\mathrm m$. How large must $F_2$ be for both to have the same angular acceleration? (A) $1\,\mathrm N$ (B) $2\,\mathrm N$ (C) $4\,\mathrm N$ (D) $8\,\mathrm N$

Solution

B. $\alpha=\frac MJ=\frac{FR}{mR^2}=\frac F{mR}$. Equal $\alpha$: $\frac{4\,\mathrm N}{m\cdot1\,\mathrm m}=\frac{F_2}{m\cdot0.5\,\mathrm m}$, so $F_2=2\,\mathrm N$.

Exercise 6 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.2(d), 1 point, original)

Two wheels with radii $r$ and $\frac r2$ are connected by a belt. The small wheel turns with $\omega_k$. What is the angular velocity $\omega_g$ of the large wheel? (A) $\frac{\omega_k}4$ (B) $\frac{\omega_k}2$ (C) $\omega_k$ (D) $2\omega_k$

Solution

B. The belt doesn't slip, so both rims move at the belt's speed: $\omega_gr=\omega_k\frac r2$.

Exercise 7 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.2(c), 1 point, original)

A cylinder of radius $R$ rolls without slipping in $x$ direction with angular velocity $\omega$. What is the velocity $v_x$, relative to the ground, of its momentary contact point? (A) $0$ (B) $\frac{\omega R}2$ (C) $\omega R$ (D) $2\omega R$

Solution

A. That is the rolling condition: $v_S-\omega R=0$ at the contact point (the “Try it” wheel).

Exercise 8 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.2(c), 1 point, original)

A rim rolls without slipping, seen from a frame at rest. Which is correct? (A) Every point of the rim moves equally fast. (B) The centre-of-mass velocity $v$ is independent of the angular velocity $\omega$. (C) Rolling is a superposition of two rotations. (D) At every moment there is a point of the rim at rest.

Solution

D. The contact point. (A) The top moves at $2v$, the contact point at $0$. (B) $v=R\omega$. (C) Rolling is a translation plus a rotation (or one rotation about the contact point).

Exercise 9 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.1(d), 1 point, original)

Two balls of the same mass and radius lie on an inclined plane and are released at the same time from rest. One rolls perfectly (without slipping), the other slides without friction (it doesn't turn). Which reaches the bottom first? (A) The rolling ball. (B) The sliding ball. (C) Both at the same time. (D) It depends on the coefficient of rolling friction.

Solution

B. The sliding ball has $a=g\sin\alpha$; the rolling one only $\frac{g\sin\alpha}{1+2/5}$, because part of its energy goes into rotation.

Exercise 10 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.2(b), 1 point, original)

The two rims of Exercise 5 ($J=mR^2$, radii $1\,\mathrm m$ and $0.5\,\mathrm m$, same mass) are released from rest and roll the same distance down an incline without slipping. Which has the larger centre-of-mass speed at the bottom? (A) Rim 1 (B) Rim 2 (C) Both the same (D) Not enough information

Solution

C. Both are hoops, $k=1$: $v=\sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+1}}=\sqrt{gh}$, independent of $R$ and $m$.

Exercise 11 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.3(a), 1 point, original)

Two balls of the same mass $m$ and radius $r$ roll down a slope without slipping; one is hollow, the other solid. Which is correct? (A) At the bottom both have the same rotational energy. (B) At the bottom both have the same total energy. (C) At the bottom both have the same speed. (D) At the bottom both have the same angular velocity.

Solution

B. Both turned the same $mgh$ into kinetic energy. It is shared differently: the hollow ball ($k=\frac23$) puts more into rotation and is slower, so (A), (C), (D) are false.

Exercise 12 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.3(b), 1 point, original)

You replace the solid ball by another solid ball of the same mass but a larger radius, and race it against the hollow ball. Which arrives first? (A) Both at the same time. (B) The larger solid ball. (C) The hollow ball. (D) No statement without the ratio of the radii.

Solution

B. The radius cancels: $a=\frac{g\sin\alpha}{1+k}$ with $k=\frac25$ for any solid ball, against $k=\frac23$ for the hollow one.

Exercise 13 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.2(b), 1 point, original)

You race tyres down a hill from rest (pure rolling, no air or rolling resistance). How do you make yours arrive first? (A) Use a geometrically identical but heavier tyre. (B) Use a geometrically identical but lighter tyre. (C) Fix a heavy weight exactly on the axis (centre) of your tyre. (D) Nothing helps.

Solution

C. A weight on the axis adds mass $m_G$ but no moment of inertia about the axis. The ratio $k=\frac{J}{mR^2}$ becomes $\frac{J_{\text{tyre}}}{(m+m_G)R^2}$, smaller, so the acceleration $\frac{g\sin\alpha}{1+k}$ grows. (A), (B): scaling the mass leaves $k$ unchanged.

Exercise 14 (FS25, Konzeptfragen 1.3(c)–(e), 3 points, original)

The two discs of lesson 8, Exercise 7: radius $R$, disc 1 (mass $M$) moves right with $v_1$ spinning clockwise with $\omega_1$; disc 2 (mass $2M$) moves left with $v_2$ spinning counter-clockwise with $\omega_2$. They collide centrally and stick, then turn with $\omega'$ counter-clockwise about their common centre of mass. A disc has $J_S=\frac12MR^2$. (c) For which $\frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2}$ do the stuck discs not rotate? (A) $\frac12$ (B) $\frac34$ (C) $\frac43$ (D) $2$ (d) What is $\omega'$ for $v_1=v_2=v$ and $\omega_1=\omega_2=\omega$? (A) $\frac6{25}\omega-\frac32\frac vR$ (B) $\frac23\omega+2\frac vR$ (C) $\frac3{25}\omega$ (D) $-\frac6{25}\omega$ (e) For $v_1=v_2$ and $\omega_1=\omega_2$, what fraction of the rotational energy is lost? (A) $54\,\%$ (B) $75\,\%$ (C) $96\,\%$ (D) $100\,\%$

Solution

The centres move on one line, so the orbital angular momentum about the common centre of mass is zero before and after; only the spins count (counter-clockwise positive).

(c) D. $L=-\frac12MR^2\omega_1+\frac12(2M)R^2\omega_2=0$ gives $\omega_1=2\omega_2$.

(d) C. Before: $L=\frac12MR^2\omega(-1+2)=\frac12MR^2\omega$. After: the centres are $2R$ apart, the common centre of mass is $\frac43R$ from disc 1 and $\frac23R$ from disc 2. Steiner:

$$J'=\tfrac12MR^2+M\left(\tfrac43R\right)^2+MR^2+2M\left(\tfrac23R\right)^2=\tfrac{25}6MR^2.$$

So $\omega'=\frac{\frac12MR^2\omega}{\frac{25}6MR^2}=\frac3{25}\omega$.

(e) C. Before: $\frac12\cdot\frac12MR^2\omega^2+\frac12\cdot MR^2\omega^2$ $=\frac34MR^2\omega^2$. After: $\frac12\cdot\frac{25}6MR^2\cdot\frac9{625}\omega^2$ $=\frac3{100}MR^2\omega^2$, which is $4\,\%$ of before: $96\,\%$ lost.

Exercise 15 (HS23, problem 3 “Kran”, parts (b), (c), (f), 3 points, original)

The crane of lesson 10, Exercise 3: a homogeneous horizontal rod (length $L$, mass $m_K$) turning about a vertical axis at $\frac L4$ from one end, with the counterweight $M=m_K$ (a point mass) at the short end. (b) Compute the moment of inertia $J_K$ of the crane about its axis in terms of $L$ and $m_K$; the rod has $J=\frac1{12}m_KL^2$ about its centre. [1 point] (c) Give its rotational energy at angular velocity $\omega$. [1 point] (f) A mass $m$ hangs from the crane at the middle of the rod. With the motor switched off, the crane turns freely with $\omega$; the mass is moved out to the end of the long arm. Find the new angular velocity $\omega'$. [1 point]

Solution

(b) The rod's centre is $\frac L4$ from the axis: Steiner gives $\frac1{12}m_KL^2+m_K\frac{L^2}{16}=\frac7{48}m_KL^2$. The block adds $M\frac{L^2}{16}=\frac3{48}m_KL^2$. Together $J_K=\frac{10}{48}m_KL^2=\frac5{24}m_KL^2$.

(c) $E_{\text{rot}}=\frac12J_K\omega^2$.

(f) No external torque about the axis, so $L$ is conserved. The middle of the rod is $\frac L4$ from the axis, the end of the long arm $\frac34L$:

$$\left(J_K+m\tfrac{L^2}{16}\right)\omega=\left(J_K+m\tfrac{9L^2}{16}\right)\omega'\ \Rightarrow\ \omega'=\frac{J_K+\frac1{16}mL^2}{J_K+\frac9{16}mL^2}\,\omega.$$

Exercise 16 (HS25, problem 2 “Tontaubenschiessen”, parts (a)–(e), 7 points, original)

A clay-pigeon thrower: a rod-shaped arm (mass $m_{\text{Arm}}$, length $l$) turns about its end M in a horizontal plane; at its other end a clay disc (mass $m_S$, radius $r_S$) is fixed at its centre. A spring transfers the energy $E_s$ to the arm over the angle $\Delta\theta$; then the arm turns without losses until the disc is released. (a) Compute the moment of inertia of arm plus disc about M. Hints: rod about its middle $\frac1{12}mL^2$, disc about its centre $\frac12mr^2$. [2 points] (b) Find the angular velocity $\omega$ after the energy $E_s$ is transferred. [1 point] (c) Assuming constant angular acceleration over $\Delta\theta$, find $\alpha$. [2 points] (d) Give the torque about M during $\Delta\theta$ and afterwards. [1 point] (e) Find the speed of the disc just after it is released. [1 point]

Solution

(a) Arm about its end (Steiner, $d=\frac l2$): $\frac1{12}m_{\text{Arm}}l^2+m_{\text{Arm}}\frac{l^2}4=\frac13m_{\text{Arm}}l^2$. Disc about M (Steiner, $d=l$): $\frac12m_Sr_S^2+m_Sl^2$. Total:

$$J_M=\tfrac13m_{\text{Arm}}l^2+m_S\left(l^2+\tfrac12r_S^2\right).$$

(b) $E_s=\frac12J_M\omega^2$, so $\omega=\sqrt{\frac{2E_s}{J_M}}$.

(c) Constant $\alpha$ from rest: $\omega^2=2\alpha\,\Delta\theta$ (like $v^2=2as$), so $\alpha=\frac{\omega^2}{2\Delta\theta}=\frac{E_s}{J_M\,\Delta\theta}$.

(d) During $\Delta\theta$: $M=J_M\alpha=\frac{E_s}{\Delta\theta}$ (work $=$ torque $\times$ angle, as in the table). Afterwards: $M=0$, the rotation is free.

(e) The centre of the disc moves on a circle of radius $l$: $v_0=\omega l=l\sqrt{\frac{2E_s}{J_M}}$.

Exercise 17 (FS24, problem 2 “Kugel auf Rampe”, parts (a)–(f), 10 points, original)

A solid ball (mass $m$, radius $R$, $I=\frac25mR^2$) is released at the top of a ramp at height $h$. At the bottom ($y=0$) it leaves the ramp at $60^\circ$ above the horizontal (moving to the right, $+x$), flies a parabola and lands at $y=0$ on a cart at rest. (a) First the ball slides without friction. Find the $x$ and $y$ components of its velocity at the end of the ramp. [2 points] (b) From now on it rolls without slipping. Find the components and its angular velocity $\omega$. [2 points] (c) For the rolling ball, find the maximum height of the flight and the time in the air. [2 points] (d) Find the horizontal velocity of the ball's contact point relative to the cart just before it lands. Which way does it point? Hint: $\sqrt{\frac5{14}}-\sqrt{\frac{10}7}=-\sqrt{\frac5{14}}$. [1 point] (e) At the landing a horizontal impulse $P_x$ is transferred at the contact point; afterwards the cart (mass $M$) moves left with speed $v_W$. Use momentum conservation in $x$ to find the ball's $v_x'$. [1 point] (f) Use the angular momentum of the ball to find $\omega'$ after the landing. [2 points]

Solution

(a) $mgh=\frac12mv^2$, $v=\sqrt{2gh}$: $v_x=\frac12\sqrt{2gh}$, $v_y=\frac{\sqrt3}2\sqrt{2gh}$.

(b) $mgh=\frac12\left(1+\frac25\right)mv^2$, $v=\sqrt{\frac{10}7gh}$: $v_x=\frac12\sqrt{\frac{10}7gh}=\sqrt{\frac5{14}gh}$, $v_y=\frac{\sqrt3}2\sqrt{\frac{10}7gh}$, and $\omega=\frac vR=\frac1R\sqrt{\frac{10}7gh}$ (clockwise, since it rolls to the right).

(c) In the air the spin doesn't matter for the path. $h_{\max}=\frac{v_y^2}{2g}=\frac34\cdot\frac{10}7h\cdot\frac12=\frac{15}{28}h$. Time up and down: $t=\frac{2v_y}g=\sqrt{\frac{30h}{7g}}$.

(d) The ball still spins with $\omega$ (no torque in the air) and has $v_x$ unchanged. Its lowest point moves relative to the centre with $\omega R=v$ backwards (clockwise spin): $v_{x,\text{contact}}=v_x-\omega R$ $=\sqrt{\frac5{14}gh}-\sqrt{\frac{10}7gh}$ $=-\sqrt{\frac5{14}gh}$, pointing to the left.

(e) The contact point rubs to the left on the cart, so friction pushes the cart left and the ball right. Momentum in $x$ (right positive): $mv_x=mv_x'-Mv_W$, so $v_x'=v_x+\frac Mmv_W=\sqrt{\frac5{14}gh}+\frac Mmv_W$.

(f) The impulse $P_x=Mv_W$ acts at the lowest point, a distance $R$ below the centre, pointing right: it changes the angular momentum about the centre by $RP_x$ against the clockwise spin. With $I=\frac25mR^2$ (clockwise positive): $I\omega'=I\omega-RMv_W$, so

$$\omega'=\omega-\frac{5Mv_W}{2mR}=\frac1R\sqrt{\tfrac{10}7gh}-\frac{5Mv_W}{2mR}.$$

Exercise 18 (FS26, problem 4 “Die Schaukel”, parts (b) and (d), 4 points, original)

The swing of lesson 10, Exercise 6: a homogeneous half-cylinder (mass $M=\frac12\rho\pi R^2H$, radius $R$, length $H$), its centre of mass $y_S=\frac{4R}{3\pi}$ from the flat base, hanging with its flat base up on a massless rope of length $L$ from the point O. (b) Compute $I_A$ about the axis along $z$ through the centre A of the flat base, with $I=\int\rho r_\perp^2\,dV$ in cylindrical coordinates. [2 points] (d) Compute the moment of inertia $I_O$ of the swing about O (axis parallel to $z$, at distance $L$ above A). [2 points]

Solution

(b) $I_A=\rho\int_0^H\!dz\int_0^\pi\!d\varphi\int_0^Rr^2\,r\,dr$ $=\rho H\pi\frac{R^4}4=\frac12MR^2$: like a full cylinder, since only the distance from the axis matters.

(d) A is not the centre of mass, so go via S. First $I_S=I_A-My_S^2$, then from S to O at distance $L+y_S$:

$$I_O=I_S+M(L+y_S)^2=\tfrac12MR^2+M\left(L^2+2Ly_S\right),\quad y_S=\tfrac{4R}{3\pi}.$$

The tempting $I_A+ML^2$ is wrong: it uses Steiner between two axes that both miss S.

Exercise 19 (HS23, problem 4 “Ringpendel”, part (f), 3 points, original)

A flat ring (annulus) of mass $M$, uniform area density $\rho_0$, inner radius $R-\frac d2$ and outer radius $R+\frac d2$, hangs on a horizontal axis at distance $R$ from its centre. Show that its moment of inertia about this axis is $J_D=\frac14M(d^2+8R^2)$. Hint: polar coordinates and $(a+b)^4-(a-b)^4=8a^3b+8ab^3$.

Solution

About the centre, with $dA=r\,dr\,d\varphi$:

$$J_S=\rho_0\int_0^{2\pi}\!d\varphi\int_{R-d/2}^{R+d/2}r^3\,dr=\frac{\pi\rho_0}2\left[\left(R+\tfrac d2\right)^4-\left(R-\tfrac d2\right)^4\right].$$

With the hint ($a=R$, $b=\frac d2$): the bracket is $4R^3d+Rd^3$. The mass: $M=\pi\rho_0\left[(R+\frac d2)^2-(R-\frac d2)^2\right]$ $=2\pi\rho_0Rd$. So $J_S=\frac M{4Rd}\left(4R^3d+Rd^3\right)$ $=M\left(R^2+\frac{d^2}4\right)$.

Steiner with distance $R$: $J_D=M\left(R^2+\frac{d^2}4\right)+MR^2$ $=\frac14M\left(d^2+8R^2\right)$.

Exercise 20 (FS23, problem 3 “Zylinder als Rampe”, part (b), 6 points, original)

A cube with a square base of side $2r$ and mass $m$ slides down the outside of a large fixed cylinder of radius $R$, its centre at distance $R+r$ from the cylinder's axis; its moment of inertia about its own centre is $J=\frac23mr^2$. As it slides, it turns once round the cylinder's axis per revolution, so its angular velocity is $\omega=\frac{v_\theta}{R+r}$ with $v_\theta$ the speed of its centre. Give its kinetic and rotational energy in terms of $v_\theta$, show $E_{\text{rot}}=\frac13mr^2\frac{v_\theta^2}{(R+r)^2}$, and show with a Taylor expansion that $E_{\text{rot}}$ is negligible against $E_{\text{kin}}$ for $R\gg r$.

Solution

$E_{\text{kin}}=\frac12mv_\theta^2$ and $E_{\text{rot}}=\frac12J\omega^2$ $=\frac12\cdot\frac23mr^2\cdot\frac{v_\theta^2}{(R+r)^2}$ $=\frac13mr^2\frac{v_\theta^2}{(R+r)^2}$.

The ratio: $\frac{E_{\text{rot}}}{E_{\text{kin}}}=\frac23\frac{r^2}{(R+r)^2}=\frac23\left(\frac rR\right)^2\left(1+\frac rR\right)^{-2}$. For $\frac rR\ll1$, the Taylor expansion $(1+x)^{-2}=1-2x+\ldots$ gives $\frac23\left(\frac rR\right)^2+O\!\left(\left(\frac rR\right)^3\right)$: there is no zeroth- or first-order term, so to first order the ratio is $0$.

6. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Why a cat lands on its feet. A falling cat has no angular momentum and nothing to push against, yet it turns over in mid-air. It does it with changing moments of inertia: it pulls its front legs in (small $J$ in front) and stretches its back legs out (large $J$ behind), then twists. The front half turns a lot, the back half a little the other way, total $L=0$. Then it swaps: front legs out, back legs in, and twists the back half round. Two half-turns with net rotation and zero angular momentum throughout. Astronauts learn the same trick to turn in space.

The billiard ball that rolls back (script, pages 218–220). Hit a ball $\frac23R$ below its centre and it gets a backward spin: it slides forward, friction slows its sliding and reverses its spin, and only after a while does it roll. Snooker players call it a “stun” or “screw” shot.
