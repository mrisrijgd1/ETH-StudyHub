Serie 3, explained

The valve on a rolling wheel, the swimmer and the dry bag, the hammer throw, and Atwood's machine. For every task: the idea in plain words, a sketch, up to three hints that each give away a little more, then the solution with every step explained next to what you write, and the trap people fall into.

How to use this page.

  1. Read the task and try it on paper for 10 minutes. Draw a sketch first: in physics it is half the work.
  2. Stuck on where to start? Read The idea and open You need.
  3. Still stuck? Open Hint 1, try again, then Hint 2, then Hint 3. Hint 3 is almost the whole solution.
  4. Only then open the solution. The left column says why each step is there, the right column is what you write.

The solutions follow the official Lösung (Serie 3, Physik I HS26). The task texts are short English summaries: keep the official sheet next to you for the exact wording. With the StudyHub connector, your own AI can check your attempt before you look.

Aufgabe 1 · The rolling wheel

A scooter's back wheel has a red light on the valve and a green light on the hub. Radius $R=\frac16\,\text m\approx0.167\,$m, one turn takes $T=0.4\,$s. At $t=0$ the valve touches the ground. The scooter rolls in the $+x$-direction.

  1. Move along with the scooter so that the green light is at rest for you (the scooter's frame). Draw the valve's height $y(t)$ over time. What kind of curve is it? Name characteristic points.
  2. Give the valve's position $\vec r(t)$ in the scooter's frame.
  3. Find $\vec v(t)$ and $\vec a(t)$. Show $\vec r\perp\vec v$ and $\vec v\perp\vec a$. Compute $|\vec r|$, $|\vec v|$, $|\vec a|$ and show they don't depend on time.
  4. Back in the frame of the road: write the valve's position as $\vec r(t)=\vec r\,'(t)+\vec R(t)$ (valve relative to hub plus hub position) and sketch the path.
The idea

Two observers, two simple motions. Riding along with the scooter, the valve just goes round a circle at constant speed. Standing on the road, you see that circle carried forward by the hub. So the road-frame motion is the sum: circle plus straight line. Rolling without slipping links the two: in one turn the hub moves one circumference, so the hub speed is $v=\frac{2\pi R}{T}=\omega R$. The valve's path on the road is a cycloid, a row of arches that touch the ground with a sharp tip.

In the scooter's frame the valve's height is a (negative) cosine: on the ground at $t=0, 0.4\,\text s, 0.8\,\text s,\dots$; at the top at $0.2\,\text s, 0.6\,\text s,\dots$; it rises in the first half of each turn and falls in the second.

On the road the valve draws arches (a cycloid). Each arch is one turn long, $2\pi R$, and touches the ground in a sharp tip: at that instant the valve is at rest.

You need

Uniform circular motion: $\varphi(t)=\omega t$ with $\omega=\frac{2\pi}T$; $|\vec v|=\omega R$, $|\vec a|=\omega^2R$ pointing to the centre (Physics lesson 1). Two vectors are perpendicular when their dot product is $0$. Change of frame by adding positions: Physics lesson 4.

Hint 1

a), b) Put the origin at the hub. At $t=0$ the valve is at $(0,-R)$. Which way does it turn if the scooter rolls to $+x$? The bottom point of a forward-rolling wheel moves backwards relative to the hub. Write $\vec r(t)=R\,(\,?\,,\,?\,)$ with $\sin\omega t$ and $\cos\omega t$ so that $t=0$ gives $(0,-R)$.

Hint 2

$\vec r(t)=R\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\omega t\\-\cos\omega t\end{pmatrix}$. Differentiate component by component for $\vec v$ and once more for $\vec a$. For the dot products, multiply matching components and add: the terms cancel in pairs.

Hint 3

d) The hub moves along the ground at the scooter's speed: $\vec R(t)=\begin{pmatrix}vt\\0\end{pmatrix}$ with $v=\frac{2\pi R}T=\omega R$ (one circumference per turn, no slipping). Add $\vec r(t)$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Origin at the hub. Going round a circle at constant speed, the height goes up and down like a cosine.

$y(t)=-R\cos(\omega t)$, a cosine curve. Valve on the ground at $t=0,\,T,\,2T,\dots$ ($T=0.4\,$s), at the top at $t=\frac T2=0.2\,$s; it rises for $0<t<0.2\,$s and falls for $0.2\,\text s<t<0.4\,$s.

b) Start at the bottom; rolling to $+x$ means the wheel turns clockwise as seen in the sketch, so the valve first moves to $-x$ relative to the hub.

$$\vec r(t)=R\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\omega t\\-\cos\omega t\end{pmatrix},\quad\omega=\frac{2\pi}{T}.$$ Check: $\vec r(0)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\-R\end{pmatrix}$.

c) Velocity and acceleration: differentiate each component (chain rule gives a factor $\omega$ each time).

$$\vec v=\omega R\begin{pmatrix}-\cos\omega t\\\sin\omega t\end{pmatrix},$$ $$\vec a=\omega^2R\begin{pmatrix}\sin\omega t\\\cos\omega t\end{pmatrix}.$$

Perpendicular means dot product zero. The two products cancel each other.

$$\begin{aligned}\vec r\cdot\vec v&=\omega R^2(\sin\omega t\cos\omega t\\&\qquad-\cos\omega t\sin\omega t)=0,\end{aligned}$$ and in the same way $\vec v\cdot\vec a=0$.

Lengths: $\sin^2+\cos^2=1$ removes the time.

$|\vec r|=R\sqrt{\sin^2\omega t+\cos^2\omega t}=R=0.167\,$m,
$|\vec v|=\omega R=\frac{2\pi}{0.4\,\text s}\cdot0.167\,\text m\approx2.6\,\frac{\text m}{\text s}$,
$|\vec a|=\omega^2R\approx41.2\,\frac{\text m}{\text s^2}$.

d) Back to the road: the hub moves forward at the scooter's speed. No slipping means one circumference per turn.

$\vec R(t)=\begin{pmatrix}vt\\0\end{pmatrix}$, $\quad v=\dfrac{2\pi R}{T}=\omega R$.

Add the two motions. The result is a cycloid (see the second sketch).

$$\vec r_{\text{road}}(t)=R\begin{pmatrix}\omega t-\sin\omega t\\-\cos\omega t\end{pmatrix}.$$

Sense check. At $t=0$ the road-frame velocity is $\omega R\,(1-\cos0,\ \sin0)=(0,0)$: the point touching the ground is momentarily at rest. That is what “rolling without slipping” means. The official solution plots the curve with WolframAlpha: parametric plot (R*(omega*t - sin(omega*t)), -R*cos(omega*t)) from -0.5 to 1 where R = 0.167, omega = 2*pi/0.4.

Trap

Getting the sense of rotation wrong: with $\vec r=R(\sin\omega t,-\cos\omega t)$ the wheel would roll backwards. Check with the contact point: in the road frame its velocity must be $0$, so relative to the hub it must move backwards at speed $v$. Second trap: drawing the road-frame path as a sine wave. It has sharp tips on the ground, not round dips.

Aufgabe 2 · Relative motion: the swimmer and the dry bag

A swimmer swimming upstream loses her dry bag exactly under a bridge; the bag drifts downstream with the water. She notices only a quarter of an hour later, turns round and swims back to the bag. She swims at about $4\,$km/h relative to the water; the water flows at about $3\,$km/h. How long was the bag on its own? Tip: you can use the frame of the flowing water or the frame of the bank. Which is easier?

The idea

Ride along with the water, as if you were on a raft. In that frame the bag doesn't move at all, and the swimmer swims at $4\,$km/h, first away from the bag, then back. Same speed both ways, so going back takes exactly as long as going away: $15+15=30$ minutes. The current never enters, because it carries the swimmer and the bag equally. The bank frame gives the same answer with more work.

Bank frame, bridge at $x=0$. Against the current the swimmer only makes $4-3=1\,$km/h; with it she makes $4+3=7\,$km/h. The lines meet at $t=0.5\,$h.

You need

Velocities add when you change frame: speed relative to the bank $=$ speed relative to the water $+$ speed of the water (Galilean transformation, Physics lesson 4). Constant velocity: $x(t)=x_0+vt$.

Hint 1

Choose the frame in which as many things as possible are at rest. In the water's frame, how fast does the bag move? How fast does the swimmer move, upstream and downstream?

Hint 2

In the water's frame: the bag rests, the swimmer moves at $4\,$km/h away from it for $15\,$min. How long does the way back take at the same speed?

Hint 3

To check in the bank frame: bag $x_B=3t$; swimmer $x_S=-1\cdot t$ until $t_u=0.25\,$h, then $x_S=-0.25+7(t-t_u)$ (km, h). Set $x_B=x_S$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Pick the frame where the bag is at rest: the flowing water. The current then disappears from the problem.

Frame of the flowing water: the bag rests; the swimmer moves at $4\,$km/h, first away from the bag, then towards it.

Same speed, same distance, so the same time back.

Away for $\frac14\,$h, back for $\frac14\,$h. The bag was on its own for $\frac12\,$h $=30\,$min.

Another way (from the official solution): the bank frame

Origin under the bridge, $+x$ downstream. Add the water's speed to each relative speed.

$v_{\text{bag}}=+3$, $\ v_{\text{up}}=-4+3=-1$, $\ v_{\text{down}}=+4+3=+7$ (km/h).

Where she turns.

$x_S(t_u)=v_{\text{up}}t_u=-0.25\,$km, with $t_u=0.25\,$h.

She finds the bag when both are at the same place at the same time.

$$\begin{aligned}v_{\text{bag}}t_f&=v_{\text{up}}t_u\\&\quad+v_{\text{down}}(t_f-t_u)\end{aligned}$$ so $t_f\,(v_{\text{bag}}-v_{\text{down}})=t_u\,(v_{\text{up}}-v_{\text{down}})$, i.e. $$\begin{aligned}-4\,t_f&=-8\,t_u\\ \Rightarrow t_f&=2t_u=0.5\,\text h.\end{aligned}$$

Trap

Using $4\,$km/h relative to the bank, or forgetting that the bag drifts too. In the bank frame the speeds are $1\,$km/h up and $7\,$km/h down, not $4$. The clean way out is to choose the water's frame, where the answer $2\times15\,$min is obvious.

Aufgabe 3 · Betty Heidler's hammer throw, revisited

Look at the hammer throw: final distance $75\,$m, diameter of the circular path $2\,$m.

  1. Describe the motion in words (circular motion, accelerated circular motion, release) using Newton's first law.
  2. From the distance, find the release speed. Ignore the release height.
  3. Which mean angular acceleration does she need to reach this speed in three turns? Which radial acceleration must she supply just before release?
The idea

Newton's first law: without a force, the hammer would fly straight on at constant speed. While she turns, she has to pull it inwards all the time (otherwise it would leave the circle), and she also speeds it up along the circle. When she lets go, the inward pull stops, and the hammer flies off along the tangent, as a projectile. So b) is a projectile problem run backwards (distance → speed), and c) is uniformly accelerated circular motion: like the constant-acceleration formulas, but with angles.

Top view, just before release. The radial (centripetal) acceleration keeps the hammer on the circle; the tangential one makes it faster. Let go, and only gravity is left.

You need

Newton's first law; projectile range on level ground $x=\frac{v_0^2}{g}\sin2\alpha$ (Serie 2, Aufgabe 2); circular motion in polar coordinates: $v=R\dot\varphi$, $a_{\text{radial}}=-R\dot\varphi^2=-\frac{v^2}R$ (Physics lesson 1). Constant angular acceleration $\alpha$: $\dot\varphi=\alpha t$, $\varphi=\frac12\alpha t^2$ when starting from rest.

Hint 1

a) Ask at each stage: which force acts, and what would happen without it? b) No angle is given: take the best angle, $45^\circ$ (that is what the official number corresponds to). Use the range formula and solve for $v_0$.

Hint 2

c) Three turns is the angle $\varphi=3\cdot2\pi$. Starting from rest with constant $\alpha$: $\varphi=\frac12\alpha t^2$ and $\dot\varphi=\alpha t$. Eliminate the time $t$ between these two.

Hint 3

At release the speed is $v_0=R\dot\varphi=R\alpha t$ with $t=\sqrt{\frac{12\pi}\alpha}$. So $v_0^2=R^2\alpha\cdot12\pi$. Solve for $\alpha$. The radial acceleration is $\frac{v_0^2}R$ with $R=1\,$m (radius, not diameter).

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Go through the stages with the first law: “no force ⇒ straight line, constant speed”.

While she holds the hammer it moves on an accelerated circle: she must pull it inwards (radial acceleration) to keep it on the circle, and she speeds it up along the circle (tangential acceleration). At release the inward force is gone: by the first law the hammer moves on along the tangent with constant horizontal velocity. Vertically, gravity bends the path into a projectile curve (otherwise it would fly forever).

b) Projectile on level ground: write $x$ and $y$ at landing.

$x_L=v_0\cos\alpha\,t_L=75\,$m, $\quad0=v_0\sin\alpha\,t_L-\frac12gt_L^2$.

Eliminate the landing time: take it from the $x$-equation and put it into the $y$-equation.

$$\begin{aligned}t_L&=\frac{x_L}{v_0\cos\alpha},\\ v_0&=\sqrt{\frac{g\,x_L}{2\cos\alpha\sin\alpha}}.\end{aligned}$$

At the best angle $45^\circ$, $2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha=1$.

$v_0=\sqrt{g\,x_L}=\sqrt{9.81\cdot75}\,\frac{\text m}{\text s}\approx27\,\frac{\text m}{\text s}$.

c) Constant angular acceleration from rest, three turns.

$\varphi(t_A)=3\cdot2\pi=\frac12\alpha t_A^2\ \Rightarrow\ t_A=\sqrt{\dfrac{12\pi}{\alpha}}$.

The speed at release must be $v_0$. In polar coordinates $v=R\dot\varphi$ with $\dot\varphi=\alpha t$.

$$\begin{aligned}v_0&=R\,\alpha\,t_A=R\sqrt{12\pi\,\alpha}\\ \Rightarrow\ \alpha&=\frac{v_0^2}{12\pi R^2}=19.3\,\frac1{\text s^2}.\end{aligned}$$ ($R=1\,$m.)

Radial acceleration just before release: $R\dot\varphi^2=\frac{v_0^2}R$, pointing to the centre.

$$\begin{aligned}\vec a_{\text{radial}}&=-R\dot\varphi^2\,\hat e_r=-\frac{v_0^2}{R}\,\hat e_r\\&\approx-729\,\frac{\text m}{\text s^2}\,\hat e_r.\end{aligned}$$

Sense check. $729\,$m/s² is about $74\,g$: the hammer (about $4\,$kg) pulls on her hands with roughly $3\,$kN. The tangential acceleration, $R\alpha\approx19\,$m/s², is tiny by comparison. Almost all of her effort goes into keeping the hammer on the circle.

Trap

Using the diameter ($2\,$m) as $R$: that makes $\alpha$ four times too small and the radial acceleration half as big. Also: “mean angular acceleration” is $\alpha$ in $1/\text s^2$, not a linear acceleration in m/s². And in a), saying the hammer flies off radially outwards: it flies along the tangent.

Aufgabe 4 · Atwood's machine

Masses $M=5.2\,$kg and $m$ hang on the two ends of a rope over a pulley; at $t=0$ both are at rest at the same height. Use $g=10\,$m/s². After $1\,$s the heavier mass $M$ is $20\,$cm lower. No friction, massless rope. The setup can be used to find $m$ from the acceleration.

  1. Give the resulting force. (Reduce the system to one direction and treat the two masses joined by the rope as one system.)
  2. Which total mass is accelerated? Write the equation of motion.
  3. Use it to find the acceleration of the system.
  4. What is $m$?
The idea

Straighten the rope in your head: the two masses become one train on a straight track. Gravity pulls $M$ forwards with $Mg$ and $m$ backwards with $mg$, so the net pull is $(M-m)g$. The rope tension pulls on both ends equally and cancels inside the train. But the train that has to be accelerated weighs $M+m$. So $a=g\,\frac{M-m}{M+m}$. Measure $a$ from “$20\,$cm in $1\,$s” and solve for $m$.

The same tension $T$ pulls up on both masses. Along the rope, the net force is $Mg-mg$, and it has to accelerate both masses.

You need

Newton's second law $F_{\text{net}}=m_{\text{total}}\,a$ (Physics lesson 2). A massless rope over a frictionless pulley has the same tension $T$ everywhere. Constant acceleration from rest: $s=\frac12at^2$.

Hint 1

a) List the forces on each mass: weight down, tension up. Take “$M$ moves down, $m$ moves up” as the positive direction along the rope. What is left when you add the forces along that direction?

Hint 2

b) The net force must accelerate both masses, since they move together. c) Start from rest: $0.2\,\text m=\frac12a(1\,\text s)^2$.

Hint 3

d) From $a(M+m)=g(M-m)$, collect the $m$-terms on one side: $m(a+g)=M(g-a)$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Forces along the rope, positive = “$M$ down, $m$ up”. The tension appears on both with opposite signs and drops out.

$$\begin{aligned}F_{\text{res}}&=(Mg-T)-(mg-T)\\&=(M-m)\,g.\end{aligned}$$

b) Both masses move together, so the whole $M+m$ is accelerated.

$(M-m)\,g=(M+m)\,a\ \Rightarrow\ a=g\,\dfrac{M-m}{M+m}$.

c) Measure $a$ from the motion: from rest, $s=\frac12at^2$.

$a=\dfrac{2s}{t^2}=\dfrac{2\cdot0.2\,\text m}{(1\,\text s)^2}=0.4\,\dfrac{\text m}{\text s^2}$ (down for $M$, up for $m$).

d) Solve the equation of motion for $m$.

$$\begin{aligned}m&=M\,\frac{g-a}{g+a}\\&=5.2\,\text{kg}\cdot\frac{9.6}{10.4}=4.8\,\text{kg}.\end{aligned}$$

Sense check. The masses are nearly equal, so the acceleration is tiny ($0.4\,$m/s² instead of $10$). That is the point of Atwood's machine: it slows free fall down enough to measure it. The tension is $T=m(g+a)\approx49.9\,$N, between $mg=48\,$N and $Mg=52\,$N, as it must be.

Trap

Dividing the net force by $M$ (or $M-m$) instead of $M+m$: the rope makes both masses move, so both have to be accelerated. Second trap: setting the tension equal to $Mg$. If it were, $M$ would not accelerate.
