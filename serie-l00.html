Serie 0, explained: Abelian groups

Six structures, one checklist: a trivial group, the rationals under multiplication, integer vectors, sequences and Fibonacci sequences, sets under symmetric difference, and the clock $\mathbb Z_m$. For every task: the idea, hints on the hard ones, the solution with every step explained next to what you write, and the trap.

🌧
Bad day? Do Aufgabe 2 only: write out the five checks (closed, associative, commutative, neutral element, inverses) for integer vectors. That counts. Next time, simply carry on from here: a missed or light day is not a restart.
You're done when
you can (1) list the five things to check for an abelian group and check each one in a new example; (2) show that a subset of a known group is again a group by checking only closure, the neutral element and inverses; (3) prove associativity of the symmetric difference by counting in how many sets an element lies; (4) explain why the sum on $\mathbb Z_m$ is well defined (exactly one remainder).
Time
About 90 minutes: 20 for Aufgaben 0 to 2, 25 for Aufgabe 3, 45 for Aufgaben 4 and 5.
Lessons
Lesson 1, section 3 (groups) and section 4 (the clock $\mathbb Z_m$); lesson 3, section 2 (sets).

Aufgabe 0 · The trivial group

Show: if $G:=\{0\}$ and $0\circ0:=0$, then $(G,\circ)$ is an abelian group.

In short: One element, one product. Check the list anyway.

The idea

With only one element there is only one pair to multiply, so every rule of the checklist turns into the single equation $0\circ0=0$. The point of the task is to see the checklist once in its simplest form.

You need

Lesson 1, section 3: an abelian group is a set $G$ with an operation $\circ$ that is (0) a 2-place operation on $G$ ($x\circ y\in G$), (1) associative, (2) commutative, with (3) a neutral element and (4) an inverse for every element.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

First check that $\circ$ really is an operation on $G$: the only pair is $(0,0)$.

Since $G=\{0\}$, the only pair of elements is $(0,0)$, and $0\circ0=0\in G$. So $\circ$ is a 2-place operation on $G$.

Associativity: both bracketings reduce to $0\circ0$.

$(0\circ0)\circ0=0\circ0=0=0\circ0=0\circ(0\circ0)$.

Commutativity: both arguments are the same element.

$0\circ0=0\circ0$.

Neutral element and inverse: $0$ does both jobs.

$0$ is neutral, since $0\circ0=0$. And $0$ is its own inverse, since $0\circ0=0$ is the neutral element. Hence $(G,\circ)$ is an abelian group (the trivial group). ∎

Trap

Forgetting the first check. “Operation on $G$” means the result lands in $G$ again; in every later task this is the step that can actually fail.

Aufgabe 1 · The rationals under multiplication

(a) Show that $(\mathbb Q^+,\cdot)$ is an abelian group, where $\mathbb Q^+:=\{q\in\mathbb Q: q>0\}$.

(b) Show that $(\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is an abelian group.

In short: Multiplication of positive (or non-zero) fractions: show it stays inside, and that $\frac ab$ has the inverse $\frac ba$.

The idea

Associativity and commutativity come for free: they hold for all rationals, so in particular for the ones in our subset. What really has to be shown is that the subset is closed (a product of two positive numbers is positive; a product of two non-zero numbers is non-zero) and that the inverse $\frac ba$ of $\frac ab$ is again in the subset.

You need

Lesson 1, section 3: an abelian group is a set $G$ with an operation $\circ$ that is (0) a 2-place operation on $G$ ($x\circ y\in G$), (1) associative, (2) commutative, with (3) a neutral element and (4) an inverse for every element. For (b): a product of non-zero numbers is non-zero (lesson 4, section 4, calculation rules in a field).

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

(a) Closure: product of two positive rationals.

(a) If $p,q\in\mathbb Q^+$, then $p\cdot q\in\mathbb Q$ and $p\cdot q>0$ as a product of positive numbers, so $p\cdot q\in\mathbb Q^+$.

The laws that hold in all of $\mathbb Q$ hold in the subset.

For all $p,q,r\in\mathbb Q^+\subseteq\mathbb Q$: $(p\cdot q)\cdot r=p\cdot(q\cdot r)$ and $p\cdot q=q\cdot p$, since multiplication of rationals is associative and commutative.

Neutral element: $1$, and check it lies in the subset.

$1>0$, so $1\in\mathbb Q^+$, and $1\cdot q=q$ for all $q\in\mathbb Q^+$.

Inverse: flip the fraction, and check it is still positive.

Let $q\in\mathbb Q^+$, $q=\frac ab$ with positive $a,b\in\mathbb Z$. Put $q^{-1}:=\frac ba$. Then $q^{-1}\in\mathbb Q^+$ and $q\cdot q^{-1}=\frac ab\cdot\frac ba=1$. So $(\mathbb Q^+,\cdot)$ is an abelian group.

(b) Closure is the only real step: suppose a product were $0$ and divide.

(b) Let $p,q\in\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}$. If $p\cdot q=0$, then, since $p\ne0$, $q=\frac1p\cdot(p\cdot q)=\frac1p\cdot0=0$, a contradiction. So $p\cdot q\in\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}$.

Everything else as in (a).

Associativity and commutativity carry over from $\mathbb Q$. $1\in\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}$ is neutral. For $q=\frac ab$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}$, $q^{-1}:=\frac ba\in\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}$ satisfies $q\cdot q^{-1}=1$. So $(\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is an abelian group. ∎

Trap

Taking all of $\mathbb Q$: $(\mathbb Q,\cdot)$ is not a group, because $0$ has no inverse. That is exactly why (a) and (b) remove $0$ first. Also, in (a) writing $q^{-1}=\frac1q$ without saying why it is positive.

Aufgabe 2 · Vectors with integer entries

Let $V$ be the set of all 4-tuples $(a,b,c,d)^T$ with $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb Z$, with entrywise addition $+_V$: $(a,b,c,d)^T+_V(a',b',c',d')^T:=(a+a',b+b',c+c',d+d')^T$. Show that $(V,+_V)$ is an abelian group.

In short: Add four integers at once. Every law is the law in $\mathbb Z$, entry by entry.

The idea

Two vectors are equal exactly when all four entries agree. So every rule we want for vectors can be checked entry by entry, where it is a rule we already know for integers. Write $v_i$ for the $i$-th entry; then $(v+_Vw)_i=v_i+w_i$.

You need

Lesson 1, section 3: an abelian group is a set $G$ with an operation $\circ$ that is (0) a 2-place operation on $G$ ($x\circ y\in G$), (1) associative, (2) commutative, with (3) a neutral element and (4) an inverse for every element.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Name the entries so that “entry by entry” can be written down.

For $v\in V$ write $v_1,\dots,v_4\in\mathbb Z$ for its entries. Two vectors $v,w$ are equal iff $v_i=w_i$ for $i=1,\dots,4$, and $(v+_Vw)_i=v_i+w_i$.

Closure: sums of integers are integers.

For $v,w\in V$, every $v_i+w_i\in\mathbb Z$, so $v+_Vw\in V$.

Associativity: in each entry it is associativity in $\mathbb Z$.

For $u,v,w\in V$ and each $i$: $((u+_Vv)+_Vw)_i=(u_i+v_i)+w_i=u_i+(v_i+w_i)=(u+_V(v+_Vw))_i$. All four entries agree, so $(u+_Vv)+_Vw=u+_V(v+_Vw)$.

Commutativity, the same way.

$(v+_Vw)_i=v_i+w_i=w_i+v_i=(w+_Vv)_i$ for each $i$, so $v+_Vw=w+_Vv$.

Neutral element: the zero vector, which lies in $V$.

$0_V:=(0,0,0,0)^T\in V$ and $(0_V+_Vv)_i=0+v_i=v_i$, so $0_V+_Vv=v$.

Inverse: negate every entry.

For $v\in V$ let $-v:=(-v_1,-v_2,-v_3,-v_4)^T\in V$. Then $(v+_V(-v))_i=v_i-v_i=0$, so $v+_V(-v)=0_V$. Hence $(V,+_V)$ is an abelian group. ∎

Trap

Writing “$(u+v)+w=u+(v+w)$ because addition is associative” for vectors. That is the statement to prove; the reason is associativity in $\mathbb Z$, used in each entry. Say which addition you mean.

Aufgabe 3 · Sequences and Fibonacci sequences

Let $S$ be the set of all real sequences $(a_0,a_1,\dots)$, with termwise addition $(a_n)+_S(b_n):=(a_n+b_n)$. Let $\mathrm{Fib}\subseteq S$ be the set of sequences $(f_n)$ with $f_{i+2}=f_{i+1}+f_i$ for all $i\in\mathbb N$.

(a) Show that $(S,+_S)$ is an abelian group.

(b) Show that $(\mathrm{Fib},+_S)$ is an abelian group. Official hint: with (a) it is enough to show that $+_S$ is an operation on $\mathrm{Fib}$.

In short: (a) is Aufgabe 2 with infinitely many entries. (b): a sum of two Fibonacci-type sequences obeys the same recursion.

The idea

(a) works exactly like the integer vectors, term by term. For (b), $\mathrm{Fib}$ sits inside the group $S$. Laws like associativity hold for all of $S$, so they hold inside $\mathrm{Fib}$ for free. What can fail is staying inside: is the sum of two sequences with $f_{i+2}=f_{i+1}+f_i$ again such a sequence? And are the zero sequence and $-f$ in $\mathrm{Fib}$? This “check only closure, zero and inverses” pattern is the subgroup test, and it returns as the subspace test in lesson 5.

You need

Lesson 1, section 3: an abelian group is a set $G$ with an operation $\circ$ that is (0) a 2-place operation on $G$ ($x\circ y\in G$), (1) associative, (2) commutative, with (3) a neutral element and (4) an inverse for every element. Subgroups: lesson 1, section 5.

Hint 1

For (b), let $h_n:=f_n+g_n$ and compute $h_{i+2}$ using the recursions for $f$ and for $g$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

(a) Term by term, like Aufgabe 2.

(a) Two sequences are equal iff they agree in every term, and $(a_n)+_S(b_n)=(a_n+b_n)$. Sums of reals are real, so $+_S$ is an operation on $S$. For each $n$: $(a_n+b_n)+c_n=a_n+(b_n+c_n)$ and $a_n+b_n=b_n+a_n$, so $+_S$ is associative and commutative.

Neutral element and inverses, termwise.

The zero sequence $0=(0,0,\dots)$ lies in $S$ and $0+_S(a_n)=(a_n)$. For $(a_n)\in S$, also $(-a_n)\in S$ and $(a_n)+_S(-a_n)=(0)=0$. So $(S,+_S)$ is an abelian group.

(b) Associativity and commutativity hold in all of $S$, hence in the subset. What is left: closure, zero, inverses.

(b) $\mathrm{Fib}\subseteq S$, so by (a) $+_S$ is associative and commutative on $\mathrm{Fib}$. It remains to show that $+_S$ is an operation on $\mathrm{Fib}$ and that $0$ and the inverses lie in $\mathrm{Fib}$.

Closure: add the two recursions.

Let $(f_n),(g_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$ and $h_n:=f_n+g_n$. For every $i\in\mathbb N$: $$\begin{aligned}h_{i+2}&=f_{i+2}+g_{i+2}\\&=(f_{i+1}+f_i)+(g_{i+1}+g_i)\\&=(f_{i+1}+g_{i+1})+(f_i+g_i)\\&=h_{i+1}+h_i,\end{aligned}$$ using associativity and commutativity in $\mathbb R$. So $(h_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$.

Zero sequence: $0=0+0$.

The zero sequence satisfies $0=0+0$ in every term, so $0\in\mathrm{Fib}$; it is neutral in $S$, hence in $\mathrm{Fib}$.

Inverses: negate the recursion.

If $(f_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$, then $-f_{i+2}=-(f_{i+1}+f_i)=(-f_{i+1})+(-f_i)$, so $(-f_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$, and by (a) it is the inverse of $(f_n)$. Hence $(\mathrm{Fib},+_S)$ is an abelian group. ∎

Trap

Checking the recursion only for the first few terms, for example $h_2=h_1+h_0$. The recursion must hold for every $i$, so compute with a general $i$. And forgetting that $0$ and $-f$ must lie in $\mathrm{Fib}$ too, not only in $S$.

Aufgabe 4 · Sets under symmetric difference

For a set $M$ let $\mathcal P(M)$ be the set of all subsets of $M$ (including $\emptyset$ and $M$). For $x,y\in\mathcal P(M)$ let $$\begin{aligned}x\mathbin\triangle y&:=(x\setminus y)\cup(y\setminus x)\\&=(x\cup y)\setminus(x\cap y).\end{aligned}$$ Show: if $M$ is non-empty, then $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle)$ is an abelian group.

In short: “In exactly one of the two sets” is an addition on subsets. The empty set is zero, every set is its own inverse, and associativity is the real work.

The idea

Think of $x\mathbin\triangle y$ as a light switch: an element $t$ is in $x\mathbin\triangle y$ when it is in exactly one of $x,y$. Applying a second $\triangle$ flips once more. So $t$ lies in $(x\mathbin\triangle y)\mathbin\triangle z$ exactly when it lies in an odd number of the three sets, and “odd number of $x,y,z$” does not care about order or brackets. That one sentence is the whole associativity proof; the rest of the task is quick.

Shaded: the points that lie in an odd number of the sets $x,y,z$ (exactly one, or all three). That is $(x\mathbin\triangle y)\mathbin\triangle z$, and the picture is symmetric in $x,y,z$.

You need

Lesson 1, section 3: an abelian group is a set $G$ with an operation $\circ$ that is (0) a 2-place operation on $G$ ($x\circ y\in G$), (1) associative, (2) commutative, with (3) a neutral element and (4) an inverse for every element. Sets, difference, union, and equality of sets by “same elements” (lesson 3, section 2).

Hint 1

First note: $t\in x\mathbin\triangle y$ iff $t$ lies in exactly one of $x$ and $y$. Then the neutral element and the inverses are one line each. Which set changes nothing? What is $x\mathbin\triangle x$?

Hint 2

For associativity, fix $t\in M$ and split into two cases: $t\in z$ and $t\notin z$. In each case, when is $t\in(x\mathbin\triangle y)\mathbin\triangle z$?

Hint 3

Show: $t\in(x\mathbin\triangle y)\mathbin\triangle z$ iff $t$ lies in an odd number of the sets $x,y,z$. The right-hand side does not depend on the order, so with commutativity, $x\mathbin\triangle(y\mathbin\triangle z)=(y\mathbin\triangle z)\mathbin\triangle x$ has the same elements.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Closure: both differences lie inside $x\cup y\subseteq M$.

For $x,y\in\mathcal P(M)$: $x\mathbin\triangle y=(x\setminus y)\cup(y\setminus x)\subseteq x\cup y\subseteq M$, so $x\mathbin\triangle y\in\mathcal P(M)$.

The working tool: membership in $x\mathbin\triangle y$ in words. The two parts cannot overlap.

For all $x,y\in\mathcal P(M)$ and $t\in M$: $$\begin{aligned}&t\in x\mathbin\triangle y\iff\\&t\text{ lies in exactly one of }x,y.\quad(*)\end{aligned}$$ Indeed $t\in x\setminus y$ means $t\in x,t\notin y$, and $t\in y\setminus x$ means $t\in y,t\notin x$; these cases exclude each other.

Commutativity: union is commutative.

$x\mathbin\triangle y=(x\setminus y)\cup(y\setminus x)=(y\setminus x)\cup(x\setminus y)=y\mathbin\triangle x$.

Neutral element: the empty set changes nothing.

$\emptyset\in\mathcal P(M)$ and $\emptyset\mathbin\triangle x=(\emptyset\setminus x)\cup(x\setminus\emptyset)=\emptyset\cup x=x$.

Inverses: every set cancels itself.

$x\mathbin\triangle x=(x\setminus x)\cup(x\setminus x)=\emptyset$, so each $x$ is its own inverse.

Associativity, step 1: describe $(x\mathbin\triangle y)\mathbin\triangle z$ by counting. Case $t\in z$.

Claim: for all $x,y,z\in\mathcal P(M)$ and $t\in M$, $$\begin{aligned}&t\in(x\mathbin\triangle y)\mathbin\triangle z\iff\\&t\text{ is in an odd number}\\&\text{of the sets }x,y,z.\quad(**)\end{aligned}$$ If $t\in z$: by $(*)$, $t\in(x\mathbin\triangle y)\mathbin\triangle z$ iff $t\notin x\mathbin\triangle y$, i.e. iff $t$ lies in none or both of $x,y$. Together with $t\in z$: in exactly one or in all three sets.

Case $t\notin z$.

If $t\notin z$: by $(*)$, $t\in(x\mathbin\triangle y)\mathbin\triangle z$ iff $t\in x\mathbin\triangle y$, i.e. iff $t$ lies in exactly one of $x,y$. Together with $t\notin z$: in exactly one of the three sets. In both cases this means an odd number, which proves $(**)$.

Step 2: the count does not care about order. Use commutativity to move $x$ to the end, then $(**)$ for $y,z,x$.

For every $t\in M$: $$\begin{aligned}&t\in x\mathbin\triangle(y\mathbin\triangle z)\\\iff&t\in(y\mathbin\triangle z)\mathbin\triangle x\\\iff&t\text{ is in an odd number}\\&\text{of the sets }y,z,x\\\iff&t\in(x\mathbin\triangle y)\mathbin\triangle z.\end{aligned}$$ Both sets are subsets of $M$ with the same elements, so $(x\mathbin\triangle y)\mathbin\triangle z=x\mathbin\triangle(y\mathbin\triangle z)$. Hence $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle)$ is an abelian group. ∎

Another way: a membership table

For a fixed $t$ there are only $2^3=8$ possibilities for ($t\in x$?, $t\in y$?, $t\in z$?). Write 1 for “in” and 0 for “out”; then membership in $x\mathbin\triangle y$ is addition in $\mathbb Z_2$. A table with 8 rows shows that both bracketings give the same column. This is the same argument as $(**)$, written as a truth table (lesson 2, section 4).

Trap

Trying to prove associativity by drawing one Venn diagram and saying “see”. A picture is a good guide, but the proof must say for every element $t$ why it is on both sides. The case split $t\in z$ / $t\notin z$ does that.

Aufgabe 5 · The clock $\mathbb Z_m$

For a positive integer $m$ let $\mathbb Z_m:=\{\bar0,\bar1,\dots,\overline{m-1}\}$. For $\bar k,\bar l\in\mathbb Z_m$ define $\bar k+_m\bar l:=\bar a$, where $0\le a\lt m$ and there is a non-negative $q\in\mathbb Z$ with $m\cdot q+a=k+l$.

(a) Show that for any two $\bar k,\bar l\in\mathbb Z_m$ there is exactly one $a\in\mathbb Z$ with $\bar k+_m\bar l=\bar a$.

(b) Show that $(\mathbb Z_m,+_m)$ is an abelian group for every positive $m$.

In short: Addition on a clock with $m$ hours: add, then take the remainder. (a) the remainder exists and is unique; (b) the five checks.

The idea

On a clock with $m$ hours you add and then go round once if you passed $m$. Part (a) makes sure that “the remainder” is one definite number, so $+_m$ really is an operation. For (b) the trick is a small helper: write $\rho(n)$ for the remainder of $n$ when divided by $m$. Then $\bar k+_m\bar l=\overline{\rho(k+l)}$, and the one fact that does all the work is “you may take the remainder early”: $\rho(r+s)=\rho(\rho(r)+s)$.

In $\mathbb Z_5$: $\bar3+_5\bar4=\bar2$, because $3+4=7=5\cdot1+2$.

You need

Lesson 1, section 4 (the clock, residue classes). Lesson 1, section 3: an abelian group is a set $G$ with an operation $\circ$ that is (0) a 2-place operation on $G$ ($x\circ y\in G$), (1) associative, (2) commutative, with (3) a neutral element and (4) an inverse for every element. For uniqueness: a multiple of $m$ that lies between $0$ and $m-1$ is $0$.

Hint 1

(a) Existence: with $0\le k,l\le m-1$ you have $0\le k+l\le2m-2$. So you need to go round at most once: try $q=0$ and $q=1$. Uniqueness: if $mq+a=mq'+a'$, what is $a-a'$?

Hint 2

(b) Define $\rho(n)$ as the unique remainder of $n\ge0$ (the proof of (a) works for any $n\ge0$). Prove the helper $\rho(r+s)=\rho(\rho(r)+s)$: write $r=mq+\rho(r)$ and $\rho(r)+s=mq'+\rho(\rho(r)+s)$, add, and use uniqueness.

Hint 3

Associativity: $(\bar k+_m\bar l)+_m\bar n=\overline{\rho(\rho(k+l)+n)}=\overline{\rho((k+l)+n)}$, and the same for the other bracketing. Inverse of $\bar k\ne\bar0$: $\overline{m-k}$, since $\rho(m)=0$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

(a) Pick the representatives $0\le k,l\lt m$. Then the sum is between $0$ and $2m-2$.

(a) Let $\bar k,\bar l\in\mathbb Z_m$ with $0\le k,l\lt m$. We show there is exactly one $a\in\mathbb Z$ with $0\le a\lt m$ and $mq+a=k+l$ for some $q\ge0$. Since $0\le k,l\le m-1$, $0\le k+l\le2m-2$.

Existence: no wrap-around or one wrap-around.

If $k+l\lt m$: take $q:=0$, $a:=k+l$. If $k+l\ge m$: take $q:=1$, $a:=k+l-m$; then $0\le a\le m-2\lt m$ and $m\cdot1+a=k+l$.

Uniqueness: two remainders differ by a multiple of $m$, which must be $0$.

Let $mq+a=k+l=mq'+a'$ with $0\le a,a'\lt m$ and $q,q'\ge0$; without loss of generality $a\ge a'$. Then $a-a'=m(q'-q)$ is a multiple of $m$ with $0\le a-a'\lt m$, so $a-a'=0$. Then $mq=mq'$ and, since $m>0$, $q=q'$. So $+_m$ is a well-defined operation on $\mathbb Z_m$.

(b) Name the remainder. The proof of (a) works for any $n\ge0$.

(b) For every $n\in\mathbb Z$, $n\ge0$, there is exactly one $a$ with $0\le a\lt m$ and $mq+a=n$ for some $q\ge0$ (existence: subtract $m$ until the result is below $m$; uniqueness as in (a)). Write $\rho(n):=a$. For $0\le n\lt m$, $\rho(n)=n$. Then $\bar k+_m\bar l=\overline{\rho(k+l)}$.

The helper: taking the remainder early does not change the final remainder.

Helper. For all $r,s\ge0$: $\rho(r+s)=\rho(\rho(r)+s)$. Proof: $r=mq+\rho(r)$ and $\rho(r)+s=mq'+\rho(\rho(r)+s)$ with $q,q'\ge0$. Adding, $r+s=m(q+q')+\rho(\rho(r)+s)$ with $q+q'\ge0$ and $0\le\rho(\rho(r)+s)\lt m$. By uniqueness, $\rho(r+s)=\rho(\rho(r)+s)$.

Associativity: both bracketings become “remainder of $k+l+n$”.

For $0\le k,l,n\lt m$, by the helper and commutativity in $\mathbb Z$: $$\begin{aligned}(\bar k+_m\bar l)+_m\bar n&=\overline{\rho(\rho(k+l)+n)}\\&=\overline{\rho((k+l)+n)},\\ \bar k+_m(\bar l+_m\bar n)&=\overline{\rho(\rho(l+n)+k)}\\&=\overline{\rho((l+n)+k)}.\end{aligned}$$ Since $(k+l)+n=(l+n)+k$ in $\mathbb Z$, the two agree.

Commutativity and the neutral element come from $\mathbb Z$.

$\bar k+_m\bar l=\overline{\rho(k+l)}=\overline{\rho(l+k)}=\bar l+_m\bar k$. And $\bar0+_m\bar k=\overline{\rho(k)}=\bar k$.

Inverses: $\bar0$ is its own inverse; otherwise go the rest of the way round, $m-k$.

$\bar0+_m\bar0=\bar0$. For $1\le k\le m-1$ put $a:=m-k$; then $1\le a\le m-1$, so $\bar a\in\mathbb Z_m$, and since $m=m\cdot1+0$, $\rho(m)=0$, so $\bar k+_m\bar a=\overline{\rho(m)}=\bar0$. Hence $(\mathbb Z_m,+_m)$ is an abelian group. ∎

Trap

Using the representative $\overline{m}$ or negative numbers: in this definition the elements are $\bar0,\dots,\overline{m-1}$ and the remainder must satisfy $0\le a\lt m$. So the inverse of $\bar k$ is $\overline{m-k}$, not $\overline{-k}$. And in (a), proving only existence: “exactly one” always means existence and uniqueness.
