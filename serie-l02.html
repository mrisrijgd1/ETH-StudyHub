Serie 2, explained: Vector spaces

First calculation rules straight from the axioms, then four vector spaces you already met as groups: Fibonacci sequences, polynomials, $\mathbb Z_p^n$ and all functions $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$. The pattern to learn: define the scalar multiplication, check it stays inside, check the four axioms coordinatewise.

🌧
Bad day? Do Aufgabe 13 only: count the elements of $\mathbb Z_p^n$ and compute $\bar7\cdot(\bar0,\dots,\overline{10})$ in $\mathbb Z_{11}^{11}$. That counts. Next time, simply carry on from here: a missed or light day is not a restart.
You're done when
you can (1) prove $0\cdot x=0_V$, $\lambda\cdot0_V=0_V$, $(-1)\cdot x=-x$ and “$\lambda x=0_V$ implies $\lambda=0$ or $x=0_V$” from the axioms, naming the axiom in each step; (2) show that a given set with termwise or pointwise operations is a vector space by checking (V1) to (V4) entry by entry; (3) compute in $\mathbb Z_p^n$.
Time
About 75 minutes: 30 for Aufgabe 10, 30 for Aufgaben 11, 12 and 14 (the same pattern three times), 15 for Aufgabe 13.
Lessons
Lesson 5, sections 2–4 (definition, calculation rules, standard examples).

Aufgabe 10 · Calculation rules from the axioms

Let $(V,+_V,0_V)$ be a vector space over the field $(K,+,\cdot,0,1)$. Show: (a) $\forall x\in V\ (0\cdot x=0_V)$; (b) $\forall\lambda\in K\ (\lambda\cdot0_V=0_V)$; (c) $\forall x\in V\ ((-1)\cdot x=-x)$; (d) $\forall\lambda\in K\ \forall x\in V\ (\lambda\cdot x=0_V\to(\lambda=0\lor x=0_V))$.

In short: Four rules everyone uses without thinking. Here you derive each one from (V1) to (V4) and the group axioms.

The idea

One small helper does most of the work: if $u+u=u$, then $u=0_V$ (add $-u$ on both sides). For (a), split $0=0+0$ in the field and use (V2): $0x=0x+0x$. For (b), split $0_V=0_V+0_V$ and use (V1). (c) shows that $(-1)x$ does the job of an inverse, and inverses in a group are unique. (d) is the only one with a case split: if $\lambda\ne0$, multiply by $\lambda^{-1}$.

You need

Lesson 5, section 2: a vector space over $K$ is an abelian group $(V,+_V,0_V)$ with a scalar multiplication $K\times V\to V$ such that for all $\lambda,\mu\in K$, $x,y\in V$: (V1) $\lambda(x+_Vy)=\lambda x+_V\lambda y$; (V2) $(\lambda+\mu)x=\lambda x+_V\mu x$; (V3) $(\lambda\mu)x=\lambda(\mu x)$; (V4) $1\cdot x=x$. Lesson 5, section 3 does the same rules with proofs. Inverses in a group are unique (lesson 1, section 3).

Hint 1

Prove first: if $u\in V$ and $u+_Vu=u$, then $u=0_V$. Then (a): $0\cdot x=(0+0)\cdot x$. (b): $\lambda\cdot0_V=\lambda\cdot(0_V+_V0_V)$.

Hint 2

(c): compute $x+_V(-1)\cdot x$ using (V4), (V2) and (a). (d): if $\lambda\ne0$, write $x=1\cdot x=(\lambda^{-1}\lambda)\cdot x$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

The helper: a vector that equals its own double is the zero vector. Add $-u$ and regroup.

Helper. If $u\in V$ with $u+_Vu=u$, then $u=0_V$: $$\begin{aligned}0_V&=u+_V(-u)\\&=(u+_Vu)+_V(-u)\\&=u+_V(u+_V(-u))\\&=u+_V0_V=u.\end{aligned}$$

(a) Split the scalar $0=0+0$ in $K$ and use (V2).

(a) Since $0+0=0$ in $K$, (V2) gives $0\cdot x=(0+0)\cdot x=0\cdot x+_V0\cdot x$. The helper with $u:=0\cdot x$ gives $0\cdot x=0_V$.

(b) Split the vector $0_V=0_V+0_V$ and use (V1).

(b) Since $0_V+_V0_V=0_V$, (V1) gives $\lambda\cdot0_V=\lambda\cdot(0_V+_V0_V)=\lambda\cdot0_V+_V\lambda\cdot0_V$. The helper gives $\lambda\cdot0_V=0_V$.

(c) Show that $(-1)x$ behaves like an inverse of $x$.

(c) By (V4), (V2) and (a): $x+_V(-1)\cdot x=1\cdot x+_V(-1)\cdot x=(1+(-1))\cdot x=0\cdot x=0_V$.

In a group the inverse is unique, so $(-1)x$ is the inverse.

If $y$ and $y'$ are both inverses of $x$, then $y=y+_V(x+_Vy')=(y+_Vx)+_Vy'=y'$. So $(-1)\cdot x=-x$.

(d) Case split on $\lambda$. If $\lambda\ne0$, it has an inverse in the field; use (V4), (V3) and (b).

(d) Let $\lambda\cdot x=0_V$. If $\lambda=0$ we are done. If $\lambda\ne0$, then $\lambda^{-1}\in K$ exists, and $$\begin{aligned}x&=1\cdot x=(\lambda^{-1}\lambda)\cdot x\\&=\lambda^{-1}\cdot(\lambda\cdot x)\\&=\lambda^{-1}\cdot0_V=0_V.\end{aligned}$$ So $\lambda=0$ or $x=0_V$. ∎

Trap

Writing $0\cdot x=(1-1)\cdot x=x-x=0_V$ in (a). It is not wrong as an idea, but “$1\cdot x-1\cdot x$” already uses $(-1)\cdot x=-x$, which is (c), proved later from (a). Keep the order: (a) and (b) from the helper, then (c), then (d). Also note: there are two zeros, $0\in K$ and $0_V\in V$; say which one you mean.

Aufgabe 11 · Fibonacci sequences form a vector space

Let $(\mathrm{Fib},+)$ be as in Aufgabe 3 (b). Show that $(\mathrm{Fib},+)$ is a vector space over $\mathbb R$.

In short: Multiply every term by $\lambda$. The recursion survives, and the axioms hold term by term.

The idea

The group part is already done in Serie 0, so only the scalar multiplication is new. Define it the natural way (multiply every term), check that the result is still in the set, and then check (V1) to (V4) in each term, where they are rules of the field you already know.

Here: $\lambda\cdot(f_n):=(\lambda f_n)$. The recursion survives scaling: $\lambda f_{i+2}=\lambda(f_{i+1}+f_i)=\lambda f_{i+1}+\lambda f_i$.

You need

Lesson 5, section 2: a vector space over $K$ is an abelian group $(V,+_V,0_V)$ with a scalar multiplication $K\times V\to V$ such that for all $\lambda,\mu\in K$, $x,y\in V$: (V1) $\lambda(x+_Vy)=\lambda x+_V\lambda y$; (V2) $(\lambda+\mu)x=\lambda x+_V\mu x$; (V3) $(\lambda\mu)x=\lambda(\mu x)$; (V4) $1\cdot x=x$. Serie 0, Aufgabe 3 for the group.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

The group part is Aufgabe 3 (b).

By Aufgabe 3 (b), $(\mathrm{Fib},+)$ is an abelian group with zero $(0,0,\dots)$ and inverse $(-f_n)$ of $(f_n)$.

Define the scalar multiplication termwise.

For $\lambda\in\mathbb R$ and $(f_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$ set $\lambda\cdot(f_n):=(\lambda f_n)$.

It stays in $\mathrm{Fib}$: the recursion survives by the distributive law in $\mathbb R$.

For every $i$: $\lambda f_{i+2}=\lambda(f_{i+1}+f_i)=\lambda f_{i+1}+\lambda f_i$. So $(\lambda f_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$, and $\cdot$ is a map $\mathbb R\times\mathrm{Fib}\to\mathrm{Fib}$.

(V1) to (V4) term by term: sequences are equal when all terms are equal.

For $\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb R$, $(f_n),(g_n)\in\mathrm{Fib}$ and every $n$: $\lambda(f_n+g_n)=\lambda f_n+\lambda g_n$, $(\lambda+\mu)f_n=\lambda f_n+\mu f_n$, $(\lambda\mu)f_n=\lambda(\mu f_n)$, $1\cdot f_n=f_n$. These are (V1) to (V4) in each term, so $\mathrm{Fib}$ is a vector space over $\mathbb R$. ∎

Remark (official): the same computation shows that all real sequences $S$ form a vector space; $\mathrm{Fib}$ is a subset closed under both operations, a subspace (lesson 5, section 6).

Trap

Only checking (V1) to (V4) and forgetting that $\lambda\cdot(f_n)$ must again be a Fibonacci-type sequence. Without that step the scalar multiplication is not even a map into $\mathrm{Fib}$.

Aufgabe 12 · Polynomials form a vector space

Let $(\mathbb Q[X],+)$ be as in Aufgabe 7. Show that $(\mathbb Q[X],+)$ is a vector space over $\mathbb Q$.

Official remark: since $\mathbb Q[X]$ is a ring and not only an abelian group, $\mathbb Q[X]$ over $\mathbb Q$ is a so-called algebra.

In short: Multiply every coefficient by $\lambda$; only finitely many stay non-zero; axioms coefficientwise.

The idea

The group part is already done in Serie 0, so only the scalar multiplication is new. Define it the natural way (multiply every term), check that the result is still in the set, and then check (V1) to (V4) in each term, where they are rules of the field you already know.

Here: $\lambda\cdot\sum a_iX^i:=\sum(\lambda a_i)X^i$. If $a_i=0$ for $i\gt n$, then also $\lambda a_i=0$, so the result is again a polynomial.

You need

Lesson 5, section 2: a vector space over $K$ is an abelian group $(V,+_V,0_V)$ with a scalar multiplication $K\times V\to V$ such that for all $\lambda,\mu\in K$, $x,y\in V$: (V1) $\lambda(x+_Vy)=\lambda x+_V\lambda y$; (V2) $(\lambda+\mu)x=\lambda x+_V\mu x$; (V3) $(\lambda\mu)x=\lambda(\mu x)$; (V4) $1\cdot x=x$. Serie 1, Aufgabe 7 for the group.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

The group part is Aufgabe 7 (a).

Write $p=\sum_{i\ge0}a_iX^i$ with $a_i=0$ for all but finitely many $i$. By Aufgabe 7 (a), $(\mathbb Q[X],+)$ is an abelian group.

Define the scalar multiplication coefficientwise and check it is a polynomial again.

For $\lambda\in\mathbb Q$ set $\lambda\cdot p:=\sum_{i\ge0}(\lambda a_i)X^i$. If $a_i=0$ for $i\gt n$, then $\lambda a_i=0$ for $i\gt n$, and all $\lambda a_i\in\mathbb Q$. So $\lambda\cdot p\in\mathbb Q[X]$.

(V1) to (V4) coefficientwise.

For $\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb Q$, $p=\sum a_iX^i$, $q=\sum b_iX^i$ and every $k$: $\lambda(a_k+b_k)=\lambda a_k+\lambda b_k$, $(\lambda+\mu)a_k=\lambda a_k+\mu a_k$, $(\lambda\mu)a_k=\lambda(\mu a_k)$, $1\cdot a_k=a_k$. So $\mathbb Q[X]$ is a vector space over $\mathbb Q$. ∎

Remark (official): viewing $\lambda$ as the constant polynomial ($e_0=\lambda$, $e_i=0$ otherwise), the product formula gives $(\lambda\cdot p)_k=\sum_{i+j=k}e_ia_j=\lambda a_k$. So scalar multiplication is just multiplication by constant polynomials in the ring.

Trap

Mixing up the two multiplications: $\lambda\cdot p$ (a number times a polynomial, the vector space structure) and $p\cdot q$ (two polynomials, the ring structure). For the vector space you only need the first.

Aufgabe 13 · Counting and computing in $\mathbb Z_p^n$

Let $p$ be prime, $\mathbb Z_p$ the field from Aufgabe 8, and $n\in\mathbb N$. (a) How many elements does the vector space $\mathbb Z_p^n$ over $\mathbb Z_p$ have? (b) For $p=11$, $n=11$ compute $\bar7\cdot(\bar0,\bar1,\bar2,\bar3,\bar4,\bar5,\bar6,\bar7,\bar8,\bar9,\overline{10})$.

In short: (a) $p$ choices in each of $n$ places: $p^n$. (b) Multiply each entry by $7$ and take the remainder mod $11$.

The idea

$\mathbb Z_p^n$ is the set of $n$-tuples with entries in $\mathbb Z_p$. For counting, the operations do not matter: each of the $n$ entries is one of $p$ values, chosen independently. For (b), scalar multiplication is entrywise, and in each entry you compute $7k$ and reduce mod $11$.

You need

The vector space $K^n$ (lesson 5, section 4); $\mathbb Z_p$ has exactly $p$ elements (Serie 0, Aufgabe 5); multiplication in $\mathbb Z_m$ (Serie 1, Aufgabe 8).

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

(a) First $|\mathbb Z_p|=p$: every class has exactly one representative $0\le k\lt p$.

(a) $\mathbb Z_p=\{\bar0,\dots,\overline{p-1}\}$, and these $p$ elements are distinct, since every element has exactly one representative $k$ with $0\le k\lt p$.

Then count tuples: independent choices multiply.

An element of $\mathbb Z_p^n$ is a tuple $(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ with each $a_i\in\mathbb Z_p$, and two tuples are equal iff all entries agree. Each entry can take $p$ values independently, so $|\mathbb Z_p^n|=p\cdot p\cdots p=p^n$.

(b) Entrywise: $7k$ and its remainder mod $11$, for $k=0,\dots,10$.

(b) $7k$ for $k=0,\dots,10$ is $0,7,14,21,28,35,42,49,56,63,70$, with remainders mod $11$: $0,7,3,10,6,2,9,5,1,8,4$. So $$\begin{aligned}&\bar7\cdot(\bar0,\bar1,\dots,\overline{10})\\&=(\bar0,\bar7,\bar3,\overline{10},\bar6,\bar2,\bar9,\bar5,\bar1,\bar8,\bar4).\end{aligned}$$

Notice: the answer in (b) contains every element of $\mathbb Z_{11}$ exactly once. That is the pigeonhole argument of Serie 1, Aufgabe 8 (d) in action: multiplying by $\bar7\ne\bar0$ is injective, so it permutes $\mathbb Z_{11}$.

Trap

Answering $n^p$ or $p\cdot n$ in (a). Each of the $n$ places has $p$ options: $p$ multiplied $n$ times. And in (b), forgetting to reduce: $\bar7\cdot\bar3=\overline{21}$ is not in the list $\bar0,\dots,\overline{10}$; write $\overline{10}$.

Aufgabe 14 · All functions $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$

Let $V$ be the set of all functions $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, with addition $f+g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, $x\mapsto f(x)+g(x)$. Show that $(V,+)$ is a vector space over $\mathbb R$.

In short: Everything pointwise: two functions are equal when they agree at every $x$.

The idea

The group part is already done in Serie 0, so only the scalar multiplication is new. Define it the natural way (multiply every term), check that the result is still in the set, and then check (V1) to (V4) in each term, where they are rules of the field you already know.

Here the “terms” are the values $f(x)$, one for each $x\in\mathbb R$. Define $(\lambda f)(x):=\lambda f(x)$; then each axiom at the point $x$ is a rule of $\mathbb R$.

You need

Lesson 5, section 2: a vector space over $K$ is an abelian group $(V,+_V,0_V)$ with a scalar multiplication $K\times V\to V$ such that for all $\lambda,\mu\in K$, $x,y\in V$: (V1) $\lambda(x+_Vy)=\lambda x+_V\lambda y$; (V2) $(\lambda+\mu)x=\lambda x+_V\mu x$; (V3) $(\lambda\mu)x=\lambda(\mu x)$; (V4) $1\cdot x=x$. Two functions are equal iff they have the same value at every point (lesson 3, section 3).

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Equality of functions and the definition of the sum.

$f,g\in V$ are equal iff $f(x)=g(x)$ for all $x\in\mathbb R$, and $(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)$. Define $(\lambda\cdot f)(x):=\lambda\cdot f(x)$ for $\lambda\in\mathbb R$.

Both operations give functions $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ again.

For every $x$, $f(x)+g(x)\in\mathbb R$ and $\lambda f(x)\in\mathbb R$, so $f+g\in V$ and $\lambda\cdot f\in V$.

Group laws pointwise.

For all $x$: $((f+g)+h)(x)=(f(x)+g(x))+h(x)=f(x)+(g(x)+h(x))=(f+(g+h))(x)$ and $(f+g)(x)=(g+f)(x)$. The zero function $0_V(x)=0$ is neutral, and $(-f)(x):=-f(x)$ gives $f+(-f)=0_V$.

(V1) to (V4) pointwise.

For all $\lambda,\mu$, $f,g$ and $x$: $\lambda(f(x)+g(x))=\lambda f(x)+\lambda g(x)$, $(\lambda+\mu)f(x)=\lambda f(x)+\mu f(x)$, $(\lambda\mu)f(x)=\lambda(\mu f(x))$, $1\cdot f(x)=f(x)$. Since functions are equal when equal at every point, (V1) to (V4) hold, and $V$ is a vector space over $\mathbb R$. ∎

Remark (official): a sequence is a function $\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$, so Aufgabe 11 and this task are the same fact with different domains. For every set $M$, the functions $M\to\mathbb R$ form a vector space.

Trap

Writing $f+g=g+f$ “because addition is commutative” without saying where: it is commutativity in $\mathbb R$, applied at each point $x$. The proof of every axiom must pass through the values $f(x)$.
