1. Warm-up (5 min)

Three quick questions. Answer each in your head, then open it.

  1. True or false: every bounded sequence in $\mathbb R$ converges.

    Answer

    False: $(-1)^n$ is bounded and jumps between $-1$ and $1$. What is true is weaker: every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence (Bolzano–Weierstrass).

  2. What is $\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}n$, and which tool gives it in one line?

    Answer

    $0$, by the sandwich lemma: $-\frac1n\le\frac{(-1)^n}n\le\frac1n$, and both bounds tend to $0$.

  3. Is $a_n=\frac n{n+1}$ monotone and bounded? What is its limit?

    Answer

    It is increasing ($\frac n{n+1}=1-\frac1{n+1}$ and $\frac1{n+1}$ decreases) and bounded by $1$, so it converges. The limit rules give the value: $\frac n{n+1}=\frac1{1+1/n}\to1$.

2. Recap card (10 min)

Read this once, slowly. Everything in bold must come out exactly in the exam.

Lesson 10: distance and convergence

  • Norm on a real or complex vector space $V$: $\|v\|\ge0$ with $\|v\|=0\iff v=0$; $\|\lambda v\|=|\lambda|\,\|v\|$; $\|v+w\|\le\|v\|+\|w\|$. Examples on $\mathbb R^d$: $\|\cdot\|_1$, $\|\cdot\|_2$, $\|\cdot\|_\infty$.
  • Metric on a set $X$: $d(x,y)=0\iff x=y$; $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$; $d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)$. Every norm gives a metric $d(v,w)=\|v-w\|$.
  • Open ball $B_r(x)=\{y\in X:d(x,y)<r\}$. $U$ is open if every point of $U$ has a ball around it inside $U$; closed means the complement is open.
  • $a_n\to A$ means $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\mathbb N\ \forall n\ge N:\ d(a_n,A)<\varepsilon$. A limit is unique, and a convergent sequence is bounded.
  • Limit rules for sums, multiples, products and quotients (non-zero limit in the denominator). In $\mathbb R^d$ and $\mathbb C$, convergence is coordinate by coordinate.
  • Continuity with sequences: $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ if and only if $x_n\to x_0$ always implies $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$ (Proof 1). A closed set contains the limit of every convergent sequence in it.

Lesson 11: sandwich, monotone sequences, limsup

  • Limits keep $\le$: $a_n\le b_n$ for all $n$ gives $\lim a_n\le\lim b_n$. Strict $<$ does not survive.
  • Sandwich lemma: if $a_n\le b_n\le c_n$ and $a_n\to A$, $c_n\to A$, then $b_n\to A$.
  • Monotone convergence: a monotone sequence converges if and only if it is bounded; an increasing one converges to $\sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$ (Proof 2).
  • For a bounded sequence: $\limsup a_n=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup\{a_k:k\ge n\}$ and $\liminf a_n=\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf\{a_k:k\ge n\}$. The tail suprema decrease and the tail infima increase, so both limits exist.
  • $(a_n)_n$ converges if and only if $\liminf a_n=\limsup a_n$, and then the limit is this common value.

Lesson 12: subsequences and Cauchy sequences

  • Bolzano–Weierstrass: every bounded sequence in $\mathbb R$ has a convergent subsequence. In fact $\limsup a_n$ and $\liminf a_n$ are the largest and the smallest accumulation point.
  • Improper limit: $a_n\to+\infty$ means $\forall M>0\ \exists N\ \forall n\ge N:\ a_n>M$. Such a sequence diverges; it only has an improper limit.
  • Cauchy sequence: $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall m,n\ge N:\ |a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon$. No limit appears in the definition.
  • Convergent $\Rightarrow$ Cauchy $\Rightarrow$ bounded. A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges.
  • Cauchy criterion: in $\mathbb R$, a sequence converges if and only if it is Cauchy (Proof 3). This is completeness in sequence form.

4. Write it on paper: three exam proofs (30 min)

For each proof: read the claim and the frame, close your eyes for ten seconds, then write the whole proof on paper without looking at the lesson. Only then open the fold and mark yourself with the grader's list.

Proof 1: ε-δ continuity ⇔ sequential continuity

Claim. Let $(X,d_X)$, $(Y,d_Y)$ be metric spaces, $f:X\to Y$ and $x_0\in X$. Then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ (ε-δ) if and only if for every sequence $x_n\to x_0$ in $X$ we have $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$. (Lesson 10, Proof E.)

What the definitions force

(⇒) First line: “Let $x_n\to x_0$ and $\varepsilon>0$.” Last line: “so $d_Y(f(x_n),f(x_0))<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.” The gap: find $N$.

(⇐) by contrapositive. First line: “Suppose $f$ is not continuous at $x_0$. Then there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that for every $\delta>0$ …” Last line: “so $f(x_n)\not\to f(x_0)$.” The gap: build the sequence $x_n$.

The proof and the grader's list

(⇒) Let $x_n\to x_0$ and $\varepsilon>0$. By continuity choose $\delta>0$ with $d_Y(f(x),f(x_0))<\varepsilon$ whenever $d_X(x,x_0)<\delta$. Since $x_n\to x_0$, choose $N$ with $d_X(x_n,x_0)<\delta$ for all $n\ge N$. Then $d_Y(f(x_n),f(x_0))<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$, so $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$.

(⇐) Suppose $f$ is not continuous at $x_0$. Then there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that for every $\delta>0$ there is an $x$ with $d_X(x,x_0)<\delta$ and $d_Y(f(x),f(x_0))\ge\varepsilon$. Taking $\delta=\frac1n$ gives, for every $n\in\mathbb N$, a point $x_n$ with $d_X(x_n,x_0)<\frac1n$ and $d_Y(f(x_n),f(x_0))\ge\varepsilon$. Then $x_n\to x_0$, but $f(x_n)\not\to f(x_0)$. So $f$ is not sequentially continuous at $x_0$. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • (⇒) the order: $\delta$ from continuity first, then $N$ from convergence with tolerance $\delta$, then the chain $n\ge N\Rightarrow d_X(x_n,x_0)<\delta\Rightarrow d_Y(f(x_n),f(x_0))<\varepsilon$;
  • (⇐) saying “contrapositive” and writing the negation of continuity correctly (one bad $\varepsilon$, for every $\delta$ a bad point);
  • the choice $\delta=\frac1n$, which produces a sequence;
  • both conclusions: $x_n\to x_0$ (because $d_X(x_n,x_0)<\frac1n$) and $f(x_n)\not\to f(x_0)$ (because the distance stays $\ge\varepsilon$).

In short: “δ from continuity, N from convergence” and “negate, take δ = 1/n”.

Proof 2: bounded monotone sequences converge (Satz 6.5)

Claim. A monotone sequence $(a_n)_n$ in $\mathbb R$ converges if and only if it is bounded. (Lesson 11, Proof B.)

What the definitions force

“⇒” is “convergent ⇒ bounded” from lesson 10. For “⇐” there is no limit yet, so you must name a candidate, and completeness only gives one: $S=\sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$. First line: “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” Last line: “so $|a_n-S|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.” The gap: find $N$ with “$S-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound”.

The proof and the grader's list

A convergent sequence is bounded (lesson 10). Conversely, let $(a_n)_n$ be monotone and bounded. Without loss of generality it is increasing (otherwise consider $(-a_n)_n$). By completeness, $S=\sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$ exists. Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $S-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound of $\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$, there is an $N$ with $a_N>S-\varepsilon$. For $n\ge N$, monotonicity and the upper bound give $$S-\varepsilon<a_N\le a_n\le S,$$ so $|a_n-S|<\varepsilon$. Hence $a_n\to S$. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • both directions named, with “⇒” referred to “convergent ⇒ bounded”;
  • “without loss of generality increasing” with the reason ($-a_n$);
  • $S=\sup$ exists by completeness, because the set is non-empty and bounded above;
  • the step “$S-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound, so some $a_N>S-\varepsilon$”;
  • the chain $S-\varepsilon<a_N\le a_n\le S$ for all $n\ge N$, using monotone and upper bound.

In short: “sup exists, one term above S − ε, monotone carries it to all later terms”.

Proof 3: the Cauchy criterion (Satz 6.26, Kobel-Keller HS24, 6 points)

Claim. A sequence $(a_n)_n$ in $\mathbb R$ converges if and only if it is a Cauchy sequence, that is, $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall m,n\ge N:\ |a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon$. (Lesson 12, Proof E. The direction “Cauchy ⇒ convergent” was a whole 6-point question in HS24.)

What the definitions force

“⇒”: $\frac\varepsilon2$ through the limit. “⇐”: there is no candidate limit, so you must produce one. The only tool that makes limits out of boundedness alone is Bolzano–Weierstrass. So: show bounded, take a convergent subsequence $a_{n_k}\to L$, then show the whole sequence follows it.

The proof and the grader's list

“⇒”: Let $a_n\to A$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $|a_n-A|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $n\ge N$. Then for $m,n\ge N$: $|a_m-a_n|\le|a_m-A|+|A-a_n|<\varepsilon$.

“⇐”: Let $(a_n)_n$ be Cauchy. For $\varepsilon=1$ choose $N_0$ with $|a_m-a_n|<1$ for $m,n\ge N_0$. Then $|a_n|<1+|a_{N_0}|$ for $n\ge N_0$, so $$\begin{aligned}|a_n|\le\max\{&|a_1|,\dots,|a_{N_0-1}|,\\&1+|a_{N_0}|\}\end{aligned}$$ for all $n$, and $(a_n)_n$ is bounded. By Bolzano–Weierstrass it has a convergent subsequence $a_{n_k}\to L$. Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $|a_n-a_m|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $m,n\ge N$, and $K$ with $|a_{n_k}-L|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $k\ge K$. Take $k=\max\{N,K\}$; then $n_k\ge k\ge N$. So for all $n\ge N$: $$\begin{aligned}|a_n-L|&\le|a_n-a_{n_k}|+|a_{n_k}-L|\\&<\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$ Hence $a_n\to L$. ∎

A grader gives the points for (this is Kobel-Keller's HS24 grading, one point each):

  • bounded, with the finite maximum for the first terms;
  • the convergent subsequence, with the name Bolzano–Weierstrass;
  • the Cauchy $\frac\varepsilon2$ (the index $N$);
  • the subsequence $\frac\varepsilon2$ (the index $K$);
  • the choice $k=\max\{N,K\}$, so that $n_k\ge N$;
  • the final triangle inequality through $a_{n_k}$.

In short: “bounded, Bolzano–Weierstrass, then the whole sequence follows its subsequence”.

5. Spot the mistake (5 min)

A student tries the “⇐” direction of Proof 3 and hands in this. The claim is true, but the proof is worth zero points. Why?

“Proof.” Let $(a_n)_n$ be Cauchy and let $A=\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n$. Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $|a_m-a_n|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $m,n\ge N$, and $M$ with $|a_m-A|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $m\ge M$. For $n\ge N$ take any $m\ge\max\{N,M\}$. Then $|a_n-A|\le|a_n-a_m|+|a_m-A|<\varepsilon$. So $a_n\to A$. ∎

The mistake

The first line already assumes what is to be proved: “let $A=\lim a_n$” only makes sense if the limit exists, and the existence of the limit is the whole claim. Everything after it is correct but circular.

The real proof has to produce a candidate first: show the sequence is bounded, use Bolzano–Weierstrass to get a subsequence with a limit $L$, and only then run this triangle-inequality argument with $a_{n_k}$ in place of $a_m$. Exam lesson: in an existence proof, never name the object before you have built it.

How to use it: skim the PDF once (about 5 minutes; the German terms are the ones in the lessons' word tables). Then read the box below and answer the quick checks in your head.

Worth a second look

  • Norms on functions: $\|f\|_\infty=\sup|f|$ and $\|f\|_1=\int|f|$. A sequence of functions can converge in one norm and not in the other.
  • Balls depend on the space you are in: in $Y=[0,1]$, the ball $B^Y_{1/2}(0)$ is $[0,\frac12)$. It is open in $Y$, although it is not open in $\mathbb R$.
  • Convergence in $\mathbb R^d$ and $\mathbb C$ is coordinate by coordinate.
  • Lipschitz: $|f(x)-f(y)|\le L|x-y|$ for all $x,y$. It implies uniform continuity, with $\delta=\frac\varepsilon L$.
  • Limits keep $\le$ but not $<$: $a_n<b_n$ for all $n$ only gives $\lim a_n\le\lim b_n$.
  • Two standard limits: $\sqrt[n]a\to1$ for $a>0$, and $\sqrt[n]n\to1$.
  • Cauchy needs no candidate limit, and whether a Cauchy sequence converges depends on the space: $\frac1n$ is Cauchy in $(0,1]$ but has no limit there. In $\mathbb R$ every Cauchy sequence converges (Proof 3). And $a_{n+1}-a_n\to0$ is not enough for Cauchy.

Quick checks from the Lernblatt

Translated from the Lernblatt's exercise list. The Lernblatt has no official answers, so the answers here are StudyHub-written.

  1. Compute $\|(3,4)\|_1$, $\|(3,4)\|_2$ and $\|(3,4)\|_\infty$.

    Answer

    $3+4=7$, $\sqrt{9+16}=5$ and $\max\{3,4\}=4$.

  2. Is $N(x,y)=|x|$ a norm on $\mathbb R^2$?

    Answer

    No: $N(0,1)=0$ although $(0,1)\ne0$. The axiom “$\|v\|=0\Rightarrow v=0$” fails.

  3. Is $d(x,y)=|x-y|^2$ a metric on $\mathbb R$? Test the triangle inequality at $0,1,2$.

    Answer

    No: $d(0,2)=4$, but $d(0,1)+d(1,2)=1+1=2<4$.

  4. Let $f_n(x)=\max\{1-nx,0\}$ on $[0,1]$. Does $f_n\to0$ in $\|\cdot\|_1$? In $\|\cdot\|_\infty$?

    Answer

    $\|f_n\|_1=\int_0^{1/n}(1-nx)\,dx=\frac1{2n}\to0$: yes. But $\|f_n\|_\infty=f_n(0)=1$ for every $n$: no.

  5. Give a Lipschitz constant for $f(x)=3x+1$ on $\mathbb R$. Is $f$ uniformly continuous?

    Answer

    $L=3$, since $|f(x)-f(y)|=3|x-y|$. Yes, with $\delta=\frac\varepsilon3$.

  6. If $a_n<b_n$ for all $n$ and both converge, must $\lim a_n<\lim b_n$?

    Answer

    No: $a_n=0<b_n=\frac1n$, and both limits are $0$.

  7. For $a_n=2+(-1)^n$, find $\liminf a_n$, $\limsup a_n$ and the accumulation points. Does it converge?

    Answer

    $\liminf=1$, $\limsup=3$, accumulation points $1$ and $3$. It diverges, since $\liminf\ne\limsup$.

  8. For $a_n=\sqrt n$ we have $a_{n+1}-a_n\to0$. Is $(a_n)_n$ Cauchy?

    Answer

    No. It is unbounded, and Cauchy sequences are bounded. (The differences $\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n=\frac1{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n}$ do go to $0$: neighbours close together is weaker than “all late terms close together”.)

  9. In $X=(0,1]$ with the usual distance, is $a_n=\frac1n$ Cauchy? Does it have a limit in $X$?

    Answer

    Cauchy: yes, $|\frac1m-\frac1n|\le\frac1N$ for $m,n\ge N$. Limit in $X$: no, the only candidate $0$ is not in $X$.
