Proofs in Linear Algebra reuse a small set of moves, even more than in Analysis: most proofs are three to ten lines of “write down what the definition says, then use one axiom at a time”. Each card says when to reach for the move, the sentences it produces, and where it has appeared so far. Lesson pills link here. The list grows with every lesson.

From lesson 1

Unfold the definition

When: always first. The statement uses a word (group, inverse, well defined, subgroup, homomorphism). Replace the word by the equations it stands for.

Sentences: “By definition, $\bar a=\overline{a'}$ means that there is $l\in\mathbb Z$ with $a-a'=ln$.” “We have to show that for all $x,y\in M$, $x\circ y\in M$.”

Seen in: well-definedness of $+$ on $\mathbb Z/n$, the subgroup test, every group check (1).

The structure checklist

When: “show that $(M,\circ)$ is a (abelian) group / ring / field”. Check each axiom in a fixed order, one line each, and name the neutral element and the inverse explicitly.

Sentences: “Operation: for $x,y\in M$ … so $x\circ y\in M$. Associativity: … Neutral element: $e:=\dots$, since $e\circ x=\dots=x$. Inverses: for $x\in M$ put $x':=\dots$; then $x'\circ x=\dots=e$. Commutativity: …”

Seen in: $(\mathbb Q_{>0},\cdot)$, $(\mathbb Z/n,+)$, $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle)$, Fibonacci sequences, $(\mathbb R,\ast)$ (1).

Inherit from the bigger structure

When: $M$ is a subset of something you already know ($\mathbb Q$, $\mathbb R$, a group $G$) and the operation is the same. Equations that hold for all elements of the big set hold in particular for the elements of $M$. Only closure, the neutral element and the inverses need work.

Sentences: “Associativity and commutativity hold for all rationals, in particular for all elements of $\mathbb Q_{>0}\subseteq\mathbb Q$.”

Seen in: $(\mathbb Q_{>0},\cdot)$, Fibonacci sequences inside all sequences, a subgroup is a group (1).

Compute one element in two ways

When: you must show two things are equal and each one has a property that “absorbs” something. Combine them into one expression and evaluate it twice, once with each property.

Sentences: “Consider $e_l\circ e_r$. Since $e_l$ is left neutral, $e_l\circ e_r=e_r$. Since $e_r$ is right neutral, $e_l\circ e_r=e_l$. Hence $e_l=e_r$.”

Seen in: the neutral element is unique (1); left-neutral is right-neutral (4).

Sneak in the neutral element

When: you want to turn one element into another and only know equations of the form “something $=e$”. Write $x=x\circ e$, replace $e$ by a product that equals it, then rebracket.

Sentences: “$x'=x'\circ e=x'\circ(x\circ x'')$ $=(x'\circ x)\circ x''=e\circ x''=x''$.”

Seen in: inverses are unique (1).

Cancel with an inverse

When: an equation has the same element on both sides of a group product, like $u=u\circ u$ or $a\circ x=a\circ y$. Multiply both sides by the inverse of that element, from the correct side.

Sentences: “Multiplying $\varphi(e_G)=\varphi(e_G)\star\varphi(e_G)$ from the left by $\varphi(e_G)^{-1}$ gives $e_H=\varphi(e_G)$.”

Seen in: a homomorphism maps $e$ to $e$ (1); cancellation in a field, $b=c\iff ab=ac$ for $a\ne0$ (4).

Two names, subtract

When: an operation on classes is defined through representatives (“$\bar a+\bar b:=\overline{a+b}$”) and you must show it is well defined. Take two names of each class, write “difference $=$ multiple of $n$”, subtract the two results and regroup.

Sentences: “Let $\bar a=\overline{a'}$ and $\bar b=\overline{b'}$, so $a-a'=ln$, $b-b'=kn$. Then $(a+b)-(a'+b')=(l+k)n$, so $\overline{a+b}=\overline{a'+b'}$.”

Seen in: addition and multiplication on $\mathbb Z/n$ (1). It returns for quotient spaces (lesson 9).

One counterexample is enough

When: you must show that something is not a group, ring or field. An axiom says “for all”, so one element (or one pair) where it fails is a complete proof. Name it and compute.

Sentences: “$(\mathbb Z,\cdot)$ is not a group: $2$ has no inverse, since $2\cdot x=1$ has no solution $x\in\mathbb Z$.” “$\mathbb Z/4$ is not a field: $\bar2\cdot\bar2=\bar0$.”

Seen in: non-examples of groups and fields, $(\mathbb R,\ast)$ with $a\ast b=a+ab+b$ (1); $\exists\forall$ versus $\forall\exists$, true/false questions on equivalences (2); subsets that are not subspaces, $\deg(f+g)$ (5).

Decide membership element by element

When: two sets must be equal ($A=B$). Show “$t\in A\iff t\in B$” for an arbitrary $t$, often by splitting into cases ($t\in z$ or $t\notin z$).

Sentences: “Let $t\in M$. Case $t\in z$: … Case $t\notin z$: … In both cases $t\in A\iff t\in B$, so $A=B$.”

Seen in: associativity of the symmetric difference, the ring $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle,\cap)$ (1); De Morgan for sets, $|\mathcal P(M)|=2^n$, classes form a partition (3).

From lesson 2

Check with a truth table

When: two expressions built from $\neg,\wedge,\vee,\to,\leftrightarrow$ only (no quantifiers) must be compared, or one must be shown to be a tautology. List every combination of truth values and compare the columns. To show “not equivalent”, one row where they differ is enough.

Sentences: “Both sides depend only on the truth values of $A$ and $B$. In the case $A=B=1$ … in the other three cases … So both sides always have the same truth value.” “With $A=1$, $B=0$ the left side is true and the right side false, so they are not equivalent.”

Seen in: De Morgan, the chain rule, FS19 question I, HS18 question V (2).

A chain of named equivalences

When: an equivalence must be shown and a truth table is too long or impossible (quantifiers). Rewrite step by step, one known equivalence per step, and name it. Remove arrows first: $A\to B\iff\neg A\vee B$.

Sentences: “$A\to B$ $\iff\neg A\vee B$ $\iff B\vee\neg A$ $\iff\neg\neg B\vee\neg A$ $\iff\neg B\to\neg A$, using in turn: implication as OR, commutativity, double negation, implication as OR.”

Seen in: the contrapositive, $\neg(A\to B)\iff A\wedge\neg B$, Quiz 1, Quiz 5 (2).

Flip every quantifier, negate the core

When: you need the negation of a statement with quantifiers: to show something is not a group, basis, homomorphism; to start a proof by contradiction; to see what a counterexample must look like. Put all quantifiers in front, flip each one ($\forall\leftrightarrow\exists$), keep the bounds, negate the core ($\neg(A\to B)$ becomes $A\wedge\neg B$, De Morgan for $\wedge,\vee$).

Sentences: “The negation of $\forall a,b\in R:\ ab=0\to(a=0\vee b=0)$ is $\exists a,b\in R:\ ab=0\wedge a\ne0\wedge b\ne0$.”

Seen in: negating a bounded quantifier, zero divisors, “has no neutral element”, convergence, the definitions of lesson 1 (2).

Take an arbitrary element

When: the statement to prove starts with $\forall x\in M$. Write “Let $x\in M$ be arbitrary”, prove the claim for this $x$ using nothing but $x\in M$, and end with “since $x$ was arbitrary, the claim holds for all $x\in M$”.

Sentences: “Let $e\in\mathbb N_{>0}$ be arbitrary. … So $e$ is not neutral. Since $e$ was arbitrary, $(\mathbb N_{>0},+)$ has no neutral element.”

Seen in: $\exists\forall$ implies $\forall\exists$, $(\mathbb N_{>0},+)$ has no neutral element (2); implicitly in every group check (1); compositions of injective and surjective maps, no largest prime (3).

Name a witness

When: the statement to prove is $\exists y\ldots$ (also inside: $\forall x\,\exists y$). Write the $y$ down explicitly, allowed to depend on everything chosen before it, and check that it works. For $\exists!$ add a second part: two such elements are equal.

Sentences: “Given $x\in\mathbb Z$, put $y:=-x$; then $x+y=0$.” “Take $a=\bar2$, $b=\bar3$ in $\mathbb Z/6$: $ab=\bar0$ although $a,b\ne\bar0$.”

Seen in: $\forall x\,\exists y\,(x+y=0)$, zero divisors in $\mathbb Z/6$, the order of quantifiers (2); neutral elements and inverses (1); surjective compositions, Cantor's set, the larger prime (3).

From lesson 3

Prove the contrapositive

When: the claim is $A\Rightarrow B$, and $\neg B$ gives you something concrete to compute with while $A$ does not. Typical: $B$ is a “not” statement (irrational, not divisible, injective written as “different inputs give different outputs”). Show $\neg B\Rightarrow\neg A$ instead; it is the same statement.

Sentences: “We prove the contrapositive. Assume $3\nmid n$, so $n=3k+1$ or $n=3k+2$. Then $n^2=\dots$ leaves remainder $1$, so $3\nmid n^2$.”

Seen in: $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$, $3\mid n^2\Rightarrow3\mid n$ (3); the two forms of linear independence (6); zero divisors (script Note 1.3.6, lesson 2); fields have no zero divisors, $\mathbb Z/n$ for $n$ not prime (4).

Induction: base, hypothesis, step

When: the claim is “for all $n\ge n_0$”, and the case $n+1$ is the case $n$ plus one more piece (one more term, one more element, one more vector). State $A(n)$, check $A(n_0)$, assume $A(n)$, split off the last piece, use the hypothesis.

Sentences: “Base case $n=0$: … Step: assume the claim for some $n\ge0$. Then $\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\dots=\sum_{k=1}^n\dots+(\text{last term})=\dots$ by the induction hypothesis. By induction, the claim holds for all $n\ge0$.”

Seen in: $|\mathcal P(M)|=2^n$, FS19 problem 6 (a), HS21 question XII (3).

Assume the opposite

When: the claim says something does not exist or is not possible: no surjection, no largest element, uncountable, irrational. Assume it exists, give it a name, and derive a statement together with its negation.

Sentences: “Assume, to the contrary, that $g(a_0)=Y$ for some $a_0\in M$. Then $a_0\in Y\iff a_0\notin g(a_0)\iff a_0\notin Y$, a contradiction. Hence …”

Seen in: Cantor's theorem, Russell's paradox and the barber, $\sqrt3\notin\mathbb Q$ (3).

Pigeonhole: more objects than boxes

When: you need two of finitely many things to coincide, or a map between finite sets to fail injectivity. Name the objects, name the boxes, count: more objects than boxes means two objects share a box. Then use the coincidence.

Sentences: “The $p$ classes $\bar a^0,\dots,\bar a^{p-1}$ lie in the $p-1$ nonzero classes, so $\bar a^s=\bar a^t$ for some $s<t$.”

Seen in: inverses in $\mathbb Z/p$, injective self-maps of finite sets are surjective, remainders of $n+1$ integers (3); Lemma 1.3.14 (4).

From lesson 4

Show it is the inverse, then use uniqueness

When: the claim is “$x$ equals $-y$” or “$x$ equals $y^{-1}$”. Don't compute $-y$; check the defining property instead: $y+x=0$ (or $yx=1$). Inverses are unique, so $x$ is the inverse.

Sentences: “$ab+a(-b)=a(b+(-b))=a\cdot0=0$. So $a(-b)$ is an additive inverse of $ab$, and by uniqueness of the inverse $a(-b)=-(ab)$.”

Seen in: $a(-b)=-(ab)$, $(-1)a=-a$, $(xy)^{-1}=x^{-1}y^{-1}$, $\bar{\bar x}=x$ (4); in vector spaces, $(-1)v=-v$ (5).

Write $0$ as $0+0$ and distribute

When: you must prove something about $0\cdot x$ (or $\lambda\cdot0$) from axioms. Distributivity is the only axiom that links $+$ and $\cdot$, so produce a sum: $0\cdot x=(0+0)x=0\cdot x+0\cdot x$, then cancel one copy by adding its negative.

Sentences: “As $0+0=0$, $0\cdot a=(0+0)a=0\cdot a+0\cdot a$. Adding $-(0\cdot a)$ to both sides gives $0=0\cdot a$.”

Seen in: $0\cdot a=0$ in every ring (4); $0\cdot v=0_V$ and $\lambda\cdot0_V=0_V$, Serie 2 exercise 10 (5).

Injective on a finite set means surjective

When: you need an element with some property (an inverse, a solution) in a finite structure. Find an injective map from a finite set to itself whose values are what you want; it is automatically surjective (pigeonhole), so the value you need is hit.

Sentences: “$m_a:b\mapsto ab$ is injective on the finite set $R\setminus\{0\}$, hence surjective; so $ab=1$ for some $b$.”

Seen in: a finite ring without zero divisors is a field, $\mathbb Z/p$ is a field (4). Returns as “injective $\iff$ surjective” for linear maps between spaces of equal finite dimension (lessons 11–12).

From lesson 5

The subspace test: zero, sums, multiples

When: “show that $W$ is a subspace” or “is $W$ a vector space?” for a subset of a known vector space. Check $0_V\in W$, closed under $+$, closed under scalars; then all eight axioms follow (Lemma 2.0.7). For “not a subspace”, one witness: $0_V\notin W$, or a sum, or a multiple that leaves $W$. Test $0_V$ first.

Sentences: “The zero sequence lies in $W$. Let $x,y\in W$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb R$. Then … so $x+y\in W$ and $\lambda x\in W$. Hence $W$ is a subspace.”

Seen in: HS21 question VI, FS19 problem 2 (a), Fibonacci sequences, $\{A\mid A^2=0\}$, unions of subspaces (5).

Check the axioms entry by entry

When: the operations are defined componentwise, termwise or pointwise ($K^n$, matrices, sequences, functions, polynomials). Two such objects are equal iff all entries are equal, so each axiom reduces to the same rule in $K$, applied in each entry.

Sentences: “The $i$-th component of $(a+b)v$ is $(a+b)v_i=av_i+bv_i$, the $i$-th component of $av+bv$. As this holds for all $i$, $(a+b)v=av+bv$.”

Seen in: $K^n$ is a vector space, Serie 2 exercises 11, 12, 14 (5).

From lesson 6

Nontrivial combination: solve for the vector with nonzero coefficient

When: you have $\sum a_iv_i=0$ with some $a_j\ne0$ and need “some vector is a combination of the others” (or of the earlier ones). Pick $j$ (the largest index with $a_j\ne0$ if order matters), move $a_jv_j$ to one side and divide by $a_j$.

Sentences: “Let $j:=\max\{i\mid a_i\ne0\}$. Then $v_j=-\frac{a_1}{a_j}v_1-\dots-\frac{a_{j-1}}{a_j}v_{j-1}$, so $v_j\in\mathrm{Sp}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1})$.”

Seen in: Satz 4.0, Lemma 2.3.2 / Lemma 4.2 (6); the Steinitz exchange (7).

Two representations: subtract them

When: you must show a representation is unique, or use that it is. Write $v=\sum a_iv_i=\sum b_iv_i$, subtract to get $\sum(a_i-b_i)v_i=0$, and use independence. Conversely, a nontrivial representation of $0$ added to one of $v$ gives a second one.

Sentences: “Then $0=v-v=\sum(a_i-b_i)v_i$, so $a_i=b_i$ for all $i$ by linear independence.”

Seen in: basis = unique coordinates, Serie 3 exercise 15, mock HS20 (7) (6).

From lesson 7

Bring one vector in, drop one: the exchange step

When: you must compare the length of an independent list with that of a spanning list, or replace basis vectors by given independent vectors. Add the new vector to the spanning list (now dependent, Lemma 2.3.5), drop the first vector that is a combination of the vectors before it (Lemma 2.3.2), and note that it is never one of the independent vectors (Lemma 2.3.4).

Sentences: “The list $u_1,\dots,u_j,w_1,\dots,w_r$ is linearly dependent, since $w_1,\dots,w_r$ together with $u_1,\dots,u_{j-1}$ span $V$. By Lemma 2.3.2 we can remove a vector without changing the span, and by Lemma 2.3.4 it is one of the $w_i$.”

Seen in: the exchange lemma / Steinitz, Serie 3 exercise 16, Quiz 3 (7).

Count first: $n$ vectors in an $n$-dimensional space

When: you must show that some vectors form a basis, or decide a true/false statement about spanning or independence. Compare the number of vectors with $\dim V$. Exactly $\dim V$: check only independence (or only spanning). Fewer: they never span. More: they are always dependent.

Sentences: “Since $\dim V=n$ and $v_1,\dots,v_n$ are linearly independent, they form a basis of $V$ (Theorem 2.3.15).” · “Four vectors in $\mathbb R^3$ are linearly dependent (Corollary 2.3.17).”

Seen in: FS17 problem 3 (a) the short way, FS20 VII, HS21 VIII and XV (7).

From lesson 8

A vector written two ways lies in the overlap

When: an equation has a $U$-part on one side and a $W$-part on the other: $u_1-u_2=w_2-w_1$, or $\sum c_iw_i=-\sum a_ip_i-\dots$. The common value lies in $U\cap W$; then use what you know about the intersection ($=\{0\}$, or spanned by given vectors).

Sentences: “The left side lies in $U$ and the right side in $W$, so $v\in U\cap W$.”

Seen in: the dimension formula, direct sums, FS17 problem 5 (c), complements (8).

Bound an intersection with the dimension formula

When: you must show two subspaces meet (or how much). $\dim(U\cap W)$ $=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U+W)$ $\ge\dim U+\dim W-\dim V$.

Sentences: “Since $U+W\subseteq V$, $\dim(U\cap W)\ge\dim U+\dim W-\dim V\ge1$, so $U\cap W\ne\{0\}$.”

Seen in: FS20 III, HS21 VIII (d), two planes in $\mathbb R^3$ (7, 8).

From lesson 9

Compare cosets by subtracting representatives

When: you must decide or use $a+W=b+W$. Replace it by $a-b\in W$ (and back). Also: any element of a coset can be used as its name.

Sentences: “By the coset rule, $a+W=a'+W$ means $a-a'\in W$.” · “Since $x\in a+W$, we have $x+W=a+W$.”

Seen in: the coset rules, the quotient space, Serie 4 exercise 22 (c) (9).

Well defined: change the representative, show nothing changes

When: a map or operation is defined on classes through a representative ($[a]\mapsto\dots a\dots$). Take two representatives of the same class, translate “same class” into a condition ($a-a'\in W$), and show the two values agree. To disprove, give two representatives with different values.

Sentences: “Let $a+W=a'+W$, i.e. $a-a'\in W$. Then $(a+b)-(a'+b)=a-a'\in W$, so the result does not depend on the choice of representative.”

Seen in: the operations on $V/W$, Serie 5 exercise 24, FS17 problem 6 (b) (9).

From lesson 10

Two linear maps that agree on a basis are equal

When: you must show $f=g$ for two linear maps or functionals (often $f=\sum_i f(b_i)b_i^*$). Do not test every vector: test the basis vectors $b_1,\dots,b_n$ only, then cite the principle. It also works with any spanning list.

Sentences: “Both sides are linear and agree on the basis $b_1,\dots,b_n$; since a linear map is determined by its values on a basis, they are equal.”

Seen in: values on a basis determine the functional, the dual basis is a basis (10).

Define a linear map by its values on a basis

When: you need a functional (or linear map) with prescribed values $a_i$ on a basis: the dual basis, a functional that is $1$ on every basis vector, Lagrange polynomials. Define it on coordinates, $f(\sum\alpha_ib_i):=\sum\alpha_ia_i$; it is well defined because coordinates are unique.

Sentences: “Since $b_1,\dots,b_n$ is a basis, there is a unique linear $f:V\to K$ with $f(b_i)=a_i$ (extend linearly).”

Seen in: the dual basis, Proof C, the infinite dual set, Serie 5 exercise 26, Quiz 4 (10).

Apply to a basis vector: the delta picks one term

When: a sum $\sum_i\lambda_ib_i^*$ is given (equal to $0$, or to some functional) and you want the coefficients. Evaluate at $b_j$: since $b_i^*(b_j)=\delta_{ij}$, only the term $i=j$ survives. Also for vectors: $b_j^*(\sum\alpha_ib_i)=\alpha_j$.

Sentences: “Evaluating at $b_j$ gives $0=\sum_i\lambda_i\,b_i^*(b_j)=\sum_i\lambda_i\delta_{ij}=\lambda_j$.”

Seen in: independence of the dual basis, the coordinate formulas, the infinite dual set, HS17 problem 4 (10).

Equal maps: compare the values at every point

When: an identity between functions ($(f+g)+h=f+(g+h)$, $\Phi(f+g)=\Phi(f)+\Phi(g)$) has to be shown. Two maps with the same domain and target are equal when they agree at every point; at one point the identity becomes a rule of the field.

Sentences: “Let $x\in V$ be arbitrary. Then $((f+g)+h)(x)=(f(x)+g(x))+h(x)=f(x)+(g(x)+h(x))=(f+(g+h))(x)$; hence the maps are equal.”

Seen in: $V^*$ is a vector space, FS17 problem 6 (c) (10).

From lesson 11

The matrix column by column

When: “find the matrix of $T$ with respect to $B$ and $C$”, or any claim about matrices of maps (sums, multiples, uniqueness). Work one column at a time: column $j$ is the coordinate vector $[T(v_j)]_C$ of the image of the $j$-th basis vector. Equal matrices means equal columns means equal values on the basis.

Sentences: “$T(v_1)=\dots=a_{11}w_1+\dots+a_{m1}w_m$, so the first column is $(a_{11},\dots,a_{m1})$.” “The $j$-th column of $[T+S]^B_C$ is $[Tv_j+Sv_j]_C=[Tv_j]_C+[Sv_j]_C$.”

Seen in: the matrix computes the map, $T\mapsto[T]^B_C$ is linear and bijective, the matrix of $p\mapsto p'$ (HS17, FS17, HS21), Serie 7 exercise 30 (11).

Injective: check the kernel

When: you must show that a linear map is injective (or decide whether it is). Instead of comparing two arbitrary vectors, check only the vectors sent to $0$. To refute injectivity, one nonzero kernel vector is enough.

Sentences: “Let $T(v)=0$. Then … so $v=0$. Hence $\mathrm{Ker}(T)=\{0\}$ and $T$ is injective.” “$T(1)=0$ with $1\ne0$, so $T$ is not injective.” Behind it: $Tv_1=Tv_2\Rightarrow T(v_1-v_2)=0$.

Seen in: the isomorphism theorem, FS17 problem 3 (c), HS21 problem 3 (e) (11).

From lesson 12

Turn matrices into maps and back

When: a statement about matrices (associativity, distributivity, inverses) looks like a messy computation with indices. Replace each matrix $A$ by the map $m_A:x\mapsto Ax$, prove the statement for maps (often it is obvious there: composition is associative), and translate back with $[S\circ T]=[S][T]$ and $[S+T]=[S]+[T]$.

Sentences: “Let $m_A,m_B,m_C$ be the maps with standard matrices $A,B,C$. Since $(m_A\circ m_B)\circ m_C=m_A\circ(m_B\circ m_C)$, taking matrices gives $(AB)C=A(BC)$.”

Seen in: associativity and distributivity of the matrix product, $A\in\mathrm{GL}_n(K)\iff m_A$ is an isomorphism, $\dim\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)=mn$, Serie 7 exercise 31 (12).

From lesson 13

Sandwich the map between identities

When: you need the matrix of a map in new bases, or the relation between two matrices of one map. Write $T=\mathrm{Id}_W\circ T\circ\mathrm{Id}_V$, put the old and new bases on the arrows, and take matrices with $[S\circ T]=[S][T]$: the identities become transition matrices.

Sentences: “Since $T=\mathrm{Id}_W\circ T\circ\mathrm{Id}_V$, read as $(V,B')\to(V,B)\to(W,C)\to(W,C')$, Lemma 3.3.29 gives $[T]^{B'}_{C'}=[\mathrm{Id}_W]^C_{C'}[T]^B_C[\mathrm{Id}_V]^{B'}_B$.”

Seen in: the change-of-basis formula $[T]_{B'}=Q^{-1}[T]_BQ$, similar matrices are one endomorphism in two bases, equivalent matrices (script Exercise 3.3.36), FS25 multiple choice (13).

From lesson 14

Dual maps: test at a vector

When: a statement about the dual map $T^*$ (linear, a composition rule, a kernel or an image) looks abstract because it lives in a space of functionals. Two functionals are equal if they agree at every vector, and $(T^*\psi)(v)=\psi(Tv)$ turns everything into a statement about $T$ and $\psi$ at one vector.

Sentences: “For every $v\in V$: $(T^*(\alpha\psi_1+\psi_2))(v)=(\alpha\psi_1+\psi_2)(Tv)=\alpha\psi_1(Tv)+\psi_2(Tv)$.”

Seen in: $T^*$ is linear (HS24, 3.A4; FS25, 4.A4), $(S\circ T)^*=T^*\circ S^*$, $[T^*]=[T]^T$, $(\mathrm{Im}\,T)^0=\mathrm{Ker}\,T^*$, FS18 problem 4 (14).

From lesson 15

Choose a basis adapted to a subspace

When: a statement compares a subspace with the whole space: a kernel inside $V$, an image inside $W$, a subspace and its annihilator, a matrix that should become simple. Start with a basis of the small space and extend it to a basis of the big one (lesson 7). Then every vector splits into a part you control and a part you added, and the matrix of a map in such a basis has blocks of zeros.

Sentences: “Let $(u_1,\dots,u_k)$ be a basis of $\mathrm{Ker}\,T$ and extend it to a basis $(u_1,\dots,u_k,v_1,\dots,v_r)$ of $V$.”

Seen in: rank–nullity (Proof B of lesson 15), the normal form $D_r$ (HS17 problem 3 (a)), $\dim U^0=\dim V-\dim U$ (FS25 problem 4), $(\mathrm{Ker}\,T)^0=\mathrm{Im}\,T^*$, the projection matrix of FS26 question 5.

Count with rank–nullity

When: a question about a linear map between finite-dimensional spaces asks whether it can be injective or surjective, or asks for the dimension of a kernel or an image. One of the two dimensions is often easy; $\dim V=\dim\mathrm{Ker}\,T+\dim\mathrm{Im}\,T$ gives the other, and a dimension that is too big or too small settles the question without computing anything.

Sentences: “By rank–nullity, $\dim\mathrm{Im}\,T=\dim V-\dim\mathrm{Ker}\,T=4-2=2<3=\dim W$, so $T$ is not surjective.”

Seen in: FS17 (xvi), FS18 (xii)–(xv), HS20 (8) and (12), FS19 (X), one-sided inverses of square matrices (HS18 (X)), injective iff surjective for $\dim V=\dim W$.

Restrict the map to a subspace, then count

When: the dimension of some subspace $V'$ of $V$ is wanted (a preimage, a kernel of a power, a sum with a kernel). Restrict $T$ to $V'$, identify the kernel and the image of the restriction (usually $\mathrm{Ker}\,T$ or $V'\cap\mathrm{Ker}\,T$, and $T(V')$), and apply rank–nullity to the restriction instead of to $T$.

Sentences: “Let $S:=T|_{V'}$. Then $\mathrm{Ker}\,S=V'\cap\mathrm{Ker}\,T$ and $\mathrm{Im}\,S=T(V')$, so $\dim V'=\dim(V'\cap\mathrm{Ker}\,T)+\dim T(V')$.”

Seen in: $\dim T^{-1}(U)=\mathrm{nullity}(T)+\dim(\mathrm{Im}\,T\cap U)$ (FS18 problem 3 (c)), $V=U+\mathrm{Ker}\,T\iff\mathrm{rank}\,T=\mathrm{rank}\,T|_U$ (HS17 problem 3 (d)), $\dim\mathrm{Ker}(T^2)\le2\dim\mathrm{Ker}\,T$, the rank of a composition.

Undo the row operation

When: a statement says that some operation changes nothing: the span of the rows, the solutions of a system, the rank. Show one inclusion because the new objects are built from the old ones, and the other inclusion because the operation can be undone by an operation of the same kind ($L_i+\lambda L_j$ by $L_i-\lambda L_j$, $\lambda L_i$ by $\lambda^{-1}L_i$, a swap by the same swap).

Sentences: “Conversely $u_i=(u_i+\lambda u_j)-\lambda u_j$ is a combination of the new rows, so the old span lies in the new one.”

Seen in: row operations keep the row space (script Lemma 2.3.23), and the solutions of $Ax=0$; later: elementary matrices are invertible (lesson 17).

Row operations keep the column relations

When: a question asks about the columns of a matrix (independent? a basis of the column space? which column is a combination of which?) and you have only eliminated rows. A relation $\sum_jx_ja_j=0$ is a solution of $Ax=0$, and row operations do not change those solutions. So read the relations off the echelon form and use the pivot columns of the original matrix.

Sentences: “The reduced form has pivots in columns 1 and 3, so the first and third columns of $A$ are a basis of $\mathrm{ColS}(A)$, and column 4 $=-$column 1 $+2\cdot$column 3, as in the reduced form.”

Seen in: script Example 2.3.33 and Corollary 2.3.34, HS17 problem 3 (b)–(c), every “basis of the image” computation.

Swap rows and columns with the transpose

When: a statement about rows looks hard but the same statement about columns is known (or the other way round). $\mathrm{RowS}(A)=\mathrm{ColS}(A^T)$, $(XY)^T=Y^TX^T$ and $(X^{-1})^T=(X^T)^{-1}$ turn it into the known one.

Sentences: “$\mathrm{row\,rank}(PAQ)=\mathrm{col\,rank}(Q^TA^TP^T)$ $=\mathrm{col\,rank}(A^T)=\mathrm{row\,rank}(A)$.”

Seen in: the script's proof of row rank $=$ column rank (Lemma 3.4.4), $[T^*]=[T]^T$ (lesson 14), $\mathrm{rank}(A)=\mathrm{rank}(A^T)$.

From lesson 17

Parametrise by the free variables

When: a question asks for all solutions of a linear system, a basis of a kernel, or the dimension of a solution space. Reduce to reduced row echelon form, choose the free variables as parameters and solve each row for its pivot variable. Setting one free variable to $1$ and the others to $0$ gives one basis vector of the kernel.

Sentences: “Free: $x_2=s$, $x_4=t$. Then $x_1=-3s-2t$ and $x_3=t$, so $\mathrm{Sol}(A,0)=\{s(-3,1,0,0)+t(-2,0,1,1)\}$, of dimension $2=4-\mathrm{rank}(A)$.”

Seen in: HS20 problem 4, FS20 problem 2 (a), every kernel basis computation; with a particular solution added, every inhomogeneous system.

Write each row operation as a matrix

When: a statement about Gaussian elimination should become a statement about matrices: inverses, products, transposes. Each row operation is a left multiplication by an elementary matrix, so a whole elimination is one product $T=T_k\cdots T_1$, and “$A$ reduces to $I_n$” becomes $TA=I_n$.

Sentences: “The row operations are left multiplications by elementary matrices $T_1,\dots,T_k$, so $T_k\cdots T_1A=I_n$ and $A^{-1}=T_k\cdots T_1$.”

Seen in: every invertible matrix is a product of elementary matrices, Gauss–Jordan, the LR decomposition (FS18 problem 5), inverses of triangular matrices; later the determinant rules (lesson 18).
