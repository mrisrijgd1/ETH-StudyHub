0. Warm-up: closed sets and unions (5 min)

In lesson 5 (Exercise 6) you proved: a union of open sets is open, and an intersection of two open sets is open. The mirror rules for closed sets follow by taking complements. The tool is De Morgan's law (Regeln von De Morgan): the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements, and vice versa.

$$\begin{aligned}&\mathbb{R}\setminus(A\cup B)=(\mathbb{R}\setminus A)\cap(\mathbb{R}\setminus B)\\&\mathbb{R}\setminus(A\cap B)=(\mathbb{R}\setminus A)\cup(\mathbb{R}\setminus B)\end{aligned}.$$

Claim. If $A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ are closed, then $A\cup B$ is closed.

Proof. $A$ and $B$ are closed, so $\mathbb{R}\setminus A$ and $\mathbb{R}\setminus B$ are open (definition of closed). Their intersection is open (lesson 5, Exercise 6(d)). By De Morgan, that intersection is $\mathbb{R}\setminus(A\cup B)$. So the complement of $A\cup B$ is open, which means $A\cup B$ is closed. ∎

In the same way, an intersection of any number of closed sets is closed (its complement is a union of open sets). Summary, worth memorising for true/false blocks:

1. The story: the lowest ceiling

The Dragon Guild keeps its dragons in a great hall. Each dragon hovers at its own height, and the heights form a set $X$ of real numbers.

The builders must put in a ceiling: a height that every dragon is at or below. Many heights work. A ceiling at a thousand metres is safe, but wasteful. The Guildmaster wants the lowest possible ceiling.

Sometimes there is a tallest dragon, and the lowest ceiling sits right on top of its head. But one season the dragons hover at heights $0,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac23,\ \tfrac34,\dots$, getting ever closer to $1$. No dragon is the tallest, because there's always a taller one. Yet the lowest ceiling clearly exists: it's at height $1$, touched by no dragon, but approached by all of them.

The builders' test for “this is the lowest ceiling” has two parts. First: no dragon is above it. Second: lower it by any amount, however tiny, and some dragon pokes through.

And why must a lowest ceiling exist at all? Because the realm of real numbers has no holes. That's the completeness axiom from lesson 4.

In the formal language: the ceilings are upper bounds, the tallest dragon is the maximum, and the lowest ceiling is the supremum.

2. Maximum and minimum

Maximum (Definition 2.56, p. 105). $x_0=\max X$ if

$$x_0\in X\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall x\in X:\ x\le x_0.$$

Both parts matter: $x_0$ must be in the set, and nothing in the set is bigger. The minimum (Definition 2.57) is the same with $\ge$.

  • Unique if it exists. If $x_0$ and $x_0'$ are both maxima, then $x_0\le x_0'$ (because $x_0\in X$ and $x_0'$ is a maximum) and $x_0'\le x_0$ (the same reason, swapped). So $x_0=x_0'$.
  • Every non-empty finite set has a maximum. For example $\max\{3,-1,7\}=7$.
  • $[a,b]$ has maximum $b$.
  • $(0,1)$ has no maximum. Proof by contradiction: suppose $m=\max(0,1)$. Then $0<m<1$, and the midpoint $\tfrac{m+1}{2}$ satisfies $m<\tfrac{m+1}{2}<1$. So it's an element of $(0,1)$ bigger than $m$, contradicting $m$ being the maximum. ∎
  • $\mathbb{R}$ and $[a,\infty)$ have no maximum, because $x+1>x$ for every $x$.

3. Bounds

Definition 2.58 (p. 105). $s\in\mathbb{R}$ is an upper bound (obere Schranke) of $X$ if

$$\forall x\in X:\ x\le s.$$

$X$ is bounded above (von oben beschränkt) if it has at least one upper bound. Lower bounds and “bounded below” are the same with $\ge$. $X$ is bounded (beschränkt) if it is bounded above and below.

Notice the difference to the maximum: an upper bound does not have to lie in $X$. For $(0,1)$, the numbers $1$, $2$ and $100$ are all upper bounds, and none of them is in the set. The number $0.9$ is not an upper bound, because $0.95\in(0,1)$ is bigger.

4. The supremum

Satz 2.59 (p. 105–106). Let $X\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be non-empty and bounded above. Then $X$ has a least upper bound $s_0=\sup X$. Formally, $s_0$ is characterised by:

(1) $s_0$ is an upper bound:

$$\forall x\in X:\ x\le s_0.$$

(2) $s_0$ is at most every upper bound:

$$\forall s\in\mathbb{R}:\ \big(\,(\forall x\in X:\ x\le s)\ \Longrightarrow\ s_0\le s\,\big).$$

Read aloud: “whenever $s$ is an upper bound, $s_0\le s$.”

In practice, condition (2) is awkward to check, because you'd have to think about all upper bounds at once. The script gives an equivalent version that is much more usable:

(2′) Anything smaller is not an upper bound:

$$\forall\,\varepsilon>0\ \ \exists\,x\in X:\ x>s_0-\varepsilon.$$

Read aloud: “for every $\varepsilon>0$, the number $s_0-\varepsilon$ is beaten by some element of $X$.” (Lower the ceiling by $\varepsilon$, and some dragon pokes through.) It's a lesson 1 duel: the Doubter picks $\varepsilon$, and the Champion must find an element of $X$ above $s_0-\varepsilon$.

Why (2) and (2′) say the same thing.

  • (2) ⇒ (2′). Let $\varepsilon>0$. Then $s_0-\varepsilon<s_0$. By (2), every upper bound is $\ge s_0$, so $s_0-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound. “Not an upper bound” is the negation of $\forall x\in X: x\le s_0-\varepsilon$, which is $\exists x\in X: x>s_0-\varepsilon$. That's (2′).
  • (2′) ⇒ (2). Suppose $s$ is an upper bound with $s<s_0$. Put $\varepsilon=s_0-s>0$. By (2′) there is an $x\in X$ with $x>s_0-\varepsilon=s$, so $s$ is not an upper bound after all. Contradiction. So every upper bound satisfies $s\ge s_0$, which is (2).

Proof A: the supremum exists (Satz 2.59)

The idea. Put the set $X$ on the left and all its upper bounds on the right. They are two armies with $X$ entirely to the left, so the completeness axiom puts a number $c$ between them. Being right of $X$ makes $c$ an upper bound; being left of all upper bounds makes it the least one.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • To show: some $c$ satisfies (1) and (2). Existence of a number: the only tool is the completeness axiom (lesson 4), so we must build two sets and check its three conditions.
  • (2) mentions “all upper bounds”, which suggests the second set.
The storyWhat you write

Line up all the possible ceilings as the second army.

Let $Y=\{s\in\mathbb R:\ s\text{ is an upper bound of }X\}$. $Y\neq\emptyset$ since $X$ is bounded above, and $X\neq\emptyset$ by assumption.

Every dragon is below every ceiling.

For $x\in X$ and $y\in Y$ we have $x\le y$, by the definition of an upper bound.

Completeness puts a height between the dragons and the ceilings.

By the completeness axiom there is $c\in\mathbb R$ with $x\le c\le y$ for all $x\in X$, $y\in Y$.

Above every dragon, below every ceiling: the lowest ceiling.

$x\le c$ for all $x\in X$ is (1), and $c\le y$ for every upper bound $y$ is (2). So $c=\sup X$. ∎

Moves used: two armies for completeness

The proof in one piece

Let $Y$ be the set of upper bounds of $X$. Both sets are non-empty and $x\le y$ for all $x\in X$, $y\in Y$. By completeness there is $c$ with $x\le c\le y$ for all such $x,y$: so $c$ is an upper bound of $X$, and at most every upper bound. Hence $c=\sup X$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why is $Y$ non-empty? Name the hypothesis you use.

    Answer

    Because $X$ is bounded above: by definition it has at least one upper bound, and that upper bound lies in $Y$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the last line: which inequality gives condition (1), and which gives condition (2)?

    Answer

    $x\le c$ for all $x\in X$ says $c$ is an upper bound, which is (1). $c\le y$ for every $y\in Y$ says $c$ is at most every upper bound, which is (2). So $c=\sup X$.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Uniqueness: if $s_0$ and $s_0'$ both satisfy (1) and (2), each is an upper bound, so by (2) each is $\le$ the other. Hence they're equal.

Supremum versus maximum

  • If $\max X$ exists, then $\sup X=\max X$. (The maximum is an upper bound, and every upper bound $s$ is $\ge$ it, because the maximum is an element of $X$.)
  • If $\sup X\in X$, then $\sup X$ is the maximum (it's an element that is an upper bound).
  • So: the maximum exists exactly when the supremum belongs to the set. The supremum always exists (for non-empty, bounded-above sets), and the maximum is the special case where it's attained.

Infimum

The infimum $\inf X$ (Übung 2.61, p. 107) is the greatest lower bound. It exists for every non-empty set that is bounded below. Its two conditions mirror the supremum's:

$$\text{(1) }\ \forall x\in X:\ x\ge i_0,\qquad\text{(2′) }\ \forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists x\in X:\ x<i_0+\varepsilon.$$

A useful shortcut: $\inf X=-\sup(-X)$, where $-X=\{-x: x\in X\}$. Flipping the line upside down turns floors into ceilings.

When there is no ceiling: improper values (§2.5.3, p. 108)

The script extends the definition to every subset of $\mathbb{R}$, using the symbols $\pm\infty$ (which are not real numbers):

  • If $X$ is not bounded above, $\sup X=+\infty$. If $X$ is not bounded below, $\inf X=-\infty$. For example $\sup\mathbb{N}=+\infty$.
  • $\sup\emptyset=-\infty$ and $\inf\emptyset=+\infty$. This looks strange, but it's consistent: every real number is an upper bound of $\emptyset$ (there is no element to violate it), so there's no least one.

5. The exam recipe for “compute sup X and prove it”

Exam technique
  1. Guess $s_0$. Write out a few elements, or sketch them on the line.
  2. Prove (1): show $x\le s_0$ for an arbitrary $x\in X$.
  3. Prove (2′): let $\varepsilon>0$ be arbitrary, and name an explicit $x\in X$ with $x>s_0-\varepsilon$. Here the Archimedean principle (section 6) is often what you need.
  4. Max or not? Check whether $s_0\in X$. If yes, $s_0=\max X$. If no, there is no maximum.

For the infimum it's the same with (1) $x\ge i_0$ and (2′) $x<i_0+\varepsilon$.

Proof B: an open set never contains its supremum (Einsiedler HS21 #7(d))

Claim. If $A\subseteq\mathbb R$ is open, non-empty and bounded above, then $\sup A\notin A$. (This is Lemma 10.13, p. 483.)

The idea. If the ceiling were a citizen of an open kingdom, it would have breathing space, including a bit above itself. That bit is in $A$ and above the ceiling: impossible.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • A “not in” claim: contradiction. “Suppose $s=\sup A\in A$.”
  • Open gives, for the point $s\in A$, some $\varepsilon>0$ with $(s-\varepsilon,s+\varepsilon)\subseteq A$.
  • The contradiction will be with (1): an element of $A$ bigger than $s$.
The storyWhat you write

Suppose the ceiling is a citizen.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $s=\sup A\in A$.

Citizens of an open kingdom have breathing space.

Since $A$ is open, there is $\varepsilon>0$ with $(s-\varepsilon,s+\varepsilon)\subseteq A$.

Step a little above the ceiling: still in the kingdom.

Then $s+\frac\varepsilon2\in A$, since $\left|s+\frac\varepsilon2-s\right|=\frac\varepsilon2<\varepsilon$.

A dragon above the ceiling: contradiction.

But $s+\frac\varepsilon2>s$, so $s$ is not an upper bound of $A$, contradicting (1). Hence $\sup A\notin A$. ∎

Moves used: assume the opposite use a definition with its ε

The proof in one piece

Suppose $s=\sup A\in A$. As $A$ is open, $(s-\varepsilon,s+\varepsilon)\subseteq A$ for some $\varepsilon>0$, so $s+\frac\varepsilon2\in A$ with $s+\frac\varepsilon2>s$, contradicting that $s$ is an upper bound. ∎

If $A$ isn't bounded above, $\sup A=+\infty\notin A$ anyway, so the statement holds for every non-empty open set.

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Assume $s=\sup A\in A$.
  2. Open: an interval $(s-\varepsilon,s+\varepsilon)\subseteq A$.
  3. The point $s+\frac\varepsilon2$ is in $A$ and above $s$: contradiction with (1).

In short: “an interval around the sup pokes above it”.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why $s+\frac\varepsilon2$ and not $s+\varepsilon$?

    The bubble is open: $s+\varepsilon$ itself is not in $(s-\varepsilon,s+\varepsilon)$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Does the claim hold for $A=(0,1)\cup\{2\}$? What breaks in the proof?

    Answer

    No: $\sup A=2\in A$. The set isn't open (no bubble around $2$), so step 2 fails.

One fact about integers is used below: no integer lies strictly between $n$ and $n+1$. So for integers $m,n$, $m<n+1$ implies $m\le n$ (script Lemma 2.18, p. 84, and Übung 2.26, p. 88).

6.1 The Archimedean principle (Satz 2.68, p. 111)

  1. Every non-empty subset of $\mathbb{Z}$ that is bounded above has a maximum.
  2. For every $x\in\mathbb{R}$ there is exactly one $n\in\mathbb{Z}$ with $n\le x<n+1$. It is written $\lfloor x\rfloor$, the floor of $x$ (Abrundungsfunktion).
  3. For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is an $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with $\tfrac1n<\varepsilon$ (Proof C below).

Proof of (i). Let $E\subseteq\mathbb{Z}$ be non-empty and bounded above. By Satz 2.59, $s=\sup E$ exists. By (2′) with $\varepsilon=1$, there is an $n_0\in E$ with $n_0>s-1$, that is, $s<n_0+1$. Now let $m\in E$ be arbitrary. By (1), $m\le s<n_0+1$, so $m<n_0+1$, and since $m,n_0$ are integers, $m\le n_0$. So $n_0\in E$ is at least every element of $E$: it's the maximum. ∎

Proof of (ii), existence. First let $x\ge0$. The set $E=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}: n\le x\}$ is non-empty ($0\in E$, because $x\ge0$) and bounded above by $x$. By (i) it has a maximum $n$. Then $n\le x$ (because $n\in E$), and $n+1\notin E$ (because $n+1>n=\max E$), which means $n+1>x$.

Now let $x<0$. Apply the first case to $-x>0$: there is $\ell\in\mathbb{Z}$ with $\ell\le -x<\ell+1$. If $-x=\ell$, then $n=-\ell$ satisfies $n=x<n+1$. Otherwise $\ell<-x<\ell+1$; multiplying by $-1$ gives $-\ell-1<x<-\ell$, so $n=-\ell-1$ works.

Proof of (ii), uniqueness. Suppose $n_1\le x<n_1+1$ and $n_2\le x<n_2+1$. Then $n_1\le x<n_2+1$, so $n_1<n_2+1$, so $n_1\le n_2$. By symmetry $n_2\le n_1$. Hence $n_1=n_2$. ∎

Proof C: $\frac1n$ gets below every $\varepsilon$ (Archimedes (iii))

The idea. “$\frac1n<\varepsilon$” is the same as “$n>\frac1\varepsilon$”. So we need a natural number beyond $\frac1\varepsilon$, and (ii) hands us the integer just after it.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • A “for all, exists” statement: “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” … “so $n\in\mathbb N$ and $\frac1n<\varepsilon$.”
  • $n$ must be a natural number, so check $n\ge1$ at the end.
The storyWhat you write

The Doubter names a tiny tolerance.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Then $\frac1\varepsilon>0$.

Find the integer step just past $\frac1\varepsilon$.

By (ii) there is $m\in\mathbb Z$ with $m\le\frac1\varepsilon<m+1$. Let $n=m+1$. Then $n>\frac1\varepsilon>0$, so $n\in\mathbb N$.

Flip both sides.

Multiplying $n>\frac1\varepsilon$ by the positive number $\frac\varepsilon n$ gives $\varepsilon>\frac1n$. ∎

Moves used: choose n by Archimedes

The proof in one piece

Let $\varepsilon>0$. By (ii) there is $m\in\mathbb Z$ with $m\le\frac1\varepsilon<m+1$. Then $n=m+1>\frac1\varepsilon>0$, so $n\in\mathbb N$ and $\frac1n<\varepsilon$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why is $n=m+1$ a natural number, and not just an integer?

    Answer

    $n>\frac1\varepsilon>0$, and a positive integer is at least $1$, so $n\in\mathbb N$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the last step with its reason: from $n>\frac1\varepsilon$ to $\frac1n<\varepsilon$.

    Answer

    Multiply both sides by the positive number $\frac\varepsilon n$. Multiplying by a positive number keeps the inequality (an order axiom), so $\varepsilon>\frac1n$.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Why completeness is needed. It feels obvious that $\mathbb{N}$ is unbounded. But there are ordered fields satisfying axioms 1–15 that contain “infinitely large” elements, bigger than every natural number. In such a field, (iii) fails. Completeness rules them out. Notice also: (ii) says exactly that $\mathbb{N}$ is not bounded above, since for every $x$ there's an integer $n+1>x$.

6.2 ℚ is dense in ℝ (Korollar 2.70, p. 112)

Statement. For all $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$ with $a<b$ there is an $r\in\mathbb{Q}$ with $a<r<b$.

Proof D: ℚ is dense in ℝ, a fraction in every gap (Korollar 2.70)

The idea. Lay down the grid of fractions with step $\frac1m$, finer than the gap $b-a$ (Archimedes). Walk along the grid to the first point right of $a$. Since the step is smaller than the gap, it can't jump over $b$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Start: “Let $a<b$.” End: “so $r=\frac nm\in\mathbb Q$ satisfies $a<r<b$.” So we must find integers $n$ and $m\ge1$.
  • Tools available: Archimedes (iii) gives a small $\frac1m$; Archimedes (ii) gives the integer $n$ just above a real number.
The storyWhat you write

Choose a grid finer than the gap.

Let $a<b$. Since $b-a>0$, Archimedes (iii) gives $m\in\mathbb N$ with $\frac1m<b-a$.

Find the first grid point to the right of $a$.

By Archimedes (ii) for $x=ma$ there is $n\in\mathbb Z$ with $n-1\le ma<n$. Dividing by $m>0$: $\frac{n-1}m\le a<\frac nm$.

One step from at most $a$ can't reach $b$.

$$\frac nm=\frac{n-1}m+\frac1m\le a+\frac1m<a+(b-a)=b.$$

The grid point sits in the gap.

So $a<\frac nm<b$, and $r=\frac nm\in\mathbb Q$. ∎

Moves used: choose n by Archimedes

The proof in one piece

Let $a<b$. Choose $m\in\mathbb N$ with $\frac1m<b-a$, and $n\in\mathbb Z$ with $n-1\le ma<n$. Then $a<\frac nm\le a+\frac1m<b$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Grid: $\frac1m<b-a$ (Archimedes).
  2. First grid point after $a$: $n-1\le ma<n$ (floor).
  3. Doesn't overshoot: $\frac nm\le a+\frac1m<b$.

In short: “grid finer than the gap, first point after $a$”.

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Where is $\frac1m<b-a$ used?

    Answer

    In the last inequality: $a+\frac1m<a+(b-a)=b$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Run the proof for $a=0.3$, $b=0.4$. Which $m$ and $n$ do you get?

    Answer

    $b-a=0.1$, so $m=11$ works ($\frac1{11}\approx0.091$). $ma=3.3$, so $n=4$, and $r=\frac4{11}\approx0.364$. ✓

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the proof from the exam box alone.

The irrationals are dense too. Given $a<b$, apply the statement to $a-\sqrt2<b-\sqrt2$: there's a rational $r$ with $a-\sqrt2<r<b-\sqrt2$. Then $a<r+\sqrt2<b$, and $r+\sqrt2$ is irrational (if it were rational, $\sqrt2=(r+\sqrt2)-r$ would be too).

So every interval $(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)$ contains both rationals and irrationals. That's why $\mathbb{Q}$ was neither open nor closed in lesson 5.

Dense in general: three equivalent versions. The script calls a set $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ dense if every non-empty open interval $I$ meets $A$, that is, $I\cap A\neq\emptyset$ (§2.7.2, Übung “Charakterisierung von Dichtheit”, p. 125). Einsiedler uses two other versions, and all three are equivalent:

  1. every non-empty open interval meets $A$;
  2. every non-empty open set $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ meets $A$;
  3. every $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$ is the limit of a sequence $(a_n)_n$ in $A$.

Proof. (1)⇒(2): $U$ is non-empty, so pick $x\in U$. Since $U$ is open, there is $\varepsilon>0$ with $(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq U$. By (1) this interval meets $A$, so $U$ does too. (2)⇒(1): an open interval is an open set.

(1)⇒(3): for each $n\in\mathbb{N}$, the interval $(x_0-\tfrac1n,x_0+\tfrac1n)$ meets $A$, so choose $a_n\in A$ in it. Then $|a_n-x_0|<\tfrac1n$, hence $a_n\to x_0$. (3)⇒(1): let $I$ be a non-empty open interval and $x_0\in I$. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ with $(x_0-\varepsilon,x_0+\varepsilon)\subseteq I$. By (3) there is a sequence $a_n\to x_0$ in $A$, so $|a_N-x_0|<\varepsilon$ for some $N$, and then $a_N\in I\cap A$. ∎

For $A=\mathbb{Q}$: every real number is a limit of rationals, for example of its decimal truncations. Sequences come properly in lesson 10; for now, (1) is the version you need.

6.3 Nested intervals (Intervallschachtelungsprinzip, Satz 2.77, p. 115)

Statement. Let $I_n=[a_n,b_n]$ be non-empty, closed, bounded intervals with $I_1\supseteq I_2\supseteq I_3\supseteq\cdots$, that is, $a_1\le a_2\le\dots\le b_2\le b_1$. Then

$$\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}I_n=\Big[\,\sup_n a_n,\ \inf_n b_n\,\Big]\neq\emptyset.$$

Proof E: nested intervals have a common point (Satz 2.77)

The idea. The left ends climb, the right ends fall, and no left end ever passes any right end. So $a=\sup a_n$ (the highest left end) exists and is still left of every right end. It lies in every interval.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • To use $\sup_n a_n$, show $\{a_n\}$ is bounded above. Candidates for bounds: the $b_m$.
  • $x\in I_n$ for all $n$ means $a_n\le x\le b_n$ for all $n$: an upper bound of the $a$'s and a lower bound of the $b$'s.
The storyWhat you write

No left end passes a right end, even from different intervals.

Let $k,m\in\mathbb N$ and $n=\max(k,m)$. By nesting, $a_k\le a_n\le b_n\le b_m$, so $a_k\le b_m$.

So the highest left end exists, and stays left of every right end.

So $\{a_k\}$ is non-empty and bounded above by every $b_m$; $a=\sup_ka_k$ exists, and by (2) $a\le b_m$ for all $m$.

Likewise the lowest right end.

So $a$ is a lower bound of $\{b_m\}$; $b=\inf_mb_m$ exists and $a\le b$.

The common points are exactly what lies between.

$x\in\bigcap_nI_n$ $\iff$ $a_n\le x\le b_n$ for all $n$ $\iff$ $a\le x\le b$, by (1) and (2) for $\sup$ and $\inf$. So $\bigcap_nI_n=[a,b]\neq\emptyset$. ∎

Moves used: sup ≤ an upper bound

The proof in one piece

For all $k,m$, with $n=\max(k,m)$: $a_k\le a_n\le b_n\le b_m$. So $a=\sup_ka_k$ exists and $a\le b_m$ for all $m$; hence $b=\inf_mb_m$ exists and $a\le b$. Finally $x\in\bigcap I_n$ iff $x$ is an upper bound of the $a_n$ and a lower bound of the $b_n$, iff $a\le x\le b$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why does the first line need $n=\max(k,m)$?

    Answer

    $a_k$ and $b_m$ come from different intervals. The interval $I_n$ lies inside both $I_k$ and $I_m$, so $a_k\le a_n$ and $b_n\le b_m$, and $a_n\le b_n$ connects them.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Give the reason for $a\le b$ in one line.

    Answer

    $a\le b_m$ for every $m$, so $a$ is a lower bound of $\{b_m\}$. The infimum $b$ is the greatest lower bound, so $a\le b$.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Both conditions are needed. Not closed: $\bigcap_n\left(0,\tfrac1n\right]=\emptyset$, since any $x>0$ is excluded once $\tfrac1n<x$ (Archimedes). Not bounded: $\bigcap_n[n,\infty)=\emptyset$, since any $x$ is excluded once $n>x$.

6.4 ℝ is uncountable (Korollar 2.81, p. 116)

The game of Alice and Bob. Bob claims he can list all numbers in $[0,1]$ as $x_1,x_2,x_3,\dots$ Alice doesn't believe him. Each time Bob names a number, Alice shrinks her closed interval so that it avoids that number. At the end, nested intervals hand her a number Bob never named.

Statement. There is no surjective map $\mathbb{N}\to[0,1]$. So $[0,1]$, and hence $\mathbb{R}$, is uncountable.

Proof F: ℝ is uncountable (Alice beats every list)

The idea. Each time Bob names $x_n$, Alice keeps a closed third of her current interval that avoids $x_n$. Nested intervals give a point in all of them, and it differs from every $x_n$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • “Not surjective”: “Let $n\mapsto x_n$ be any map $\mathbb N\to[0,1]$” … “so $x\in[0,1]$ with $x\neq x_n$ for all $n$.”
  • The tool to produce $x$: nested intervals (Proof E), so build $I_1\supseteq I_2\supseteq\dots$ closed, bounded, non-empty.
The storyWhat you write

Bob names his first number; Alice moves away from it.

Let $n\mapsto x_n$ be a map $\mathbb N\to[0,1]$. If $x_1\in[0,\frac12]$, let $I_1=[\frac23,1]$; otherwise $I_1=[0,\frac13]$. Then $x_1\notin I_1$.

Each new number: keep the third on the far side.

Given $I_n$: if $x_{n+1}\notin I_n$, let $I_{n+1}=I_n$. If $x_{n+1}$ is in the left half of $I_n$, let $I_{n+1}$ be its right third; otherwise its left third. Then $I_{n+1}\subseteq I_n$ and $x_{n+1}\notin I_{n+1}$.

Completeness hands Alice a point in every interval.

By Proof E there is $x\in\bigcap_nI_n\subseteq[0,1]$.

Bob never named it.

For each $n$: $x\in I_n$ and $x_n\notin I_n$, so $x\neq x_n$. Hence the map is not surjective. ∎

Moves used: beat every entry of a list

The proof in one piece

Given $n\mapsto x_n$, choose closed intervals $I_1\supseteq I_2\supseteq\dots$ in $[0,1]$ with $x_n\notin I_n$ (keep the third of $I_{n-1}$ on the far side of $x_n$). By nested intervals some $x$ lies in all $I_n$, so $x\neq x_n$ for every $n$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why thirds, and not the two closed halves?

    Answer

    The closed halves $[l,\text{mid}]$ and $[\text{mid},r]$ share the midpoint. If Bob names the midpoint, neither half avoids it. The far third always leaves a gap between it and the named number.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the last line with its reason: why is $x\neq x_n$ for every $n$?

    Answer

    For each $n$, $x\in I_n$ (it lies in all the intervals) and $x_n\notin I_n$ (by construction), so $x\neq x_n$. Hence the map $n\mapsto x_n$ is not surjective.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Uncountability of $\mathbb{R}$ then follows from script Lemma 1.80 (p. 51): a subset of a countable set is countable, and $[0,1]\subseteq\mathbb{R}$.

This is a second proof, besides Cantor's diagonal argument from lesson 3. Here completeness does the work, in the form of nested intervals.

7. Accumulation points (Häufungspunkte)

Some places on the line are crowded: however closely you look around them, you always find other members of the set nearby. Those crowded places are the accumulation points. A member standing alone, with nobody else nearby, is isolated.

Definition 2.73 (p. 113). Let $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ and $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$. Then $x_0$ is an accumulation point (Häufungspunkt) of $A$ if

$$\forall\,\varepsilon>0\ \ \exists\,a\in A:\ \ 0<|a-x_0|<\varepsilon.$$

Read aloud: “every $\varepsilon$-neighbourhood of $x_0$ contains a point of $A$ other than $x_0$ itself.” The part $0<|a-x_0|$ means exactly $a\neq x_0$. The point $x_0$ may or may not be in $A$, as with border points.

Compare with border points (lesson 5). They're different notions:

  • Border point: every neighbourhood meets $A$ and the complement. Accumulation point: every neighbourhood contains points of $A$ other than $x_0$.
  • For $A=(0,1)$, the point $\tfrac12$ is an accumulation point (it's surrounded by points of $A$), but not a border point.
  • For $A=\{0\}\cup(1,2)$, the point $0$ is a border point but not an accumulation point. It's an isolated point of $A$: some neighbourhood contains no other point of $A$.

Calibration:

  • $(0,1)$: the accumulation points are all of $[0,1]$.
  • $\left\{\tfrac1n:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}$: only $0$. Each $\tfrac1n$ itself is isolated.
  • A finite set has no accumulation points (Übung 2.74): choose $\varepsilon$ smaller than the distance from $x_0$ to every other element.
  • $\mathbb{Z}$ has none either: neighbours are $1$ apart, so $\varepsilon=\tfrac12$ works around any point.
  • $\mathbb{Q}$: every real number, because $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense.

Proof G: a bounded infinite set has an accumulation point (Satz 2.75, p. 113)

Claim. Every bounded infinite set $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ has at least one accumulation point. (Infinitely many points squeezed into a bounded space must crowd together somewhere.) This is another consequence of completeness, and Einsiedler asked for it in FS22 as a “state and prove” question.

The idea. Walk along the line from left to right and count the points of $A$ behind you. At the left wall there are finitely many; at the right wall, all of $A$, infinitely many. The place where “finitely many” switches to “infinitely many” is a supremum, and every window around it must contain infinitely many points.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • To show: some $x_0$ with $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists a\in A:\ 0<|a-x_0|<\varepsilon$. A number has to be produced, and the only tool so far is the supremum. First line: “Let $A\subseteq[m,M]$ be infinite, and let $X=\dots$”. Last line: “so $x_0$ is an accumulation point of $A$.”
  • The gap: for an arbitrary $\varepsilon>0$, show the window $(x_0-\varepsilon,x_0+\varepsilon]$ contains infinitely many points of $A$: use (2′) on the left of $x_0$ and (1) on the right.
The storyWhat you write

Mark every spot that has only finitely many dragons behind it.

Let $A\subseteq[m,M]$ be infinite, and let $X=\{\,x\in\mathbb{R}:\ A\cap(-\infty,x]\text{ is finite}\,\}$.

The left wall is such a spot; the right wall is not. So the switching place exists.

$m\in X$, because $A\cap(-\infty,m]\subseteq\{m\}$. If $x\ge M$, then $A\cap(-\infty,x]=A$ is infinite, so $x\notin X$; hence $M$ is an upper bound of $X$. So $x_0=\sup X$ exists (Proof A). Let $\varepsilon>0$.

A little before the switch: still finitely many behind you.

By (2′) there is $x\in X$ with $x>x_0-\varepsilon$. Then $A\cap(-\infty,x_0-\varepsilon]\subseteq A\cap(-\infty,x]$, which is finite.

A little after the switch: infinitely many.

$x_0+\varepsilon>x_0=\sup X$, so by (1) $x_0+\varepsilon\notin X$. That is, $A\cap(-\infty,x_0+\varepsilon]$ is infinite.

So infinitely many crowd into the window around the switch.

Removing the finite set from the infinite one, $A\cap(x_0-\varepsilon,x_0+\varepsilon]$ is infinite. Removing also the (at most two) points $x_0$ and $x_0+\varepsilon$, there is still an $a\in A$ with $0<|a-x_0|<\varepsilon$. Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $x_0$ is an accumulation point of $A$. ∎

Moves used: the supremum trick approach a supremum

The proof in one piece

Let $A\subseteq[m,M]$ be infinite and $X=\{x\in\mathbb{R}: A\cap(-\infty,x]\text{ is finite}\}$. Then $m\in X$, and every $x\ge M$ is not in $X$, so $x_0=\sup X$ exists. Let $\varepsilon>0$. By (2′) some $x\in X$ satisfies $x>x_0-\varepsilon$, so $A\cap(-\infty,x_0-\varepsilon]$ is finite. By (1), $x_0+\varepsilon\notin X$, so $A\cap(-\infty,x_0+\varepsilon]$ is infinite. Hence $A\cap(x_0-\varepsilon,x_0+\varepsilon]$ is infinite and contains some $a\neq x_0$ with $|a-x_0|<\varepsilon$. So $x_0$ is an accumulation point. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. The set: $X=\{x: A\cap(-\infty,x]\text{ finite}\}$, with $m\in X$ and upper bound $M$. Put $x_0=\sup X$.
  2. Left of $x_0$: (2′) gives $x\in X$ above $x_0-\varepsilon$, so only finitely many points of $A$ are $\le x_0-\varepsilon$.
  3. Right of $x_0$: (1) gives $x_0+\varepsilon\notin X$, so infinitely many points of $A$ are $\le x_0+\varepsilon$.
  4. Subtract: infinitely many points in $(x_0-\varepsilon,x_0+\varepsilon]$; pick one other than $x_0$ and $x_0+\varepsilon$.

In short: “count what's behind you, take the sup of the switch, the window is crowded”.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why is $m\in X$?

    Answer

    $A\subseteq[m,M]$, so every point of $A$ that is $\le m$ equals $m$. Hence $A\cap(-\infty,m]\subseteq\{m\}$ has at most one element, which is finite.

  2. Rung 2 (3 min). Write steps 2 and 3 of the exam box as full sentences, with the reasons.

    Answer

    “By (2′) there is $x\in X$ with $x>x_0-\varepsilon$. Then $A\cap(-\infty,x_0-\varepsilon]\subseteq A\cap(-\infty,x]$, which is finite. Since $x_0+\varepsilon>\sup X$, condition (1) gives $x_0+\varepsilon\notin X$, so $A\cap(-\infty,x_0+\varepsilon]$ is infinite.”

  3. Rung 3 (5 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

The sequence version of this result is Bolzano–Weierstrass, in lesson 12.

Equivalent form (Übung 2.76): $x_0$ is an accumulation point exactly when every $\varepsilon$-neighbourhood of $x_0$ contains infinitely many points of $A$. (The idea: if some neighbourhood had only finitely many, shrink $\varepsilon$ below the smallest of their distances to $x_0$, and none would remain.)

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

Worked example 1: $\sup(0,1)=1$, and there is no maximum

  1. Guess the supremum and prove (1).

    Answer

    $s_0=1$. Every $x\in(0,1)$ satisfies $x<1$, so $1$ is an upper bound.

  2. For (2′): given $\varepsilon>0$, try $x=1-\frac\varepsilon2$. When does that fail?

    Answer

    When $\varepsilon$ is large: for $\varepsilon=5$, $1-\frac\varepsilon2=-1.5\notin(0,1)$.

  3. Repair it, and check all three requirements.

    Answer

    Take $x=\max\left(1-\frac\varepsilon2,\frac12\right)$. Then $x\ge1-\frac\varepsilon2>1-\varepsilon$; $x<1$ since both candidates are $<1$; and $x\ge\frac12>0$. So $x\in(0,1)$ and $x>1-\varepsilon$. It's the same trick as the $\min$ in lesson 5. ∎

  4. Is there a maximum?

    Answer

    No: $1\notin(0,1)$, and the maximum exists exactly when the supremum belongs to the set.

Worked example 2: $X=\left\{1-\tfrac1n : n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}$

The elements are $0,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac23,\ \tfrac34,\dots$ (the dragons from the story).

  1. Prove that $1$ is an upper bound.

    Answer

    For every $n$, $\frac1n>0$, so $1-\frac1n<1$.

  2. Prove (2′) for $s_0=1$. Which tool gives the element?

    Answer

    Let $\varepsilon>0$. By Archimedes (Proof C) there is $n$ with $\frac1n<\varepsilon$; then $1-\frac1n>1-\varepsilon$ and $1-\frac1n\in X$. So $\sup X=1$, not attained (that would need $\frac1n=0$).

  3. Find $\inf X$. Is it a minimum?

    Answer

    $0=1-\frac11\in X$, and $1-\frac1n\ge0$ since $\frac1n\le1$. So $0=\min X=\inf X$.

  4. What are the accumulation points?

    Answer

    Only $1$: the element from (2′) satisfies $0<\left|1-\frac1n-1\right|=\frac1n<\varepsilon$. Every other point is isolated or has a neighbourhood with no element at all.

Worked example 3: $\sup(A+B)=\sup A+\sup B$ (Proposition 2.63, p. 107)

Let $A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be non-empty and bounded above, $A+B=\{a+b: a\in A,\ b\in B\}$, $s_A=\sup A$, $s_B=\sup B$.

  1. Show (1): $s_A+s_B$ is an upper bound of $A+B$.

    Answer

    An element of $A+B$ is $a+b$ with $a\le s_A$ and $b\le s_B$ (by (1) for $A$ and $B$). Adding (axiom 14): $a+b\le s_A+s_B$. In particular $\sup(A+B)$ exists.

  2. For (2′), given $\varepsilon>0$: how much room do you give $A$ and $B$ each?

    Answer

    Adding: $a+b>s_A+s_B-\varepsilon$ and $a+b\in A+B$. So $\sup(A+B)=s_A+s_B$. ∎

Exercise 1 (compute)

Frame: the recipe from section 5: guess, prove (1) for an arbitrary element, prove (2′) for an arbitrary $\varepsilon$ with an explicit element, then check membership. Moves: approach a supremum.

  • (1): every $x\in[0,2)$ satisfies $x<2$, so $2$ is an upper bound.
  • (2′): let $\varepsilon>0$ and take $x=\max\left(2-\tfrac\varepsilon2,\ 0\right)$. Then $x\ge 2-\tfrac\varepsilon2>2-\varepsilon$, and $0\le x<2$, so $x\in[0,2)$.
  • $2\notin[0,2)$, so there's no maximum. $0\in[0,2)$ and $0\le x$ for all $x$ in the set, so $0$ is the minimum.

(b) $\left\{\tfrac1n\right\}=\left\{1,\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\dots\right\}$: $\sup=\max=1$. $\inf=0$, no min.

  • $1=\tfrac11$ is in the set, and $\tfrac1n\le1$ for all $n\ge1$. So $1$ is the maximum, hence the supremum.
  • Infimum, (1): $\tfrac1n>0$ for every $n$, so $0$ is a lower bound.
  • Infimum, (2′): let $\varepsilon>0$. By the Archimedean principle there is $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with $\tfrac1n<\varepsilon=0+\varepsilon$. So $0+\varepsilon$ is not a lower bound.
  • $0$ is not in the set ($\tfrac1n\neq0$), so there's no minimum.

(c) $\left\{(-1)^n+\tfrac1n\right\}$: write out the elements. Odd $n$: $-1+1=0$, $-1+\tfrac13=-\tfrac23$, $-1+\tfrac15=-\tfrac45$, …, climbing down towards $-1$. Even $n$: $$1+\tfrac12=\tfrac32,\ 1+\tfrac14=\tfrac54,\ 1+\tfrac16=\tfrac76,\dots,$$falling towards $1$.

  • $\sup=\max=\tfrac32$ (attained at $n=2$). Every even-$n$ element is $1+\tfrac1n\le1+\tfrac12$, and every odd-$n$ element is $-1+\tfrac1n\le0$.
  • $\inf=-1$, no min. (1): every element is $\ge -1+\tfrac1n>-1$. (2′): for $\varepsilon>0$ take an odd $n$ with $\tfrac1n<\varepsilon$ (Archimedes gives some $m$ with $\tfrac1m<\varepsilon$, and $n=2m+1$ works too, since $\tfrac1{2m+1}<\tfrac1m$). Then $-1+\tfrac1n<-1+\varepsilon$. And $-1$ is never attained, since $\tfrac1n\neq0$.

(d) $\{x\in\mathbb{Q}: x^2<2\}$: this is $\mathbb{Q}\cap(-\sqrt2,\sqrt2)$. $\sup=\sqrt2$ and $\inf=-\sqrt2$. Neither is attained, because $\pm\sqrt2$ is irrational, so there's no max or min. (2′) works by density of $\mathbb{Q}$: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a rational number between $\sqrt2-\varepsilon$ and $\sqrt2$. This is the classic example of why we need $\mathbb{R}$: inside $\mathbb{Q}$, this set has no least upper bound at all.

No max or min, since there are no elements.

Exercise 2 (Felder HS22, 3.MC1)

Open the original: HS22 exam

The supremum of a non-empty bounded subset $A$ of $\mathbb{R}$ is

(A) the smallest number $s\in\mathbb{R}$ such that $x\le s$ for all $x\in A$.
(B) the smallest number $s\in A$ such that $x\le s$ for all $x\in A$.
(C) the smallest number $s\in A$ such that there is an $x\in A$ with $x\le s$.
(D) the smallest number $s\in\mathbb{R}$ such that there is an $x\in A$ with $x\le s$.

Solution

Answer A. “Smallest $s$ with $x\le s$ for all $x\in A$” is exactly “least upper bound”: conditions (1) and (2).

  • (B) demands $s\in A$. That's the maximum, which may not exist. For $A=(0,1)$ there is no such $s$.
  • (C) and (D) say “there is an $x$ with $x\le s$”. That only asks $s$ to beat one element, not all. For (D) the set of such $s$ is $[\inf A,\infty)$ or $(\inf A,\infty)$, which has nothing to do with the supremum. For (C), the smallest such $s\in A$ would be the minimum.

Let $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$, $A\neq\emptyset$, and $-A=\{-x: x\in A\}$. Always true?

(a) $A$ is unbounded if and only if $\max(\sup A,\ \sup(-A))=\infty$.
(b) If $A$ is finite, then $\max A=\sup A$.
(c) If $\min A$ exists, then $\min A=-\max(-A)$.
(d) If $A$ is open, then $\sup A\notin A$.

Solution

All four are true: T, T, T, T. (Yes, that happens. The instructions say 0–4 statements can be true.)

  • (a) True. $\sup(-A)=-\inf A$, by the shortcut in section 4. So $\sup(-A)=\infty$ exactly when $\inf A=-\infty$, that is, when $A$ isn't bounded below. Hence $\max(\sup A,\sup(-A))=\infty$ exactly when $A$ isn't bounded above or isn't bounded below, which is exactly “$A$ is unbounded”.
  • (b) True. A non-empty finite set has a maximum, and whenever the maximum exists, it equals the supremum (section 4).
  • (c) True. Let $m=\min A$. Then $m\in A$ and $m\le x$ for all $x\in A$. Multiplying by $-1$ flips the inequality: $-m\ge -x$ for all $x\in A$, so $-m\ge y$ for every $y\in-A$. And $-m\in -A$. So $-m=\max(-A)$, which means $m=-\max(-A)$.
  • (d) True. This is Proof B.

Exercise 4 (Einsiedler mock HS21, #8, true/false)

Let $A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be non-empty and bounded above. True in general?

(a) If $A\subseteq B$, then $\sup A\le\sup B$.
(b) If $A\subseteq B$ and $A\neq B$, then $\sup A<\sup B$.
(c) $\sup(A+B)=\sup A+\sup B$.
(d) $\sup(AB)=\sup A\cdot\sup B$, where $AB=\{ab: a\in A,\ b\in B\}$.

Solution

Answer: T, F, T, F.

  • (a) True. See Exercise 6 for the proof.
  • (b) False. Counterexample: $A=(0,1)$ and $B=(0,1]$. Then $A\subseteq B$, $A\neq B$, but $\sup A=1=\sup B$. (Adding elements below the ceiling doesn't raise it.)
  • (c) True. This is Worked example 3: give $A$ and $B$ room $\frac\varepsilon2$ each in (2′).
  • (d) False. Negative numbers break it. Counterexample: $A=B=\{-2,-1\}$. Then $AB=\{4,2,1\}$, so $\sup(AB)=4$, while $\sup A\cdot\sup B=(-1)(-1)=1$. The rule does hold when $A,B$ consist of positive numbers.

Exercise 5 (accumulation points)

Find all accumulation points, and say which of them belong to the set.

(a) $(0,1)\cup\{2\}$   (b) $\left\{\tfrac1n : n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\cup\{5\}$   (c) $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$   (d) $\left\{(-1)^n+\tfrac1n : n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}$

Solution
  • (a) $(0,1)\cup\{2\}$: the accumulation points are $[0,1]$. For $x_0\in[0,1]$ and $\varepsilon>0$, the interval $(x_0-\varepsilon,x_0+\varepsilon)$ contains infinitely many points of $(0,1)$, so in particular one different from $x_0$. The point $2$ is not an accumulation point: with $\varepsilon=\tfrac12$, the interval $(1.5,2.5)$ contains no point of the set except $2$ itself. So $2$ is an isolated point. Of the accumulation points, those in $(0,1)$ belong to the set, and $0$, $1$ don't.
  • (b) $\left\{\tfrac1n\right\}\cup\{5\}$: only $0$, which is not in the set. For every $\varepsilon>0$ Archimedes gives $\tfrac1n$ with $0<|\tfrac1n-0|<\varepsilon$. Every $\tfrac1n$ is isolated: take $\varepsilon$ smaller than the gap to its neighbours, $\varepsilon=\tfrac1n-\tfrac1{n+1}$. The point $5$ is isolated too.
  • (c) $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$: all of $[0,1]$. For $x_0\in[0,1]$ and $\varepsilon>0$, the interval $(x_0-\varepsilon,x_0+\varepsilon)\cap[0,1]$ contains a non-trivial interval, and by density of $\mathbb{Q}$ a rational number other than $x_0$ inside it. Points outside $[0,1]$ have a neighbourhood missing $[0,1]$. Rational points of $[0,1]$ belong to the set, and irrational ones don't.
  • (d) $\left\{(-1)^n+\tfrac1n\right\}$: exactly $-1$ and $1$, and neither belongs to the set. Near $1$: for $\varepsilon>0$ pick an even $n$ with $\tfrac1n<\varepsilon$; then $0<|1+\tfrac1n-1|=\tfrac1n<\varepsilon$. Near $-1$: the same with odd $n$. Any other point is at positive distance from both $\pm1$, and only finitely many elements are closer to it than half that distance, so it is not an accumulation point (by the equivalent form at the end of section 7: an accumulation point has infinitely many points nearby). This is a preview of lesson 11: the sequence $(-1)^n+\tfrac1n$ has the two “accumulation values” $\pm1$.

Exercise 6 (proof)

Prove Exercise 4(a) properly: if $A\subseteq B$, $A\neq\emptyset$ and $B$ is bounded above, then $A$ is bounded above and $\sup A\le\sup B$. Hint: use (1) for $B$, then (2) for $A$.

Solution

Frame: two claims. For “bounded above”, name an upper bound of $A$ (the obvious candidate is $\sup B$). For $\sup A\le\sup B$, use (2) for $A$: it needs exactly one upper bound of $A$ in hand. Moves: sup ≤ an upper bound.

  • $A$ is bounded above: let $s_B=\sup B$, which exists because $B$ is bounded above and non-empty (it contains $A\neq\emptyset$). By (1) for $B$, $b\le s_B$ for all $b\in B$. Every $a\in A$ is also in $B$ (since $A\subseteq B$), so $a\le s_B$. Hence $s_B$ is an upper bound of $A$, and $A$ is bounded above. Since $A\neq\emptyset$, $\sup A$ exists.
  • $\sup A\le\sup B$: by (2) for $A$, $\sup A$ is at most every upper bound of $A$. We just showed that $s_B$ is an upper bound of $A$. So $\sup A\le s_B=\sup B$. ∎

Notice how short this is when you use (2) and not (2′). Use whichever form fits: (2) when you already have an upper bound in hand, (2′) when you need to construct elements.

Exercise 7 (consequences of completeness)

(a) Use Archimedes to prove $\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(0,\tfrac1n\right)=\emptyset$. Why doesn't this contradict nested intervals?
(b) Let $I_n=\left[-\tfrac1n,\tfrac1n\right]$. Compute $\bigcap_n I_n$ using the formula from 6.3.
(c) Find a rational number between $\sqrt2$ and $1.42$ by following the proof in 6.2 (use $\sqrt2\approx1.41421$).

Solution

(a) Frame: to show a set is empty, assume it has an element and reach a contradiction. Moves: assume the opposite, choose $n$ by Archimedes.

Suppose $x\in\bigcap_n(0,\tfrac1n)$. Then $x\in(0,1)$, so $x>0$. By Archimedes (iii) with $\varepsilon=x$ there is $n$ with $\tfrac1n<x$, so $x\notin(0,\tfrac1n)$. Contradiction, so the intersection is empty. This doesn't contradict 6.3 because the intervals $(0,\tfrac1n)$ are not closed. The “missing” common point would be $0$, which none of them contains.

(b) $a_n=-\tfrac1n$ and $b_n=\tfrac1n$. Then $\sup_n a_n=0$: every $-\tfrac1n<0$, and for $\varepsilon>0$ Archimedes gives $-\tfrac1n>-\varepsilon$. In the same way $\inf_n b_n=0$. So $\bigcap_n I_n=[0,0]=\{0\}$.

(c) $a=\sqrt2\approx1.41421$ and $b=1.42$, so $b-a\approx0.00579$. We need $\tfrac1m<0.00579$, that is $m>172.7$; take $m=200$. Then $ma\approx282.84$, so the integer $n$ with $n-1\le ma<n$ is $n=283$. The proof gives $r=\tfrac{283}{200}=1.415$. Check: $1.41421<1.415<1.42$. ✓ (Any other rational number in between, such as $1.417$, is also a correct answer.)

Bonus: $\sqrt2$ exists (Wichtige Übung 2.11, p. 81)

Let $X=\{x\in\mathbb{R}: x\ge0,\ x^2<2\}$ and $s=\sup X$. Show that $s^2=2$. Hint: show that $s^2<2$ and $s^2>2$ both lead to contradictions. If $s^2<2$, find a small $h>0$ with $(s+h)^2<2$. If $s^2>2$, find a small $h>0$ such that $s-h$ is still an upper bound.

Solution

Frame: to show $s^2=2$, rule out $s^2<2$ and $s^2>2$, each by contradiction: the first with (1) (an element above $s$), the second with (2) (an upper bound below $s$). Moves: assume the opposite.

Setup. $X$ is non-empty ($1\in X$, since $1^2=1<2$) and bounded above by $2$: if $x\ge2$ then $x^2\ge4>2$, so every $x\in X$ satisfies $x<2$. So $s=\sup X$ exists, and $s\ge1$ because $1\in X$.

Case $s^2<2$ is impossible. Let $h=\min\left(1,\ \dfrac{2-s^2}{2s+2}\right)>0$. Since $h\le1$, we have $h^2\le h$, so

$$\begin{aligned}(s+h)^2&=s^2+2sh+h^2\\&\le s^2+h(2s+1)\\&\le s^2+\frac{(2-s^2)(2s+1)}{2s+2}\\&<s^2+(2-s^2)\\&=2\end{aligned}.$$

The last step uses $\tfrac{2s+1}{2s+2}<1$ and $2-s^2>0$. So $s+h\in X$, but $s+h>s$, contradicting (1) for $s$.

Case $s^2>2$ is impossible. Let $h=\dfrac{s^2-2}{2s}>0$. Then $h<s$ (because $s^2-2<2s^2$), so $s-h>0$, and

$$(s-h)^2=s^2-2sh+h^2>s^2-2sh=s^2-(s^2-2)=2.$$

Now take any $x\in X$. Then $x^2<2<(s-h)^2$, and both $x$ and $s-h$ are $\ge0$. For non-negative numbers, $x^2<y^2$ implies $x<y$ (if $x\ge y\ge0$, then $x^2\ge y^2$). So $x<s-h$. Hence $s-h$ is an upper bound of $X$ smaller than $s$, contradicting (2) for $s$.

Since $s^2<2$ and $s^2>2$ are both impossible, trichotomy (axiom 10) gives $s^2=2$. ∎ So $\sqrt2$ exists in $\mathbb{R}$, and this is exactly where completeness was needed. In $\mathbb{Q}$, the same set has no supremum.

10. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Area is a supremum. How do you define the area under a curve? Fill the region with rectangles that fit inside it, and take the supremum of all their total areas. That's the Riemann integral (lesson 9), and the script hints at it in Figur 1.2. Archimedes did exactly this for the parabola around 250 BC, without having the word.

Good fractions. Density says every real number can be approximated by fractions. But some approximations are astonishingly efficient. Übung 2.72 (Dirichlet) shows that for every irrational $a$ there are infinitely many fractions $\tfrac pq$ with $\left|a-\tfrac pq\right|<\tfrac1{q^2}$. The Chinese astronomer Zu Chongzhi found $\pi\approx\tfrac{355}{113}$ around 480 AD. It's correct to six decimal places, from a denominator of only $113$.

Why the supremum is the axiom in disguise. The script remarks (p. 106) that “every non-empty bounded-above set has a supremum” is equivalent to the completeness axiom. Many books take it as the axiom. In $\mathbb{Q}$ it fails: Exercise 1(d) is a bounded set of rationals with no rational least upper bound. That one missing ceiling is the hole at $\sqrt2$.
