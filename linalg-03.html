Proof methods: direct, contrapositive, contradiction, induction, pigeonhole

Halbeisen's §1, second half (notes pp. 7–10). What a proof is, three rules of inference, and five model proofs: no largest prime, $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$, $|\mathcal P(M)|=2^n$, Cantor's theorem, and inverses in $\mathbb Z_p$ by the pigeonhole principle.

1. What a proof is: rules of inference

Definition. A mathematical proof is a finite sequence of statements, each of which is an axiom or follows from earlier statements of the sequence.

Modus ponens: from $A$ and $A\to B$ conclude $B$. (This rule alone suffices to prove everything.)
Contrapositive rule: from $A$ and $\neg B\to\neg A$ conclude $B$ (since $A\to B\iff\neg B\to\neg A$, lesson 2).
Rule of contradiction: from $\neg A\to(C\wedge\neg C)$ conclude $A$, for any statement $C$.
Further rules: from $A\to B$ and $B\to C$ conclude $A\to C$; from $A\to B$ and $A\to C$ conclude $A\to(B\wedge C)$; from $A\to C$ and $B\to C$ conclude $(A\vee B)\to C$.

2. A direct proof: there is no largest prime

Let $P\subseteq\mathbb N$ be the set of primes. Claim $A$: $\neg\exists p\in P\ \forall q\in P\ (q\le p)$.

Proof. Let $p\in P$ be any prime and $r:=q_0\cdot q_1\cdots q_n$ the product of all primes $q_i\le p$. Then $r+1$ is not divisible by any $q_i$ (it leaves remainder $1$). So the prime factorisation of $r+1$ contains a prime $p'>p$. As $p$ was arbitrary, we showed $$\forall p\in P\ \exists p'\in P\ (p'>p)\iff\neg\exists p\in P\ \neg\exists p'\in P\ (p'>p)\iff\neg\exists p\in P\ \forall q\in P\ (q\le p),$$ which is $A$ (renaming $p'$ to $q$). ∎

The quantifier gymnastics at the end is Halbeisen's style: he shows that what you proved is the claim, using the negation rules from lesson 2.

3. Contrapositive: $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$

Let $B$: “$\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$” and $A$: “$(\sqrt2)^2=2$”. $A$ is true by definition of $\sqrt2$, so by modus ponens it suffices to show $A\to B$. Instead we show the contrapositive $\neg B\to\neg A$, i.e. $\sqrt2\in\mathbb Q\to(\sqrt2)^2\ne2$:

If $\sqrt2\in\mathbb Q$, write $\sqrt2=\frac mn$ with $m,n\ge1$ and $\gcd(m,n)=1$. Then $\gcd(m^2,n^2)=1$, so $\frac{m^2}{n^2}$ is in lowest terms; it equals the integer $2$ only if $n^2=1$, i.e. $m^2=2$, which no integer satisfies. So $\frac{m^2}{n^2}\ne2$, i.e. $(\sqrt2)^2\ne2$. By the contrapositive rule, $B$ holds. ∎

Usually one doesn't mention modus ponens and just shows $A\to B$.

4. Induction: the power set of an $n$-element set

Induction axiom. If $A(n)$ is a statement about $n\in\mathbb N$ and $A(0)\wedge\forall n\,\big(A(n)\to A(n+1)\big)$ holds, then $\forall n\,A(n)$. (Base case $A(0)$; induction step $A(n)\to A(n+1)$. Note: Halbeisen starts at $0$.)

Claim. If $|M|=n$, then $|\mathcal P(M)|=2^n$. Let $A(n)$: “$|M|=n\to|\mathcal P(M)|=2^n$”.

Base case. $|M|=0$ means $M=\emptyset$, and $\mathcal P(\emptyset)=\{\emptyset\}$ has $1=2^0$ element.

Step. Assume $A(n)$. A set with $n+1$ elements has the form $\tilde M=M\cup\{a\}$ with $|M|=n$, $a\notin M$. Every subset of $\tilde M$ either avoids $a$ or contains it: $$\mathcal P(\tilde M)=\mathcal P(M)\cup\{x\cup\{a\}:x\in\mathcal P(M)\},$$ two disjoint parts with $|\mathcal P(M)|$ elements each. So $|\mathcal P(\tilde M)|=2\cdot2^n=2^{n+1}$. By the induction axiom, $\forall n\,A(n)$. ∎

5. Contradiction: Cantor's theorem

Theorem (Cantor). For every set $M$ (finite or infinite) and every function $g:M\to\mathcal P(M)$, $g$ is not surjective: $\exists x\in\mathcal P(M)\ \forall a\in M\ (g(a)\ne x)$.

Proof. Define $Y:=\{a\in M: a\notin g(a)\}$. Then $Y\subseteq M$, so $Y\in\mathcal P(M)$. Let $A$ be the statement $\neg\exists a\in M\,(g(a)=Y)$. Suppose $\neg A$: there is $a_0\in M$ with $g(a_0)=Y$. Then $$a_0\in Y\iff a_0\notin g(a_0)\iff a_0\notin Y.$$ So $\neg A\to(a_0\in Y\wedge a_0\notin Y)$, and by the rule of contradiction $A$ holds: no $a$ is mapped to $Y$, so $g$ is not surjective. ∎

How to reproduce Cantor under exam pressure. 1. Take any $g:M\to\mathcal P(M)$. 2. Define the “diagonal” set $Y=\{a\in M:a\notin g(a)\}$. 3. Suppose $g(a_0)=Y$. 4. Ask “is $a_0\in Y$?” and unfold the definition: both answers contradict. In short: collect everyone not in their own image, nobody can be mapped there.

6. Countable and uncountable

Definition. $M$ is countable if it is finite or there is a bijection $f:\mathbb N\to M$; otherwise uncountable.

Countably infinite: $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q$, the primes, $2\mathbb Z$. One can show there is a bijection between $\mathbb R$ and $\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$; by Cantor there is no surjection $\mathbb N\to\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$, so $\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$ and hence $\mathbb R$ are uncountable.

7. Pigeonhole: inverses in $\mathbb Z_p$

Pigeonhole principle. If $m+1$ objects are put into $m$ drawers, some drawer contains at least two objects.

Theorem. Let $p\ge3$ be prime and $1\le a\le p-1$. Then there is $k$ with $0\le k\le p-1$ such that $\bar a\cdot\bar a^k=\bar1$ in $\mathbb Z_p$. (So every nonzero $\bar a$ has a multiplicative inverse, namely $\bar a^k$.)

Proof. Consider the $p$ objects $a^0,a^1,\dots,a^{p-1}$ and put $a^n$ into the drawer $\overline{a^n}\in\{\bar1,\dots,\overline{p-1}\}$: since $p$ is prime and $p\nmid a$, $\overline{a^n}\ne\bar0$. There are only $p-1$ drawers, so there are $0\le s<t\le p-1$ with $\overline{a^s}=\overline{a^t}$. Write $t=s+(k+1)$ with $k\ge0$. Then $$\overline{a^s}\cdot\big(\overline{a^{k+1}}-\bar1\big)=\overline{a^t}-\overline{a^s}=\bar0.$$ Since $\overline{a^s}\ne\bar0$ and $\mathbb Z_p$ has no zero divisors ($p$ prime), $\overline{a^{k+1}}=\bar1$, i.e. $\bar a\cdot\bar a^k=\bar1$. ∎

Why it's cool. This is the reason $\mathbb F_p=\mathbb Z_p$ is a field (lesson 5): a counting argument produces inverses without ever computing one.

Exercises

Exercise 1

Prove by contrapositive: for $n\in\mathbb Z$, if $n^2$ is even then $n$ is even.

Solution

Contrapositive: if $n$ is odd then $n^2$ is odd. Let $n=2k+1$ with $k\in\mathbb Z$. Then $n^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1$, which is odd. Since $A\to B\iff\neg B\to\neg A$, the claim follows. ∎

Exercise 2

Prove by induction: $\sum_{i=0}^{n}2^i=2^{n+1}-1$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$.

Solution

Base $n=0$: $2^0=1=2^1-1$. Step: assume $\sum_{i=0}^n2^i=2^{n+1}-1$. Then $\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}2^i=(2^{n+1}-1)+2^{n+1}=2^{n+2}-1$. By the induction axiom the formula holds for all $n$. ∎

Exercise 3

In $\mathbb Z_7$, use the idea of the pigeonhole proof to find the inverse of $\bar3$: compute powers of $\bar3$ until you hit $\bar1$.

Solution

$\bar3^1=\bar3$, $\bar3^2=\bar2$, $\bar3^3=\bar6$, $\bar3^4=\bar4$, $\bar3^5=\bar5$, $\bar3^6=\bar1$. So $k+1=6$, $k=5$: $\bar3\cdot\bar3^5=\bar1$, and $\bar3^{-1}=\bar3^5=\bar5$. Check: $3\cdot5=15=2\cdot7+1$. ✓

Exercise 4

Let $M=\{1,2,3\}$ and $g(1)=\{1,2\}$, $g(2)=\emptyset$, $g(3)=\{1,3\}$. Compute Cantor's set $Y$ and check that it is not in the image of $g$.

Solution

$1\in g(1)$, so $1\notin Y$. $2\notin g(2)=\emptyset$, so $2\in Y$. $3\in g(3)$, so $3\notin Y$. Hence $Y=\{2\}$. The images are $\{1,2\},\emptyset,\{1,3\}$, none equal to $\{2\}$. ✓ (Each $g(a)$ differs from $Y$ exactly at $a$.)

You're done when

You can name the three rules of inference, and reproduce from memory: $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$, $|\mathcal P(M)|=2^n$ and Cantor's theorem. You can also explain why each nonzero element of $\mathbb Z_p$ has an inverse. Safe to skip: the bijection $\mathbb R\leftrightarrow\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$ (only quoted in the notes).
