1. The story: the court of the Free City

The scribes of lesson 2 write charters; now the city needs a court that decides which charters are proven. The court is strict. A verdict must be a finite list of sentences, each one either a law of the city or obtained from earlier sentences by one of a few accepted rules of reasoning. Over the years the judges have developed five styles of argument, and every famous case in the city archive is won by one of them.

The straight road walks from the facts to the claim. The back door proves “if not the claim, then not the facts”. The staircase proves a claim for step $0$ and shows that every step carries it to the next. The trap assumes the opposite and lets it destroy itself. And the dovecote: if more doves come home than there are holes, two doves share a hole.

Before the court can sit, the city needs a register: who belongs to which district (sets), who is assigned to whom (maps), and who counts as “of the same family” (equivalence relations). Today you learn the register and the five styles. You will meet each style again in every later lesson.

In mathematics: the register is sets, maps and relations; the five styles are the direct proof, the proof by contrapositive, induction, the proof by contradiction, and the pigeonhole principle. Linear Algebra uses all of them constantly. A linear map is first of all a map, and the questions “is it injective, is it surjective?” are the heart of chapter 3 of the script. The dimension of a vector space is a count, and counting arguments are pigeonhole arguments. Quotient spaces (lesson 9) are built from an equivalence relation, exactly as $\mathbb Z/n$ was in lesson 1.

2. Sets

The script treats sets as known from Analysis and fixes a few operations (p. 7). For sets $P,Q$:

Definition (operations on sets).

  • intersection $P\cap Q:=\{x\mid x\in P\wedge x\in Q\}$;
  • union $P\cup Q:=\{x\mid x\in P\vee x\in Q\}$;
  • difference $P\setminus Q:=\{x\mid x\in P\wedge x\notin Q\}$;
  • symmetric difference $P\triangle Q:=(P\cup Q)\setminus(P\cap Q)$, the elements in exactly one of the two;
  • complement: if all sets considered lie in a fixed set $X$, then $P^C:=X\setminus P$.

For a nonempty family $\mathcal A$ of sets: $\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal A}A=\{x\mid\exists A\in\mathcal A: x\in A\}$ and $\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal A}A=\{x\mid\forall A\in\mathcal A: x\in A\}$.

Look at the definitions: each set operation is a logical operator from lesson 2 ($\cap$ is $\wedge$, $\cup$ is $\vee$, $\setminus$ is “and not”; a big union is $\exists$, a big intersection is $\forall$). That is why every rule of lesson 2 becomes a rule for sets.

Equality and inclusion. $P\subseteq Q$ means $\forall x\,(x\in P\to x\in Q)$. Two sets are equal when they have the same elements, $P=Q\iff\forall x\,(x\in P\leftrightarrow x\in Q)$, equivalently $P\subseteq Q$ and $Q\subseteq P$. So there are two standard ways to prove $P=Q$: show $x\in P\iff x\in Q$ for an arbitrary $x$ (as in lesson 1 for the symmetric difference), or show both inclusions.

Proof A: De Morgan for sets, $X\setminus(P\cap Q)=(X\setminus P)\cup(X\setminus Q)$

Claim. For all sets $X,P,Q$: $\ X\setminus(P\cap Q)=(X\setminus P)\cup(X\setminus Q)$.

The idea. Translate membership in each side into a logical statement, and use De Morgan from lesson 2. The set identity is the logical law in disguise.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $x$ be arbitrary. We show $x\in X\setminus(P\cap Q)$ $\iff x\in(X\setminus P)\cup(X\setminus Q)$.”
  • Last line: “Since $x$ was arbitrary, the two sets are equal.”
  • The gap: unfold each operation into its logical operator, then one logical step.
The storyWhat you write

The register asks: who lives in the city but is not in both guilds? The clerk translates the question into the scribes' language.

Let $x$ be arbitrary. $x\in X\setminus(P\cap Q)$ $\iff x\in X\wedge\neg(x\in P\wedge x\in Q)$ (definitions of $\setminus$, $\cap$)

“Not in both” is “not in the first, or not in the second”: De Morgan.

$\iff x\in X\wedge(x\notin P\vee x\notin Q)$ (De Morgan)

A city resident who misses at least one guild is a resident outside the first guild, or a resident outside the second.

$\iff(x\in X\wedge x\notin P)$ $\vee\,(x\in X\wedge x\notin Q)$ (distributivity)
$\iff x\in(X\setminus P)\cup(X\setminus Q)$ (definitions of $\setminus$, $\cup$). Since $x$ was arbitrary, the sets are equal. ∎

Moves used: decide membership element by element a chain of named equivalences

The proof in one piece

Let $x$ be arbitrary. Then

$$\begin{aligned}x\in X\setminus(P\cap Q)&\iff x\in X\wedge\neg(x\in P\wedge x\in Q)\\&\iff x\in X\wedge(x\notin P\vee x\notin Q)\\&\iff(x\in X\wedge x\notin P)\vee(x\in X\wedge x\notin Q)\\&\iff x\in(X\setminus P)\cup(X\setminus Q),\end{aligned}$$

using the definitions, De Morgan and distributivity. As $x$ was arbitrary, the two sets have the same elements. ∎

Definition (Cartesian product, power set; script p. 7). For sets $X,Y$, the Cartesian product is $X\times Y:=\{(x,y)\mid x\in X,\ y\in Y\}$, the set of ordered pairs; more generally $X_1\times\dots\times X_n$ is the set of $n$-tuples, and $X^n:=X\times\dots\times X$ ($n$ factors). The power set $\mathcal P(X)$ is the set of all subsets of $X$.

Definition (finite set, cardinality; script p. 8). $|\emptyset|:=0$. For $n\ge1$, a set $X$ has cardinality $n$, written $|X|=n$, if there is a bijection $\{1,\dots,n\}\to X$. $X$ is finite if $|X|=n$ for some $n\in\mathbb N_0$, otherwise infinite, written $|X|=\infty$.

Examples: $\mathbb R^2=\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$ is the plane, $K^n$ will be the coordinate space of lesson 5, and $\mathcal P(\{1,2\})=\{\emptyset,\{1\},\{2\},\{1,2\}\}$ has $4=2^2$ elements. Notice how the definition of “$n$ elements” uses a bijection: counting is a bijection with $\{1,\dots,n\}$. Section 3 defines bijections, and section 6 pushes this idea to infinite sets.

Russell's paradox and the barber (script p. 8)

Let $R$ be “the set of all sets that do not contain themselves”, $R=\{X\mid X\notin X\}$. Is $R\in R$? By the definition of $R$: $R\in R\iff R\notin R$. A statement equivalent to its own negation is a contradiction, so this $R$ cannot be a set. Axiomatic set theory forbids such unrestricted constructions; the script, like most courses, works with “naive” set theory and only uses reasonable constructions.

The script's exercise: Bart, the barber of Seville, shaves exactly those men of Seville who don't shave themselves. Does Bart shave himself? If he does, he doesn't; if he doesn't, he does. The way out is the same as for Russell: such a barber cannot exist. The description is contradictory, so nobody fits it. Keep this argument in mind: it returns as Cantor's theorem in section 6.

Worked example 1: true or false? (FS17 and FS18, true/false)

For sets $A,B,C$: (i) $A\setminus(B\setminus C)=(A\setminus B)\cup(A\cap B\cap C)$ (FS17). (ii) $A\setminus(B\setminus C)=(A\cup B)\setminus C$ (FS18). Try them in your head first.

  1. Unfold $x\in A\setminus(B\setminus C)$.
    Answer

    $x\in A\wedge\neg(x\in B\wedge x\notin C)$ $\iff x\in A\wedge(x\notin B\vee x\in C)$. So: $x\in A$, and either $x\notin B$ or $x\in C$.

  2. Compare with (i), splitting by whether $x\in B$.
    Answer

    If $x\notin B$: the left side says $x\in A$; the right side says $x\in A\setminus B$, i.e. $x\in A$. If $x\in B$: the left side says $x\in A\wedge x\in C$; the right side says $x\in A\cap B\cap C$, i.e. (given $x\in B$) $x\in A\wedge x\in C$. Both cases agree: (i) is true.

  3. Find one element that breaks (ii).
    Answer

    (ii) contains elements of $B$ outside $A$, the left side never does. Take $A=\emptyset$, $B=\{1\}$, $C=\emptyset$: left $\emptyset$, right $\{1\}$. (ii) is false.

For a true/false question, draw three overlapping circles and shade both sides: seven regions, two minutes. For a proof, use the element chain.

3. Maps

Definition (map; script p. 13). Let $X,Y$ be sets. A map $f:X\to Y$ assigns to every $x\in X$ a uniquely determined $y=f(x)\in Y$. $X$ is the domain, $Y$ the target. In quantifiers: $\forall x\in X\ \exists!\,y\in Y: f(x)=y$. The image is $f(X)=\{y\in Y\mid\exists x\in X: y=f(x)\}$.

Two maps $f_1:X_1\to Y_1$, $f_2:X_2\to Y_2$ are equal if $X_1=X_2$, $Y_1=Y_2$ and $\forall x\in X_1: f_1(x)=f_2(x)$. For $A\subseteq X$, the restriction $f|_A:A\to Y$ is $f|_A(x)=f(x)$.

Note that the target belongs to the map: $x\mapsto x^2$ as a map $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ and as a map $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}$ are two different maps (and only the second is surjective). Standard examples from the script: the identity $\mathrm{id}_X(x)=x$; a constant map $f(x)=y_0$; the characteristic function $\mathbb 1_A:X\to\{0,1\}$, $1$ on $A$ and $0$ outside; the projections $\pi_1(x_1,x_2)=x_1$ and $\pi_2(x_1,x_2)=x_2$ on $X_1\times X_2$.

Definition (injective, surjective, bijective; script p. 13). Let $f:X\to Y$.

  • $f$ is injective if $\forall x_1,x_2\in X:$ $f(x_1)=f(x_2)\to x_1=x_2$ (different arguments, different values);
  • $f$ is surjective if $\forall y\in Y\ \exists x\in X: f(x)=y$ (every value is hit), i.e. $f(X)=Y$;
  • $f$ is bijective if it is injective and surjective.

Definition (inverse map, composition; script p. 14). If $f$ is bijective, every $y\in Y$ has exactly one $x$ with $f(x)=y$; the inverse map $f^{-1}:Y\to X$ sends $y$ to this $x$. Then $f\circ f^{-1}=\mathrm{id}_Y$ and $f^{-1}\circ f=\mathrm{id}_X$. For $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to Z$ the composition $g\circ f:X\to Z$ is $(g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x))$: first $f$, then $g$.

By lesson 2, $f$ is not injective iff $\exists x_1,x_2:$ $f(x_1)=f(x_2)\wedge x_1\ne x_2$, and not surjective iff $\exists y\in Y\ \forall x\in X: f(x)\ne y$. So each “no” is proved by naming a witness: a colliding pair, or a missed value. Composition is associative, $h\circ(g\circ f)=(h\circ g)\circ f$, since both send $x$ to $h(g(f(x)))$. It is not commutative: on $\mathbb R^2$, with $f$ the reflection in the $y$-axis and $g$ the shift by $1$ to the right, $(g\circ f)(0,0)=(1,0)$ but $(f\circ g)(0,0)=(-1,0)$ (script p. 14).

Try it: build a map between two small sets

Choose the sizes of $X$ and $Y$, then choose where each element of $X$ goes. The verdict names the witness: a pair that collides, or a value that is missed.

Proof B: compositions keep injective, surjective, bijective (Lemma 1.2.4, p. 15)

Claim. Let $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to Z$. (i) If $f$ and $g$ are injective, so is $g\circ f$. (ii) If $f$ and $g$ are surjective, so is $g\circ f$. (iii) If $f$ and $g$ are bijective, so is $g\circ f$, and $(g\circ f)^{-1}=f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}$.

The idea. Each property is a quantifier sentence; just walk through the two maps one at a time. For (i), start with equal values of $g\circ f$ and peel off $g$, then $f$. For (ii), start with a target $z$ and pull it back through $g$, then through $f$. For the inverse: undo the last map first, like taking off shoes before socks.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (i) First line: “Let $x_1,x_2\in X$ with $(g\circ f)(x_1)=(g\circ f)(x_2)$.” Last line: “so $x_1=x_2$.”
  • (ii) First line: “Let $z\in Z$.” Last line: “so $(g\circ f)(x)=z$”, for an $x$ we must name.
  • (iii) follows from (i), (ii); for the formula, check that $f^{-1}(g^{-1}(z))$ is sent to $z$.
The storyWhat you write

Every citizen is assigned a house ($f$), every house a district ($g$). Two citizens end up in the same district.

(i) Let $x_1,x_2\in X$ with $g(f(x_1))=g(f(x_2))$.

Different houses would lie in different districts, so they live in the same house; and different citizens would live in different houses, so they are the same citizen.

Since $g$ is injective, $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$. Since $f$ is injective, $x_1=x_2$. So $g\circ f$ is injective.

Now pick any district. It has a house in it, and that house has a citizen.

(ii) Let $z\in Z$. As $g$ is surjective, choose $y\in Y$ with $g(y)=z$. As $f$ is surjective, choose $x\in X$ with $f(x)=y$. Then $(g\circ f)(x)=g(y)=z$. So $g\circ f$ is surjective.

To find the citizen of a district, first find the house ($g^{-1}$), then the citizen ($f^{-1}$).

(iii) By (i) and (ii), $g\circ f$ is bijective. For $z\in Z$: $(g\circ f)\big(f^{-1}(g^{-1}(z))\big)$ $=g\big(f(f^{-1}(g^{-1}(z)))\big)$ $=g(g^{-1}(z))=z$. So $f^{-1}(g^{-1}(z))$ is the unique preimage of $z$, i.e. $(g\circ f)^{-1}=f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}$. ∎

Moves used: unfold the definition take an arbitrary element name a witness

The proof in one piece

(i) Let $f,g$ be injective and $x_1,x_2\in X$ with $g(f(x_1))=g(f(x_2))$. Since $g$ is injective, $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$; since $f$ is injective, $x_1=x_2$. Hence $g\circ f$ is injective.

(ii) Let $f,g$ be surjective and $z\in Z$. Choose $y\in Y$ with $g(y)=z$ and then $x\in X$ with $f(x)=y$. Then $(g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x))=g(y)=z$. Hence $g\circ f$ is surjective.

(iii) If $f,g$ are bijective, $g\circ f$ is bijective by (i) and (ii). For $z\in Z$ we have $(g\circ f)(f^{-1}(g^{-1}(z)))=g(g^{-1}(z))=z$, so the unique preimage of $z$ under $g\circ f$ is $f^{-1}(g^{-1}(z))$, that is, $(g\circ f)^{-1}=f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. In (i), which injectivity is used first, $f$'s or $g$'s?
    Answer

    $g$'s: the equation $g(f(x_1))=g(f(x_2))$ has $g$ on the outside, so $g$ is peeled off first.

  2. Rung 2. Show: if $g\circ f$ is injective, then $f$ is injective. (Nothing is assumed about $g$.)
    Answer

    Let $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$. Apply $g$: $g(f(x_1))=g(f(x_2))$ (equal inputs give equal outputs). As $g\circ f$ is injective, $x_1=x_2$.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof B (i) and (ii) from the frame alone.
How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Injective: start with two arguments with equal values, end with “so the arguments are equal”.
  2. Peel off the maps from the outside in, one injectivity per step.
  3. Surjective: start with “let $z$ be in the target”, pull back through the outer map, then the inner one, and check.
  4. Inverse of a composition: reverse order, $(g\circ f)^{-1}=f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}$.

In short: injective peels from outside in, surjective pulls back from outside in.

Worked example 2: “$f:M\to M$ is surjective if and only if $f\circ f$ is surjective.” True or false? (mock exam HS20, (3))

  1. Direction “$\Rightarrow$”: is it a special case of Proof B?
    Answer

    Yes: (ii) with $g=f$. If $f$ is surjective, so is $f\circ f$.

  2. Direction “$\Leftarrow$”: compare the images of $f\circ f$ and $f$.
    Answer

    Every value of $f\circ f$ is a value of $f$: $f(f(x))=f(y)$ with $y=f(x)$. So $(f\circ f)(M)\subseteq f(M)$. If $f\circ f$ is surjective, $M=(f\circ f)(M)\subseteq f(M)\subseteq M$, so $f(M)=M$. True (and the official solution agrees).

  3. Does the same work with “injective”?
    Answer

    Also true. “$\Rightarrow$” is (i) with $g=f$; “$\Leftarrow$” is Rung 2 of the ladder with $g=f$.

4. What a proof is: rules of inference

Halbeisen's definition: a mathematical proof is a finite sequence of statements such that each statement is either an axiom or follows from earlier statements by a rule of inference. The basic rule is modus ponens:

$$\frac{A,\qquad A\to B}{B}\qquad\text{“from $A$ and $A\to B$ we may conclude $B$”}.$$

In logic, this one rule suffices to prove everything. In practice we use convenient derived rules. Since $A\to B\iff\neg B\to\neg A$ (lesson 2), modus ponens can be applied to the contrapositive; and since a contradiction $C\wedge\neg C$ is never true, a statement whose negation leads to one must hold:

$$\begin{aligned}&\frac{A,\qquad\neg B\to\neg A}{B} &&\text{(contraposition rule)}\\[4pt]&\frac{\neg A\to(C\wedge\neg C)}{A} &&\text{(contradiction rule)}\end{aligned}$$

Further rules from the lecture: from $A\to B$ and $B\to C$ conclude $A\to C$ (chains; Exercise 3 of lesson 2); from $A\to B$ and $A\to C$ conclude $A\to(B\wedge C)$; from $A\to C$ and $B\to C$ conclude $(A\vee B)\to C$ (proof by cases). In written proofs nobody names these rules; you just write the steps. But knowing which rule the proof as a whole uses tells you how to start and how to end, and that is what the rest of this lesson trains.

Proof C (direct): there is no largest prime

Claim (lecture). Let $P\subseteq\mathbb N$ be the set of primes. Then $\neg\exists p\in P\ \forall q\in P\,(q\le p)$.

The idea. Negate the “there is no” with lesson 2: it says $\forall p\in P\ \exists p_0\in P\,(p_0>p)$, every prime has a bigger prime. That is a direct statement. Given $p$, build a number that no prime up to $p$ divides: the product of all of them, plus one. Its prime factors must be bigger than $p$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Rewrite the claim: $\neg\exists p\in P\ \forall q\in P\,(q\le p)$ $\iff\forall p\in P\ \exists p_0\in P\,(p_0>p)$.
  • First line: “Let $p\in P$ be arbitrary.” Last line: “So there is a prime $p_0>p$.”
  • The gap: name the witness $p_0$.
The storyWhat you write

A boastful guildmaster claims to own the largest prime. The judge takes his prime and multiplies together every prime up to it.

Let $p\in P$ be arbitrary, let $q_0,\dots,q_n$ be all the primes $\le p$, and put $r:=q_0\cdot q_1\cdots q_n$.

Then she adds one. Every small prime divides the product, so it leaves remainder $1$ in the new number.

For each $i$, $q_i\mid r$, so $q_i\nmid r+1$ (otherwise $q_i\mid(r+1)-r=1$).

But the new number, like every number above $1$, is built from primes. Those primes are none of the small ones, so they are bigger than the boast.

$r+1\ge2$ has a prime factor $p_0$, and $p_0\ne q_i$ for all $i$, so $p_0>p$. As $p$ was arbitrary, $\forall p\in P\ \exists p_0\in P\,(p_0>p)$, which is the claim. ∎

Moves used: flip every quantifier, negate the core name a witness

The proof in one piece

By the negation rules, the claim is equivalent to $\forall p\in P\ \exists p_0\in P\,(p_0>p)$. Let $p\in P$, let $q_0,\dots,q_n$ be the primes $\le p$ and $r:=q_0\cdots q_n$. No $q_i$ divides $r+1$, since it divides $r$ and would then divide $1$. So every prime factor $p_0$ of $r+1\ge2$ is different from all $q_i$, hence $p_0>p$. As $p$ was arbitrary, the claim follows. ∎

Proof D (contrapositive): $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$

Claim (lecture). $\sqrt2$ is not rational.

The idea. The lecture sets it up as modus ponens with the contraposition rule. Let $B$ be “$\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$” and $A$ be “$(\sqrt2)^2=2$”. $A$ is true by the definition of $\sqrt2$. So it suffices to show $A\to B$, and instead we show $\neg B\to\neg A$: if $\sqrt2$ were a fraction, its square could not be $2$. A fraction in lowest terms stays in lowest terms when squared, and a fraction in lowest terms equal to $2$ would need denominator $1$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “We show $\sqrt2\in\mathbb Q\to(\sqrt2)^2\ne2$. Assume $\sqrt2\in\mathbb Q$.”
  • Last line: “So $(\sqrt2)^2\ne2$. Since $(\sqrt2)^2=2$ holds, the contraposition rule gives $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$.”
  • The gap: from a fraction $\frac mn$ in lowest terms to $\frac{m^2}{n^2}\ne2$.
The storyWhat you write

The court uses the back door: it does not attack the claim, it shows that a rational $\sqrt2$ would contradict what everyone knows about $\sqrt2$.

Let $A$: $(\sqrt2)^2=2$ and $B$: $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$. $A$ holds by definition. We show $\neg B\to\neg A$.

Suppose $\sqrt2$ is a fraction; cancel it as far as possible.

Assume $\sqrt2\in\mathbb Q$. Then $\sqrt2=\frac mn$ with $m,n\ge1$ and $\gcd(m,n)=1$.

Squaring cannot create a common factor: a prime dividing $m^2$ and $n^2$ would divide $m$ and $n$.

Then $\gcd(m^2,n^2)=1$ (a prime dividing $m^2$ divides $m$, one dividing $n^2$ divides $n$).

A fraction in lowest terms equal to the whole number $2$ would need denominator $1$, and no square number is $2$.

If $\frac{m^2}{n^2}=2$, then $m^2=2n^2$, so $n^2\mid m^2$; with $\gcd(m^2,n^2)=1$ this forces $n^2=1$, hence $m^2=2$, impossible for $m\in\mathbb N$. So $(\sqrt2)^2=\frac{m^2}{n^2}\ne2$, i.e. $\neg A$. By the contraposition rule, $B$: $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$. ∎

Moves used: prove the contrapositive

The proof in one piece

Since $(\sqrt2)^2=2$, it suffices to show $\sqrt2\in\mathbb Q\Rightarrow(\sqrt2)^2\ne2$. Assume $\sqrt2=\frac mn$ with $m,n\ge1$ and $\gcd(m,n)=1$. Then also $\gcd(m^2,n^2)=1$, because a prime dividing $m^2$ and $n^2$ would divide $m$ and $n$. If $\frac{m^2}{n^2}=2$, then $m^2=2n^2$, so $n^2\mid m^2$, which with $\gcd(m^2,n^2)=1$ forces $n=1$ and $m^2=2$, impossible. So $(\sqrt2)^2\ne2$, a contradiction to the definition of $\sqrt2$. Hence $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$. ∎

  1. Rung 1. Which statement is $\neg B$, and which is $\neg A$?
    Answer

    $\neg B$: $\sqrt2\in\mathbb Q$. $\neg A$: $(\sqrt2)^2\ne2$.

  2. Rung 2. Adapt the proof to $\sqrt3\notin\mathbb Q$. Which line changes?
    Answer

    Only the last: $m^2=3n^2$ gives $n^2\mid m^2$, so $n=1$ and $m^2=3$, impossible. (The same argument shows $\sqrt k\notin\mathbb Q$ whenever $k$ is not a perfect square.)

Contrapositive or contradiction? The one-piece version ends with “a contradiction to the definition of $\sqrt2$”: written out, a proof by contrapositive and a proof by contradiction look almost the same. The difference is in the logic: the contrapositive proves $\neg B\to\neg A$ and concludes with modus ponens; the contradiction proves $\neg B\to(C\wedge\neg C)$. Both are fine in the exam. 5. Induction

The induction axiom (lecture). Let $A(n)$ be a statement about the natural number $n$. If

$$\underbrace{A(0)}_{\text{base case}}\quad\wedge\quad\underbrace{\forall n\,\big(A(n)\to A(n+1)\big)}_{\text{induction step}},$$

then $\forall n\,A(n)$. In words: if a statement holds for $0$, and whenever it holds for $n$ it also holds for $n+1$, then it holds for all natural numbers.

Halbeisen's $\mathbb N$ starts at $0$, so his base case is $n=0$. If a claim is only stated for $n\ge1$ (as in many exams), the base case is $n=1$; the axiom works from any starting point. In the step, $A(n)$ is called the induction hypothesis; you may use it, and you must use it, or the step is not an induction step.

Proof E (induction): a set with $n$ elements has $2^n$ subsets

Claim (lecture; exercise in the script, p. 8). For every $n\in\mathbb N$: if $|M|=n$, then $|\mathcal P(M)|=2^n$.

The idea. Add one new element $a$ to a set $M$. The subsets of the bigger set come in two kinds: those without $a$ (these are exactly the old subsets) and those with $a$ (each is an old subset with $a$ added). Both kinds have as many members as $\mathcal P(M)$, so the number doubles.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • The statement: $A(n)$: “for every set $M$ with $|M|=n$, $|\mathcal P(M)|=2^n$”.
  • Base case: $A(0)$. Step: assume $A(n)$, take any $\tilde M$ with $|\tilde M|=n+1$, show $|\mathcal P(\tilde M)|=2^{n+1}$.
  • The gap: write $\tilde M$ as $M\cup\{a\}$ and split $\mathcal P(\tilde M)$ into two disjoint pieces.
The storyWhat you write

The register counts committees. A guild with no members has exactly one possible committee: the empty one.

Base case. If $|M|=0$, then $M=\emptyset$ and $\mathcal P(M)=\{\emptyset\}$, so $|\mathcal P(M)|=1=2^0$.

Suppose every guild of $n$ members has $2^n$ committees. A guild of $n+1$ members: pick one member, call her $a$; the rest form a guild of $n$.

Step. Assume $A(n)$. Let $|\tilde M|=n+1$, choose $a\in\tilde M$ and put $M:=\tilde M\setminus\{a\}$, so $|M|=n$ and $\tilde M=M\cup\{a\}$ with $a\notin M$.

Every committee either leaves $a$ out (an old committee) or includes her (an old committee plus $a$). No committee is of both kinds.

Then $\mathcal P(\tilde M)=\mathcal P(M)\cup\big\{x\cup\{a\}:x\in\mathcal P(M)\big\}$, and the two parts are disjoint (sets in the second contain $a$, those in the first don't).

Different old committees stay different when $a$ joins. So both kinds have $2^n$ members.

$x\mapsto x\cup\{a\}$ is injective on $\mathcal P(M)$ (remove $a$ again), so the second part also has $|\mathcal P(M)|$ elements. Hence $|\mathcal P(\tilde M)|=2\cdot|\mathcal P(M)|=2\cdot2^n=2^{n+1}$ by $A(n)$. By induction, $\forall n\,A(n)$. ∎

Moves used: induction: base, hypothesis, step decide membership element by element

The proof in one piece

Let $A(n)$ be the statement “$|M|=n\Rightarrow|\mathcal P(M)|=2^n$ for every set $M$”. Base case: if $|M|=0$, then $M=\emptyset$, $\mathcal P(M)=\{\emptyset\}$ and $|\mathcal P(M)|=1=2^0$. Step: assume $A(n)$ and let $|\tilde M|=n+1$. Choose $a\in\tilde M$ and set $M:=\tilde M\setminus\{a\}$, so $|M|=n$. Every subset of $\tilde M$ either does not contain $a$, so it lies in $\mathcal P(M)$, or contains $a$, so it equals $x\cup\{a\}$ for $x:=$ the subset minus $a$, $x\in\mathcal P(M)$. Hence $\mathcal P(\tilde M)=\mathcal P(M)\cup\{x\cup\{a\}:x\in\mathcal P(M)\}$, a disjoint union, and $x\mapsto x\cup\{a\}$ is injective on $\mathcal P(M)$. Therefore $|\mathcal P(\tilde M)|=2|\mathcal P(M)|=2\cdot2^n=2^{n+1}$. By induction, $A(n)$ holds for all $n$. ∎

How to reproduce an induction under exam pressure
  1. Write the statement $A(n)$ in one line, with the starting value of $n$.
  2. Base case: check $A(\text{start})$ by computing both sides.
  3. Step: “Assume $A(n)$ for some $n\ge\text{start}$.” Write down what you must show, $A(n+1)$.
  4. Split the $(n+1)$ case into “the $n$ case plus one new piece”, use the hypothesis on the first part, compute the rest.
  5. End with “By induction, $A(n)$ holds for all $n\ge\text{start}$.”

In short: state, base, assume, split off the last piece, conclude.

Worked example 3: which statement implies $A_n$ for all integers $n\ge1$? (HS21, question XII)

In each option, $A_1$ holds and, for all $k,\ell\ge1$, $(A_k\wedge A_\ell)\Rightarrow\ \dots$: (a) $A_{\ell\cdot k}$ (b) $A_{k^\ell}$ (c) $A_{2k+3\ell}$ (d) $A_{k+\ell}$.

  1. Starting from $A_1$ alone, which indices can (a) and (b) ever reach?

    Only $1$: $1\cdot1=1$ and $1^1=1$. So nothing new is ever obtained.

    Answer

6. Contradiction, and how big infinite sets are

A proof by contradiction shows $A$ by assuming $\neg A$ and deriving a contradiction $C\wedge\neg C$. It is the natural method for “there is no …”: assume there is one, and let it destroy itself.

Proof F (contradiction): Cantor's theorem

Claim (lecture). Let $M$ be any set, finite or infinite, and $g:M\to\mathcal P(M)$ any map. Then $g$ is not surjective: $\exists Y\in\mathcal P(M)\ \forall a\in M\,(g(a)\ne Y)$.

The idea. We must name a subset $Y$ of $M$ that $g$ misses. Use the barber: let $Y$ consist of the elements $a$ that are not in their own set $g(a)$. If some $a_0$ had $g(a_0)=Y$, then asking “is $a_0\in Y$?” gives the barber's answer: yes if and only if no.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Name the witness: $Y:=\{a\in M: a\notin g(a)\}$. Then $Y\subseteq M$, so $Y\in\mathcal P(M)$.
  • Show it's missed by contradiction: “Assume $g(a_0)=Y$ for some $a_0\in M$.” … “Contradiction. So no $a\in M$ has $g(a)=Y$.”
  • The gap: ask whether $a_0\in Y$.
The storyWhat you write

A clerk claims his list assigns to every citizen a committee, and that every possible committee appears on the list. The judge forms one more committee: all citizens who are not on their own assigned committee.

Let $Y:=\{a\in M: a\notin g(a)\}$. Then $Y\subseteq M$, so $Y\in\mathcal P(M)$.

If the clerk is right, this committee is on his list, assigned to some citizen $a_0$.

Assume, to the contrary, that $g(a_0)=Y$ for some $a_0\in M$.

Is $a_0$ on that committee? By its rule, $a_0$ is on it exactly when $a_0$ is not on their own assigned committee, which is this one. Yes if and only if no.

By definition of $Y$ and $g(a_0)=Y$: $a_0\in Y\iff a_0\notin g(a_0)\iff a_0\notin Y$. So $a_0\in Y\wedge a_0\notin Y$ (whichever holds implies the other), a contradiction.

So the clerk's list misses this committee.

Hence $\forall a\in M\,(g(a)\ne Y)$, and $g$ is not surjective. ∎

Moves used: assume the opposite name a witness

The proof in one piece

Let $Y:=\{a\in M: a\notin g(a)\}\in\mathcal P(M)$. Suppose $g(a_0)=Y$ for some $a_0\in M$. Then $a_0\in Y\iff a_0\notin g(a_0)\iff a_0\notin Y$, a contradiction. Hence no $a\in M$ satisfies $g(a)=Y$, so $g$ is not surjective. ∎

  1. Rung 1. Take $M=\{1,2\}$ and $g(1)=\{1\}$, $g(2)=\emptyset$. What is $Y$, and is it missed?
    Answer

    $1\in g(1)$, so $1\notin Y$; $2\notin g(2)$, so $2\in Y$. $Y=\{2\}$, which is neither $g(1)$ nor $g(2)$. Missed, as promised.

  2. Rung 2. Why does “$a_0\in Y\iff a_0\notin Y$” give a contradiction of the form $C\wedge\neg C$?
    Answer

    Either $a_0\in Y$ or $a_0\notin Y$. In the first case the equivalence gives $a_0\notin Y$ as well; in the second it gives $a_0\in Y$. Either way both hold.

Definition (countable; lecture). A set $M$ is countable if it is finite or there is a bijection $f:\mathbb N\to M$ (its elements can be listed $f(0),f(1),f(2),\dots$). Otherwise $M$ is uncountable.

Infinite countable sets: $\mathbb N$, the primes, $2\mathbb Z$, $\mathbb Z$ (list it as $0,1,-1,2,-2,3,\dots$), and even $\mathbb Q$ (list the fractions $\frac mn$ by $|m|+n=1,2,3,\dots$ and skip repetitions). With Cantor's theorem, $\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$ is uncountable: a bijection $\mathbb N\to\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$ would be a surjection, and there is none. One can show that $\mathbb R$ and $\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$ are in bijection (think of binary expansions), so $\mathbb R$ is uncountable too. The lecture states this without proof; for us, the takeaway is the method: uncountable is a “there is no” statement, so it is proved by contradiction.

Why should a Linear Algebra student care? Because of a theme that returns in lesson 10: the vector space of all finite $0$-$1$ sequences over $\mathbb F_2$ has a countable basis, but its dual space is as big as $\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$, so it is uncountable and cannot have a countable basis. Halbeisen proves exactly this kind of statement ($\dim V<\dim V^*$ for such $V$).

7. The pigeonhole principle

The pigeonhole principle (lecture). If $m+1$ objects ($m\in\mathbb N_0$) are put into $m$ boxes, then at least one box contains at least two objects. In the language of maps: if $X,Y$ are finite with $|X|>|Y|$, then no map $X\to Y$ is injective.

The lecture remarks that this follows from properties of maps between finite sets; we take it as given (it is also what the map explorer of section 3 says when $|X|>|Y|$). Its power lies in proving that something exists without constructing it: two objects in one box exist, though we may not know which.

Proof G (pigeonhole): every nonzero class in $\mathbb Z/p$ has an inverse

Claim (lecture; compare Serie 1, exercise 8 (d)). Let $p$ be prime and $1\le a\le p-1$. Then there is $k$ with $0\le k\le p-1$ such that $\bar a\cdot\bar a^k=\bar1$ in $\mathbb Z/p$. In particular $\bar a^k$ is the inverse of $\bar a$.

We use one fact proved in lesson 4 (Euclid's lemma): $\mathbb Z/p$ has no zero divisors, i.e. $\bar x\cdot\bar y=\bar0$ implies $\bar x=\bar0$ or $\bar y=\bar0$, because a prime dividing $xy$ divides $x$ or $y$.

The idea. Look at the powers $\bar a^0,\bar a^1,\dots,\bar a^{p-1}$: that is $p$ objects. None is $\bar0$ (no zero divisors), so they all lie in the $p-1$ boxes $\bar1,\dots,\overline{p-1}$. Two of them coincide, $\bar a^s=\bar a^t$ with $s<t$. Then $\bar a^s(\bar a^{t-s}-\bar1)=\bar0$, and cancelling $\bar a^s$ leaves $\bar a^{t-s}=\bar1$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • To show: $\exists k\ (0\le k\le p-1\wedge\bar a\cdot\bar a^k=\bar1)$. So we must name $k$.
  • Objects and boxes: $p$ powers, $p-1$ nonzero classes.
  • The gap: turn “two powers are equal” into “some power is $\bar1$”, using no zero divisors.
The storyWhat you write

On the clock of a prime hour count, the clockmaker turns the hand by “times $a$” again and again, and notes where it lands: $p$ landings.

Consider the $p$ classes $\bar a^0,\bar a^1,\dots,\bar a^{p-1}$ in $\mathbb Z/p$.

The hand never lands on $0$: no zero divisors on a prime clock. So the landings fall on the $p-1$ other hours.

Since $\bar a\ne\bar0$ and $\mathbb Z/p$ has no zero divisors, $\bar a^n\ne\bar0$ for all $n$ (induction on $n$). So all $p$ classes lie in $\{\bar1,\dots,\overline{p-1}\}$, which has $p-1$ elements.

More landings than hours: two landings coincide.

By the pigeonhole principle there are $0\le s<t\le p-1$ with $\bar a^s=\bar a^t$. Write $t=s+k+1$ with $k\ge0$; then $k\le p-2$.

The clockmaker cancels the common turns. What remains brings the hand back to $1$.

$\bar a^s=\bar a^s\cdot\bar a^{k+1}$ gives $\bar a^s\cdot(\bar a^{k+1}-\bar1)=\bar0$. As $\bar a^s\ne\bar0$ and there are no zero divisors, $\bar a^{k+1}-\bar1=\bar0$, i.e. $\bar a\cdot\bar a^k=\bar1$. ∎

Moves used: pigeonhole: more objects than boxes cancel with an inverse

The proof in one piece

Since $p$ is prime, $\mathbb Z/p$ has no zero divisors; as $\bar a\ne\bar0$, all powers $\bar a^n$ are $\ne\bar0$. Thus the $p$ classes $\bar a^0,\dots,\bar a^{p-1}$ lie in the $(p-1)$-element set $\{\bar1,\dots,\overline{p-1}\}$, and by the pigeonhole principle there are $0\le s<t\le p-1$ with $\bar a^s=\bar a^t$. Write $t=s+k+1$ with $0\le k\le p-2$. Then $\bar a^s(\bar a^{k+1}-\bar1)=\bar a^t-\bar a^s=\bar0$, and since $\bar a^s\ne\bar0$, $\bar a^{k+1}=\bar1$, i.e. $\bar a\cdot\bar a^k=\bar1$. ∎

    Answer

    $\bar3^0,\dots,\bar3^6=\bar1,\bar3,\bar2,\bar6,\bar4,\bar5,\bar1$. First repetition: $s=0$, $t=6$, so $k=5$ and $\bar3\cdot\bar3^5=\bar3\cdot\bar5=\overline{15}=\bar1$. The inverse of $\bar3$ is $\bar5$.

8. Equivalence relations

Definition (relation, equivalence relation). A relation on a set $M$ is a subset $R\subseteq M\times M$; we write $x\sim y$ for $(x,y)\in R$. It is an equivalence relation if it is

  • reflexive: $\forall x\in M:\ x\sim x$;
  • symmetric: $\forall x,y\in M:$ $x\sim y\to y\sim x$;
  • transitive: $\forall x,y,z\in M:$ $(x\sim y\wedge y\sim z)\to x\sim z$.

The equivalence class of $x$ is $[x]:=\{y\in M: x\sim y\}$; each $y\in[x]$ is a representative of it. The set of all classes is written $M/{\sim}$.

A partition of $M$ is a set of nonempty subsets of $M$ such that every element of $M$ lies in exactly one of them.

Examples: equality “$=$” on any set (lesson 2); congruence modulo $n$ on $\mathbb Z$, $a\equiv b\iff n\mid(b-a)$, whose classes are the residue classes of $\mathbb Z/n$ (lesson 1); “has the same number of elements” on the finite subsets of a set. Non-examples: “$\le$” on $\mathbb R$ (not symmetric), “$x-y$ is odd” on $\mathbb Z$ (not reflexive: $x-x=0$ is even; FS18), “$A\cap B=\emptyset$” on $\mathcal P(X)$ (not transitive: $\{1\}\cap\emptyset=\emptyset$ and $\emptyset\cap\{1\}=\emptyset$, but $\{1\}\cap\{1\}\ne\emptyset$; FS17).

Worked example 4: congruence modulo $n$ is an equivalence relation (script, Exercise 1.1.7)

For $a,b\in\mathbb Z$: $a\equiv b$ mod $n$ $\iff\exists l\in\mathbb Z: b-a=ln$. The script says reflexivity and symmetry are almost immediate, “only transitivity requires a moment's thought”.

  1. Reflexive and symmetric: name the witnesses $l$.
    Answer

    $a-a=0\cdot n$, so $l=0$. If $b-a=ln$, then $a-b=(-l)n$, so $l'=-l$.

  2. Transitive: from $b-a=ln$ and $c-b=kn$, find the witness for $c-a$.
    Answer

    Add the two equations: $c-a=(c-b)+(b-a)=(k+l)n$. Witness $k+l\in\mathbb Z$. So $a\equiv c$.

The pattern “name the witness, add the two equations” is the same as in the well-definedness proof of lesson 1. That is no accident: both are about the same relation.

Proof H: the classes of an equivalence relation form a partition

Claim. Let $\sim$ be an equivalence relation on $M$. Then every class is nonempty, every $x\in M$ lies in a class, and any two classes $[x],[y]$ are either equal or disjoint. So $M/{\sim}$ is a partition of $M$. Moreover $[x]=[y]\iff x\sim y$.

The idea. Reflexivity puts $x$ into its own class, which settles “nonempty” and “covers $M$”. For “equal or disjoint”: if two classes share one element $z$, then by symmetry and transitivity everything related to $x$ is related to $y$ and conversely, so the classes coincide.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Covering: “$x\in[x]$ by reflexivity.”
  • Equal or disjoint: “Assume $[x]\cap[y]\ne\emptyset$, say $z\in[x]\cap[y]$. We show $[x]=[y]$.” Two inclusions, each by element chase.
  • The gap: from $x\sim z$ and $y\sim z$, get $x\sim y$; then chase an element $w$.
The storyWhat you write

The city's families: everyone belongs to their own family, because everyone is related to themselves.

For $x\in M$, $x\sim x$ (reflexive), so $x\in[x]$. Hence every class is nonempty and every element lies in a class.

Suppose two families share a member $z$. Then the two heads are related through $z$.

Let $z\in[x]\cap[y]$, so $x\sim z$ and $y\sim z$. By symmetry $z\sim y$, and by transitivity $x\sim y$.

Then anyone in the second family is related to the first head, through the second head; and the other way round. One family.

If $w\in[y]$, then $y\sim w$; with $x\sim y$ transitivity gives $x\sim w$, so $w\in[x]$. Thus $[y]\subseteq[x]$. By symmetry of the argument (using $y\sim x$), $[x]\subseteq[y]$. So $[x]=[y]$: two classes are equal or disjoint.

And two people are in the same family exactly when they are related.

If $x\sim y$, the last step gives $[x]=[y]$. Conversely, if $[x]=[y]$, then $y\in[y]=[x]$, so $x\sim y$. ∎

Moves used: decide membership element by element unfold the definition

The proof in one piece

By reflexivity $x\in[x]$ for every $x\in M$, so the classes are nonempty and cover $M$. First we show: $x\sim y$ implies $[x]=[y]$. If $w\in[y]$, then $y\sim w$, and $x\sim y$ gives $x\sim w$ by transitivity, so $w\in[x]$; hence $[y]\subseteq[x]$. As $y\sim x$ by symmetry, the same argument gives $[x]\subseteq[y]$. Now if $[x]\cap[y]\ne\emptyset$, pick $z$ in it: $x\sim z$ and $y\sim z$, so $z\sim y$ and $x\sim y$, hence $[x]=[y]$. So two classes are equal or disjoint, and $M/{\sim}$ is a partition. Finally, if $[x]=[y]$ then $y\in[x]$, i.e. $x\sim y$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Which of the three properties does each part use?
    Answer

    Covering and nonempty: reflexivity. Equal or disjoint: symmetry and transitivity. $[x]=[y]\Rightarrow x\sim y$: reflexivity ($y\in[y]$).

  2. Rung 2. Conversely, given a partition of $M$, define $x\sim y$ iff $x,y$ lie in the same part. Why is this an equivalence relation?
    Answer

    Every $x$ lies in exactly one part, so “in the same part as” is reflexive; symmetric by its wording; and transitive because $x,y$ share a part and $y,z$ share a part, which must be the same part (the one containing $y$). So equivalence relations and partitions are two descriptions of the same thing.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof H from the frame alone.
How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Reflexivity: $x\in[x]$, so classes are nonempty and cover $M$.
  2. Lemma: $x\sim y\Rightarrow[y]\subseteq[x]$ (transitivity), and by symmetry the reverse.
  3. If $z\in[x]\cap[y]$: $x\sim z$, $z\sim y$, so $x\sim y$, so $[x]=[y]$.

In short: reflexive covers, a shared element links, transitivity merges.

Worked example 5: three exam statements on equivalence relations

True or false? (a) If $\sim$ is an equivalence relation on $X$, then $\{[x]\mid x\in X\}$ is a partition of $X$ (FS18). (b) If $|X|=n>3$ and $3\nmid n$, there is no equivalence relation on $X$ with exactly three classes (HS20). (c) For $n\ge2$ there are $\frac{n(n-1)}2$ equivalence relations on $\{1,\dots,n\}$ (mock exam HS20).

  1. (a)?
    Answer

    True: Proof H.

  2. (b)? Do classes have to be equally big?
    Answer

    No. On $\{1,2,3,4\}$, the partition $\{1,2\},\{3\},\{4\}$ gives (by Rung 2 of Proof H) an equivalence relation with exactly three classes. False.

  3. (c)? Count for $n=2$ and $n=3$.
    Answer

    Equivalence relations correspond to partitions. $\{1,2\}$ has $2$ partitions ($\{1,2\}$ and $\{1\},\{2\}$), but $\frac{2\cdot1}2=1$. False. (For $n=3$ there are $5$, formula gives $3$.)

Why it's cool

Cantor's argument fits in four lines and shows that there are different sizes of infinity, a fact that shocked mathematicians in the 1870s. The same diagonal trick, “take the thing that disagrees with every entry of the list on its own diagonal”, later proved that some problems cannot be solved by any computer (Turing) and that some true statements cannot be proved (Gödel). And the humble pigeonhole principle just proved that $\mathbb Z/p$ is a field, the field behind every encrypted message you send.

For wonder: the hotel with infinitely many rooms

Hilbert's hotel has rooms $0,1,2,\dots$, all occupied. A new guest arrives: everyone moves from room $n$ to room $n+1$, and room $0$ is free. A bus with infinitely many guests arrives: everyone moves from $n$ to $2n$, and all odd rooms are free. The maps $n\mapsto n+1$ and $n\mapsto2n$ are injective but not surjective $\mathbb N\to\mathbb N$, something impossible for finite sets (by the pigeonhole principle, an injective map from a finite set to itself is surjective). That is one way to define “infinite”. But if the bus carries one guest for each subset of $\mathbb N$, Cantor says: no rearrangement fits them all.

Worked example 6: which method?

  1. Your answers?
    Answer

(b) Induction: “Base case $n=1$: $1=\frac{1\cdot2}2$.” (c) Contradiction: “Assume $f:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$ is surjective.” (Or: $\mathbb R$ is uncountable.) Worked example 7: is $f:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z$, $f(x)=2x+1$, injective, surjective?

  1. Injective?
    Answer

    Let $2x_1+1=2x_2+1$. Then $2x_1=2x_2$, so $x_1=x_2$. Injective.

  2. Surjective? Find the witness for the negation.
    Answer

    All values are odd, so $y=0$ is never hit: $2x+1=0$ has no integer solution. Not surjective. As a map $\mathbb Q\to\mathbb Q$ the same formula is bijective, with inverse $y\mapsto\frac{y-1}2$: the domain and target matter.

Worked example 8: give the definition of a relation on $M$; when is it an equivalence relation? (FS17, problem 2 (a))

  1. Write the answer you would hand in.
    Answer

    A relation on $M$ is a subset $R\subseteq M\times M$; we write $x\sim y$ for $(x,y)\in R$. It is an equivalence relation if it is reflexive ($\forall x\in M: x\sim x$), symmetric ($\forall x,y\in M:$ $x\sim y\Rightarrow y\sim x$) and transitive ($\forall x,y,z\in M:$ $x\sim y\wedge y\sim z\Rightarrow x\sim z$). Definitions earn their points only when complete: all three properties, each with its quantifiers.

Exercise 1 (FS19, problem 6 (a), 3 points)

Prove by induction that for all integers $n\ge0$:

$$\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^kk^2=(-1)^n\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}2.$$
Hint

In the step, split off the last term $(-1)^{n+1}(n+1)^2$, use the hypothesis, and factor out $(-1)^{n+1}(n+1)$.

Solution

Frame: base case $n=0$; step from $n$ to $n+1$ by splitting off the last term. Moves: induction: base, hypothesis, step.

Let $A(n)$ be the formula. Base case $n=0$: the left side is the empty sum $0$, the right side is $(-1)^0\cdot\frac{0\cdot1}2=0$.

Step. Assume $A(n)$ for some $n\ge0$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}(-1)^kk^2&=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^kk^2+(-1)^{n+1}(n+1)^2\\&=(-1)^n\frac{n(n+1)}2+(-1)^{n+1}(n+1)^2\\&=(-1)^{n+1}(n+1)\Big((n+1)-\frac n2\Big)\\&=(-1)^{n+1}(n+1)\cdot\frac{n+2}2\\&=(-1)^{n+1}\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}2,\end{aligned}$$

using $A(n)$ in the second line and $(-1)^n=-(-1)^{n+1}$ in the third. That is $A(n+1)$. By induction, $A(n)$ holds for all $n\ge0$.

Exercise 2 (sets)

Prove for all sets: (a) $A\cap(B\cup C)=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C)$. (b) $A\triangle B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)$. (c) $A\subseteq B\iff A\cap B=A$.

Solution

Frame: element chains; for (c) two implications. Moves: decide membership element by element.

(a) $x\in A\cap(B\cup C)$ $\iff x\in A\wedge(x\in B\vee x\in C)$ $\iff(x\in A\wedge x\in B)$ $\vee\,(x\in A\wedge x\in C)$ $\iff x\in(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C)$, by distributivity of $\wedge$ over $\vee$.

(b) $x\in A\triangle B$ $\iff(x\in A\vee x\in B)$ $\wedge\,\neg(x\in A\wedge x\in B)$: $x$ lies in exactly one of $A,B$, i.e. $(x\in A\wedge x\notin B)\vee(x\in B\wedge x\notin A)$ $\iff x\in(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)$. (Check the four cases of $x\in A$, $x\in B$ if unsure.)

(c) “$\Rightarrow$”: $A\cap B\subseteq A$ always; and if $x\in A$ then $x\in B$ by assumption, so $x\in A\cap B$. “$\Leftarrow$”: if $x\in A=A\cap B$, then $x\in B$.

Exercise 3 (maps)

(a) Show: if $g\circ f$ is surjective, then $g$ is surjective. (b) Give maps $f,g$ with $g\circ f$ bijective but $f$ not surjective and $g$ not injective. (c) Let $f:X\to Y$ be bijective. Show that $f^{-1}$ is bijective with $(f^{-1})^{-1}=f$.

Solution

Frame: (a) pull back $z$; (b) a small counterexample; (c) check the two identities. Moves: name a witness; one counterexample is enough.

(a) Let $z\in Z$. As $g\circ f$ is surjective, there is $x$ with $g(f(x))=z$. Then $y:=f(x)$ satisfies $g(y)=z$.

(b) $X=\{1\}$, $Y=\{1,2\}$, $Z=\{1\}$, $f(1)=1$, $g(1)=g(2)=1$. Then $g\circ f=\mathrm{id}_{\{1\}}$ is bijective, $f$ misses $2$, and $g(1)=g(2)$.

(c) $f^{-1}(f(x))=x$ for all $x\in X$ and $f(f^{-1}(y))=y$ for all $y\in Y$. Injective: if $f^{-1}(y_1)=f^{-1}(y_2)$, apply $f$: $y_1=y_2$. Surjective: $x=f^{-1}(f(x))$. So $f^{-1}$ is bijective, and its inverse sends $x$ to the unique $y$ with $f^{-1}(y)=x$, which is $y=f(x)$. So $(f^{-1})^{-1}=f$.

Exercise 4 (contrapositive)

Prove: for all $n\in\mathbb Z$, if $n^2$ is divisible by $3$, then $n$ is divisible by $3$. Then use it to give a second proof that $\sqrt3\notin\mathbb Q$.

Solution

Frame: contrapositive “$3\nmid n\Rightarrow3\nmid n^2$” by cases on the remainder; then a contradiction with a fraction in lowest terms. Moves: prove the contrapositive; assume the opposite.

If $3\nmid n$, then $n=3k+1$ or $n=3k+2$. Then $n^2=9k^2+6k+1=3(3k^2+2k)+1$ or $n^2=9k^2+12k+4$ $=3(3k^2+4k+1)+1$: remainder $1$ in both cases, so $3\nmid n^2$. By contraposition, $3\mid n^2\Rightarrow3\mid n$.

Now assume $\sqrt3=\frac mn$ with $\gcd(m,n)=1$. Then $m^2=3n^2$, so $3\mid m^2$, so $3\mid m$, $m=3m'$. Then $9m'^2=3n^2$, $n^2=3m'^2$, so $3\mid n$. So $3$ divides both $m$ and $n$, contradicting $\gcd(m,n)=1$.

Exercise 5 (Cantor and countability)

(a) For $M=\{1,2,3\}$ and $g(1)=\{2,3\}$, $g(2)=\{2\}$, $g(3)=\{1,3\}$, compute Cantor's set $Y$ and check that it is missed. (b) Write down a bijection $\mathbb N\to\mathbb Z$. (c) True or false: $\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$ is countable.

Solution

Frame: (a) test each $a\in g(a)$; (b) alternate signs; (c) Cantor. Moves: name a witness; assume the opposite.

(a) $1\notin g(1)$, so $1\in Y$; $2\in g(2)$, so $2\notin Y$; $3\in g(3)$, so $3\notin Y$. $Y=\{1\}$, and indeed $g(1),g(2),g(3)$ are all $\ne\{1\}$.

(b) $f(n)=\frac n2$ for even $n$ and $f(n)=-\frac{n+1}2$ for odd $n$: $0,-1,1,-2,2,\dots$. Each integer appears exactly once.

(c) False. $\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$ is infinite, and a bijection $\mathbb N\to\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$ would be surjective, which Cantor's theorem rules out.

Exercise 6 (pigeonhole)

(a) Among $n+1$ integers there are two whose difference is divisible by $n$. (b) Show: if $X$ is finite and $f:X\to X$ is injective, then $f$ is surjective. (c) Find the inverse of $\bar4$ in $\mathbb Z/11$ with the method of Proof G.

Solution

Frame: objects and boxes in each part. Moves: pigeonhole: more objects than boxes.

(a) Objects: the $n+1$ integers; boxes: the $n$ residue classes mod $n$. Two integers $a\ne b$ share a class, so $n\mid a-b$.

(b) Suppose $f$ misses some $y\in X$. Then $f$ maps the $|X|$ elements of $X$ into $X\setminus\{y\}$, which has $|X|-1$ elements; by pigeonhole two of them have the same value, contradicting injectivity.

(c) Powers of $\bar4$ in $\mathbb Z/11$: $\bar1,\bar4,\overline{16}=\bar5,\overline{20}=\bar9,\overline{36}=\bar3,\overline{12}=\bar1$. So $\bar4^5=\bar1$, i.e. $\bar4\cdot\bar4^4=\bar1$ with $\bar4^4=\bar3$. Check: $4\cdot3=12\equiv1$. The inverse is $\bar3$.

Exercise 7 (equivalence relations)

Which are equivalence relations? For those that are, describe the classes. (a) On $\mathbb R$: $x\sim y\iff x-y\in\mathbb Z$. (b) On $\mathbb Z$: $x\sim y\iff xy>0$. (c) On $\mathbb R^2$: $(a,b)\sim(c,d)\iff a^2+b^2=c^2+d^2$. (d) On $\mathcal P(X)$: $A\sim B\iff A\cap B=\emptyset$.

Solution

Frame: check the three properties; one failure is enough for “no”. Moves: unfold the definition; one counterexample is enough.

(a) Yes. $x-x=0\in\mathbb Z$; if $x-y\in\mathbb Z$ then $y-x\in\mathbb Z$; if $x-y,y-z\in\mathbb Z$ then their sum $x-z\in\mathbb Z$. Classes $[x]=x+\mathbb Z$, one for each $x\in[0,1)$.

(b) No: not reflexive, since $0\cdot0=0\not>0$. (On $\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}$ it is one, with the two classes “positive” and “negative”.)

(c) Yes: “$=$” on the values of $a^2+b^2$ is reflexive, symmetric, transitive. Classes: circles around the origin, plus the class $\{(0,0)\}$.

(d) No (FS17): not transitive, as shown in section 8; also not reflexive for $A\ne\emptyset$.

Exercise 8 (true or false, old exam style)

(a) If $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to Z$ with $g\circ f$ injective, then $g$ is injective. (b) $\mathbb Q$ is countable. (c) Every map from a finite set to itself that is surjective is injective. (d) $P:=\{p\in\mathbb N\mid$ $\forall m\in\mathbb N:\ m\mid p\Rightarrow(m=1\vee m=p)\}$ is finite (FS17). (e) In every group, $\forall a,b,c:\ b=c\iff a\circ b=a\circ c$ (FS17).

Solution

(a) False: Exercise 3 (b) has $g\circ f$ injective and $g$ not injective. (Only $f$ must be injective.) (b) True: section 6. (c) True: if two elements had the same value, the remaining $|X|-1$ values could not cover all $|X|$ elements. (d) False: $P$ contains every prime (and also $1$), and there are infinitely many primes (Proof C). Note $0\notin P$, since every $m$ divides $0$. (e) True: “$\Rightarrow$” applies $a\circ$ to equal elements; “$\Leftarrow$” multiplies from the left by $a^{-1}$ (cancel with an inverse).
