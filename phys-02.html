Why it matters for the exam

Every exam problem from here on starts with Newton's second law or with a conservation law, and this lesson introduces both. The multiple-choice block “Konzeptfragen: Kinematik und Dynamik” asks about momentum, impulses and the rocket equation almost every time, and older exams have whole problems on rockets and recoil (two of them are the exercises below).

Recap of lesson 1 (3 min)

  1. A throw has $v_y(t)=v_{y0}-gt$. Which condition gives the top of the flight?

    Answer

    $v_y=0$, so $t_{\text{top}}=\frac{v_{y0}}g$.

  2. In uniform circular motion with speed $v$ on radius $r$, where does the acceleration point, and how large is it?

    Answer

    To the centre, with size $a=\frac{v^2}r=\omega^2r$.

  3. How many initial conditions fix a motion in three dimensions, and why?

    Answer

    Six: three for the position and three for the velocity, because the equation of motion is of second order in each of the three components.

1. The story: winter on the moat

In winter the moat around the fortress freezes so smooth that nothing on it ever slows down. The archers use it for a game. Two of them stand back to back on skates, push off, and glide apart. The heavy smith always drifts away slowly, the thin page boy shoots off fast, and the quartermaster, who keeps the guild's ledgers, noticed a strange rule: if you multiply each skater's weight by their speed, the two numbers are always equal, whoever pushes harder.

So the quartermaster keeps a ledger of “push”. Everything that moves on the ice carries some, as mass times velocity. Pushing never creates push or destroys it: it only moves push from one skater to the other, one gains exactly what the other loses. The only way to change the total on the ice is a push from outside, from the bank. And how fast a skater's push changes is exactly how hard they are being pushed.

Next to the moat the alchemists test their fire-sleds: a sled that throws burning powder backwards and so pushes itself forwards, with nothing to push against but its own smoke.

In physics: the “push” in the ledger is the momentum $\vec p=m\vec v$, the rule of the ledger is conservation of momentum in an isolated system, “how fast push changes” is the force $\vec F=\dot{\vec p}$ (Newton's second law), the exactly opposite gain and loss is Newton's third law, and the fire-sled is a rocket.

2. Mass and momentum

Mass. The script defines mass with a recoil experiment (section 3.1.1, page 32): two carts on a frictionless air track are tied together with a spring squeezed between them. Cut the thread, and they fly apart with speeds $v_A$ and $v_B$. The experiment shows

$$\frac{m_A}{m_B}=\frac{v_B}{v_A},$$

independently of the spring and even of gravity (it works the same way in space). This ratio defines the mass, once one standard mass is fixed as $1\,\mathrm{kg}$. Mass is a scalar property of the body. Weight is different: it is a force, the pull of gravity, and an astronaut in orbit is weightless but not massless.

Inertial and gravitational mass (section 3.1.2). The recoil experiment measures the inertial mass, a balance measures the gravitational mass. Experiments (Eötvös, Dicke) show they are equal to about one part in $10^{11}$. That's why every body falls with the same $g$, and why you can't tell gravity apart from an accelerated frame (lesson 4).

Momentum (section 3.2, page 36). Rewrite the recoil result with velocity vectors (the carts move in opposite directions):

$$m_A\vec v_A+m_B\vec v_B=\vec 0=\text{the value before the thread was cut}.$$

So the quantity $\vec p=m\vec v$, the momentum, adds up to the same total before and after. It is a vector, with unit $\mathrm{kg\,m/s}=\mathrm{N\,s}$.

Conservation of momentum (section 3.3)

In an isolated system (no external forces, or external forces that cancel), the total momentum $\vec p_{\text{tot}}=\sum_i\vec p_i$ is constant, in size and direction. Since it is a vector, this gives three separate laws, one per component: $p_{\text{tot},x}$, $p_{\text{tot},y}$ and $p_{\text{tot},z}$ are each constant.

It is an axiom: it can't be proved, only tested, and no exception is known. (The deeper reason: physics works the same everywhere in space. More in the For wonder section.)

Try it: the recoil experiment

Choose the two masses and the spring, then cut the thread. Watch the momenta (the arrows) and their sum.

Whatever you choose, $p_A=-p_B$ and the sum stays $0$. A stronger spring makes both faster, but the ratio $\frac{v_B}{v_A}=\frac{m_A}{m_B}$ never changes.

3. Newton's second law: force changes momentum

Take a ball on a string, swinging in a uniform circle (section 3.4.1, page 38). Its momentum $\vec p=m\vec v$ keeps its size but turns all the time. Differentiating, as in lesson 1:

$$\dot{\vec p}=m\dot{\vec v}=-m\omega^2\vec r,$$

which points along the string, to the centre. The string is what changes the momentum. This is the content of the second law:

Newton's second law (law of action) $$\vec F=\frac{d\vec p}{dt},\qquad\text{and for constant mass}\qquad \vec F=m\vec a.$$

Here $\vec F=\sum_i\vec F_i$ is the net force: the vector sum of all forces acting on this body, added component by component. Unit: $1\,\mathrm N=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s^2}$. Since $m$ is a scalar, $\vec a$ always points along the net force.

Two remarks the script stresses. First, $\vec F=\dot{\vec p}$ is not a definition of force but a law that experiments confirm: forces come from interactions (gravity, springs, contact, …), each described by its own law, and the second law then tells you the motion. Second, $\vec F=m\vec a$ follows from $\vec F=\dot{\vec p}$ only if $m$ is constant; for a rocket it isn't, and we go back to momentum (section 6).

In Rupp's lectures the laws are called NAx 1, 2, 3, and she writes $\vec F_{1\to2}$ for the force that body 1 exerts on body 2. Draw a box around the body you are studying (the “system boundary”): only forces from outside the box go into $\vec F$.

The impulse of a force

Integrate $\dot{\vec p}=\vec F$ over the time the force acts (section 3.4.2, page 41):

$$\vec p_E-\vec p_A=\int_{t_A}^{t_E}\vec F(t)\,dt.$$

The integral is the impulse (Kraftstoss). In one dimension it is the area under the $F(t)$ curve. You usually don't know $F(t)$ in detail, but from the momenta before and after you know the area. A large force for a short time and a small force for a long time give the same momentum change if the areas agree. Subscripts $A$ and $E$ mean before (Anfang) and after (Ende), as in Rupp's notation.

Try it: why airbags work

A passenger of $70\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $54\,\mathrm{km/h}=15\,\mathrm{m/s}$ has to be brought to rest: the impulse is always $m v=1050\,\mathrm{N\,s}$. Change how long the stop takes.

The shaded area never changes. A dashboard stops you in about $0.01\,\mathrm s$ with roughly $150$ times your weight; an airbag stretches the stop to about $0.1\,\mathrm s$ and cuts the force tenfold.

Worked example 1: a ball against a wall

A ball of mass $m$ hits a wall head-on with speed $v$. Compare the impulse the wall gives the ball if (a) the ball sticks, (b) it bounces back with the same speed.

  1. Choose an axis and write $p_A$ and $p_E$ in case (a).

    Answer

    Axis towards the wall. $p_A=mv$, and sticking means $p_E=0$. Impulse on the ball: $p_E-p_A=-mv$ (pointing away from the wall).

  2. Case (b)?

    Answer

    $p_E=-mv$, so the impulse is $-mv-mv=-2mv$: twice as large. Bouncing needs twice the impulse of stopping. (The script uses exactly this to explain gas pressure later: molecules bouncing off a wall.)

  3. Is the ball's momentum conserved in (b)? Its kinetic energy?

    Answer

    Momentum: no, it changes from $mv$ to $-mv$, because the wall exerts an external force on the ball. Kinetic energy: yes, $\frac12mv^2$ before and after. Momentum is a vector, energy only cares about the speed.

4. Newton's first law and inertial frames

Set $\vec F=\vec0$ in the second law (section 3.5, page 45): $\dot{\vec p}=\vec 0$, so for constant mass $\vec v$ is constant.

Newton's first law (law of inertia)

A body stays at rest or keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed if the net force on it is zero.

“At rest” and “constant velocity” depend on who is watching. A frame in which the first law holds is called an inertial frame. If frame $K'$ moves with constant velocity $\vec u$ relative to an inertial frame $K$, positions are related by the Galilei transformation $\vec r\,'(t)=\vec r(t)-\vec u\,t$. Then $\vec v\,'=\vec v-\vec u$ and $\vec a\,'=\vec a$: accelerations, and so forces, are the same in both frames, and $K'$ is inertial too. An accelerated or rotating frame is not inertial: lesson 4 shows what happens there.

A body moving at constant velocity has net force zero, not “no forces”. A cyclist rolling at her top speed downhill feels gravity, the road and the air, and they cancel exactly.

5. Newton's third law: action = reaction

Look at two bodies $A$ and $B$ that only interact with each other (section 3.6, page 46). Momentum conservation gives $\dot{\vec p}_A+\dot{\vec p}_B=\vec 0$, so by the second law

$$\vec F_{B\to A}=-\vec F_{A\to B}.$$
Newton's third law (action = reaction)

If body $A$ exerts a force on body $B$, then $B$ exerts a force on $A$ of the same size in the opposite direction. There is no single, isolated force: forces come in pairs.

The classic trap. The two forces of a pair act on different bodies, so they never cancel each other. A ball lying on a table (section 3.6.1): the ball pushes down on the table, $\vec F_{1\to2}$, and the table pushes up on the ball, $\vec F_{2\to1}=-\vec F_{1\to2}$. That is the third-law pair. The ball's weight (the earth pulling the ball) and the table's push on the ball also have the same size and opposite directions, but they act on the same body. That is equilibrium, a consequence of the second law with $\vec a=\vec 0$, not the third law. Its third-law partner of the weight is the ball pulling the earth up.

Worked example 2: who wins the tug of war?

A strong adult and a child pull on the two ends of a rope. By the third law, the rope pulls equally hard on both. So how can the adult win?

  1. Which forces act on the adult (as the body inside the box)?

    Answer

    The rope (pulling towards the child), gravity, and the ground: upwards, and horizontally through friction at the feet.

  2. So what decides the game?

    Answer

    The net force on each person. The rope force is the same for both, so the one whose feet get the larger horizontal push from the ground has a net force away from the middle and wins. The third law says nothing about who wins: it only pairs forces on different bodies.

6. The rocket

A rocket pushes burnt gas backwards and is pushed forwards by it (section 3.7.3, page 50). It needs no air to push against: it pushes against its own exhaust. Its mass changes, so we use momentum, not $F=ma$.

Worked example 3: deriving the rocket equation

A rocket of mass $M(t)$ moves with velocity $v$ (far from any planet, so no external force). It ejects gas with speed $u$ relative to the rocket. In a short time $dt$ it ejects the mass $dm>0$.

  1. Write the total momentum at time $t$ and at $t+dt$.

    Answer

    At $t$: $Mv$. At $t+dt$ the rocket has mass $M-dm$ and velocity $v+dv$, and the gas has mass $dm$ and velocity $v-u$:

    $$p(t+dt)=(M-dm)(v+dv)+dm\,(v-u)=Mv+M\,dv-u\,dm-dm\,dv.$$

  2. Use momentum conservation. What do you get, and what is the thrust?

    Answer

    Drop $dm\,dv$ (a product of two small quantities). Conservation: $p(t+dt)-p(t)=M\,dv-u\,dm=0$, so

    $$M\,dv=u\,dm,\qquad M\frac{dv}{dt}=u\frac{dm}{dt}.$$

    The right side acts like a force: the thrust $F=u\,\frac{dm}{dt}$ (exhaust speed times mass ejected per second). This is the line on the formula sheet.

  3. Integrate from mass $m_0$ (start, velocity $v_0$) to mass $m_1$ (end).

    Answer

    The rocket's mass decreases by what is ejected: $dM=-dm$. So $dv=-u\frac{dM}M$ and

    $$v_1-v_0=-u\int_{m_0}^{m_1}\frac{dM}M=-u\ln\frac{m_1}{m_0}=u\ln\frac{m_0}{m_1}.$$

    This is the rocket equation. The final speed depends only on the exhaust speed and the mass ratio, not on how fast the fuel is burnt.

Try it: the rocket equation

The logarithm is cruel: doubling the mass ratio only adds $u\ln2\approx0.7u$. To reach orbit ($\approx8\,\mathrm{km/s}$, plus losses) with $u\approx3\,\mathrm{km/s}$ you'd need a mass ratio of about $20$, which is why real rockets drop empty stages. Once the mass ratio exceeds $e\approx2.72$, the rocket is faster than its own exhaust, and the gas actually moves forwards as seen from the ground.

7. The recipe for any dynamics problem

Section 3.8.1 (page 52) gives a recipe that works for every problem in this course. The free fall in section 3.8.2 is the script's first test of it.

Recipe: solving with Newton's laws
  1. Choose the body (draw the system boundary).
  2. Draw all forces acting on it, in a general position (not only at the start).
  3. Choose an inertial frame and axes that make the motion simple (one axis along the motion if possible).
  4. Write the second law in components: $m\ddot x=\sum_iF_{ix}$, $m\ddot y=\sum_iF_{iy}$, …
  5. Solve the differential equations: the general solution has free constants $C_1,C_2,\dots$
  6. Fix the constants with the initial conditions $\vec r(t_0)$, $\vec v(t_0)$.

In short: body, forces, axes, components, solve, initial values.

Worked example 4: a puck pushed harder and harder

A puck of mass $m$ rests on frictionless ice at $x=0$. From $t=0$ on, a horizontal force $F(t)=F_0\frac t\tau$ pushes it along $x$ ($F_0,\tau$ constants). Find $v(t)$ and $x(t)$.

  1. Steps 1–4: which forces, and what is the equation of motion along $x$?

    Answer

    Gravity (down) and the ice (up) cancel; along $x$ only $F(t)$ acts. So $m\ddot x=F_0\frac t\tau$.

  2. Step 5: integrate twice.

    Answer

    $\dot x(t)=\frac{F_0}{2m\tau}t^2+C_1$ and $x(t)=\frac{F_0}{6m\tau}t^3+C_1t+C_2$.

  3. Step 6: the initial conditions.

    Answer

    $\dot x(0)=0$ gives $C_1=0$, $x(0)=0$ gives $C_2=0$. So $v(t)=\frac{F_0t^2}{2m\tau}$ and $x(t)=\frac{F_0t^3}{6m\tau}$. Check the dimension of $v$: $\frac{\mathrm N\cdot\mathrm s^2}{\mathrm{kg}\cdot\mathrm s}=\frac{\mathrm{kg\,m/s^2}\cdot\mathrm s}{\mathrm{kg}}=\mathrm{m/s}$. ✓ (Also check with the impulse: $\int_0^tF\,d\tilde t=\frac{F_0t^2}{2\tau}=mv$. ✓)

Why it's cool. Momentum conservation lets you predict the outcome of a messy interaction (an explosion, a push, a collision) without knowing a single force inside it. The fire-sled, the skaters and an exploding firework all obey the same ledger, and Exercise 5 below finds the angle between two firework fragments from that ledger alone.

8. Exercises

Do them on paper. Numbers only at the end, $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, no calculator.

Exercise 1 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.1(b), 1 point, original)

A ball hits a wall and afterwards moves with the same speed in the opposite direction. Which statement is correct? (A) The ball's energy and momentum are unchanged. (B) Neither the ball's energy nor its momentum is conserved. (C) The ball's energy is conserved, its momentum is not. (D) The ball's energy is not conserved, its momentum is.

Solution

C. As in Worked example 1(b): the speed is the same, so $\frac12mv^2$ is unchanged, but the momentum turns from $m\vec v$ to $-m\vec v$, a change of $2mv$ given by the wall's impulse. The ball alone is not an isolated system: the wall exerts an external force.

Exercise 2 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.1(c), 1 point, original)

A force $\vec F$ acts for a short time $\delta t$ on a dumbbell at rest (two masses $m$ joined by a light rod). The two sketches in the original differ only in where the force acts: in one, it pushes along the dumbbell's axis; in the other, it pushes one mass sideways, so the dumbbell also starts to spin. In which case does the dumbbell's centre of mass get the larger speed? Neglect friction. (A) Sketch a. (B) Sketch b. (C) No difference. (D) Not enough information.

Solution

C. The impulse $\vec F\,\delta t$ is the same in both cases, so the total momentum of the dumbbell changes by the same amount: $2m\,\vec v_S=\vec F\,\delta t$, where $\vec v_S$ is the centre-of-mass velocity. Where the force hits only decides whether the dumbbell also spins (then it gets extra rotational energy, from the longer path of the pushing point), not the momentum.

Exercise 3 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.1(b), 1 point, original)

Consider the rocket equation $v(t)-v_0=u\ln\frac{m_0}{m(t)}$. Which statement is correct? (A) It is based on conservation of kinetic energy. (B) The rocket's final speed depends on the speed at which the fuel is ejected. (C) Seen from the launch point, the exhaust gas always moves opposite to the rocket. (D) It holds in the same way for a rocket in a gravitational field.

Solution

B. The final speed is proportional to $u$. (A) is wrong: the derivation uses momentum conservation (burning fuel releases energy, so kinetic energy is not conserved). (C) is wrong: once $\frac{m_0}m>e$ the rocket is faster than $u$, and the gas moves forwards as seen from the ground. (D) is wrong: in a gravitational field the external force $-mg$ adds a term $-gt$ (Exercise 5).

Exercise 4 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.1(d), 1 point, original)

Rolling downhill without pedalling, a cyclist reaches a top speed $v_{\max}$ because of air resistance. Which statement is false? (A) $v_{\max}$ does not depend on the cyclist's posture on the bike. (B) Unlike friction, gravity is a conservative force. (C) When she moves straight at constant $v_{\max}$, no net force acts on her. (D) The friction force depends on the speed and acts against the direction of motion.

Solution

A is false. At $v_{\max}$ the velocity is constant, so by the first law the net force is zero (C is true): the downhill pull of gravity equals the air drag. The drag depends on her frontal area and shape (lesson 3), so a crouched cyclist reaches a higher $v_{\max}$. (B) and (D) are true; conservative forces come in lesson 6.

Exercise 5 (FS11, problem 1 “Silvesterrakete”, 8 points, original)

A New Year's rocket of mass $m=0.1\,\mathrm{kg}$ starts vertically. It ejects its fuel with constant speed $u=5\,\mathrm{km/s}$ (relative to the rocket).

(a) What gas output $\frac{dm}{dt}$ makes the rocket hover just above the launch pad? Treat the rocket's mass as constant. [2 points]

(b) How strongly does the rocket accelerate with three times that output? Again treat the mass as constant. [2 points]

(c) With the fuel used up, the rocket (now $0.08\,\mathrm{kg}$) flies at $20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and explodes into two pieces. Piece 1 has mass $0.03\,\mathrm{kg}$ and flies at $40\,\mathrm{m/s}$, piece 2 flies at $30\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find the angle between the paths of the two pieces. [4 points]

Solution

Frame: forces on the rocket are the thrust $u\frac{dm}{dt}$ (up) and its weight $mg$ (down).

(a) Hovering means $a=0$, so the net force is zero: $u\frac{dm}{dt}=mg$, hence

$$\frac{dm}{dt}=\frac{mg}u=\frac{0.1\cdot10}{5000}\,\mathrm{kg/s}=2\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{kg/s}=0.2\,\mathrm{g/s}.$$

(b) Thrust $3mg$, weight $mg$: $ma=3mg-mg$, so $a=2g=20\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ upwards.

(c) The explosion is internal, so momentum is conserved during it (gravity acts too, but over the instant of the explosion its impulse is negligible). Before: $p=0.08\cdot20=1.6\,\mathrm{N\,s}$. After: piece 2 has mass $0.05\,\mathrm{kg}$, so $p_1=0.03\cdot40=1.2\,\mathrm{N\,s}$ and $p_2=0.05\cdot30=1.5\,\mathrm{N\,s}$. As vectors $\vec p=\vec p_1+\vec p_2$; squaring with the angle $\theta$ between $\vec p_1$ and $\vec p_2$:

$$p^2=p_1^2+p_2^2+2p_1p_2\cos\theta\iff\cos\theta=\frac{2.56-1.44-2.25}{2\cdot1.2\cdot1.5}=\frac{-1.13}{3.6}\approx-0.31,$$

so $\theta\approx108^\circ$. (Points: momentum conservation as vectors, the masses of the pieces, the cosine rule.)

Exercise 6 (HS11, problem 3 “Manueller Raketenwagen”, 10 points, original)

A person ($m_P=80\,\mathrm{kg}$) stands on a cart ($m_W=120\,\mathrm{kg}$) loaded with four stones of $m_S=10\,\mathrm{kg}$ each. The cart is at rest. Neglect all friction.

(a) The person throws the stones horizontally one after the other, always in the same direction. Find the cart's velocity change $\Delta v_N$ (cart, person and remaining stones) caused by throwing stone $N$ ($N=1,\dots,4$), measured in the rest frame of the cart just before that throw, if each stone leaves the hands with speed $v_{S0}$ in that frame. [4 points]

(b) With $v_{S0}=2\,\mathrm{m/s}$: the cart's speed after the first stone and after the last stone, in the original rest frame. [3 points]

(c) What would the final speed be if the person could throw all four stones at once with $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$? [2 points]

(d) Are the final speeds of (b) and (c) different? Explain why. [1 point]

Solution

Frame: the system “cart + person + stones” is isolated horizontally, so its horizontal momentum is conserved. Work in the frame where the cart is at rest just before the throw: the total momentum there is $0$.

(a) Before throw $N$, the cart carries $4-N+1$ stones. After the throw it has mass $M_N=m_P+m_W+(4-N)m_S=(240-10N)\,\mathrm{kg}$. Momentum: $0=m_S(-v_{S0})+M_N\,\Delta v_N$, so

$$\Delta v_N=\frac{m_S\,v_{S0}}{M_N}=\frac{10\,v_{S0}}{240-10N}=\frac{v_{S0}}{24-N}.$$

(b) The Galilei transformation adds the changes. After the first stone: $\frac2{23}\,\mathrm{m/s}\approx0.087\,\mathrm{m/s}$. After the last:

$$v_4=2\left(\tfrac1{23}+\tfrac1{22}+\tfrac1{21}+\tfrac1{20}\right)\mathrm{m/s}\approx2\cdot0.187\,\mathrm{m/s}\approx0.37\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

(c) All at once: $0=4m_S(-2\,\mathrm{m/s})+(m_P+m_W)v$, so $v=\frac{80}{200}\,\mathrm{m/s}=0.40\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

(d) Yes: all at once is faster here. Each stone gets $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ relative to the cart before its throw. In the sequential case, the recoil of each throw also has to speed up the stones still lying on the cart (230, 220, 210 kg instead of 200 kg), and that momentum is “wasted” when those stones are thrown later from a moving cart with only the same relative speed. (A real rocket differs: its exhaust speed is fixed relative to the rocket after ejection, and there continuous ejection is the natural case.)

9. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

The newspaper that apologised 49 years late. In 1920 the New York Times mocked the rocket pioneer Robert Goddard: a rocket could never work in space, it wrote, because there is nothing there to push against. You now know why that is wrong: a rocket pushes against its own exhaust, and momentum conservation needs no air. On 17 July 1969, the day after Apollo 11 left for the Moon, the paper printed a short correction: it is now “definitely established that a rocket can function in a vacuum as well as in an atmosphere. The Times regrets the error.”

Why momentum is conserved at all. The script hints at it: the laws of physics don't change if you move the whole experiment a few metres to the side. Emmy Noether proved in 1918 that every such symmetry gives a conserved quantity: shifting in space gives momentum, shifting in time gives energy, rotating gives angular momentum. Rupp starts her chapter on conservation laws with exactly this theorem.
