Why it matters for the exam

Thermodynamics is about a quarter of every exam: a multiple-choice block “Thermodynamik” (three or four points) and the last long problem (problem 5, nine or ten points). Lessons 14–16 cover it together. Today is the foundation that every one of those questions stands on: $pV=\nu RT$, the first law with its signs, and $U=\frac f2\nu RT$. Four of the recent MC questions are answered by today alone, and every problem 5 starts with them.

Recap of lesson 13 (3 min)

  1. What is the phase between force and oscillator at resonance?

    Answer

    $-\frac\pi2$: the oscillator lags a quarter period behind, so its velocity is in phase with the force.

  2. Why is the energy of a single oscillating molecule interesting for today?

    Answer

    A vibrating molecule is a tiny oscillator with kinetic and potential energy. Today we count such “places to store energy” (degrees of freedom), and a vibration counts twice, once for each kind of energy.

  3. What is work, as an integral (lesson 5)?

    Answer

    $W=\int\vec F\cdot d\vec r$. For a gas pushing a piston of area $A$ with pressure $p$ this becomes $\int pA\,dx=\int p\,dV$, today's formula.

1. The story: the hot-air balloon

At the castle fair, the alchemist fills a silk balloon over a fire. As the air inside warms, the balloon swells, grows lighter than the air around it, and lifts off. A farmer asks how much firewood it took. The alchemist shrugs: “That depends. Did the balloon swell, or did I keep it in a pot with a lid?”

Later, the blacksmith drops a red-hot horseshoe into a bucket of water. The water warms a little, the iron cools a lot, and after a minute both have the same temperature. Nobody can tell any more which was hot.

In physics: temperature is what is equal when two bodies stop exchanging heat. Heat is energy that flows because of a temperature difference. A gas stores energy in the motion of its molecules, its internal energy $U$, and you can change $U$ in two ways: by heating it or by doing work on it. That is the first law. The alchemist's remark is the difference between $C_V$ and $C_p$: a gas that is allowed to expand spends part of the heat on pushing the air away, so it needs more heat for the same warming.

2. Temperature, heat and heat capacity

Temperature. The script defines it with a gas thermometer (pages 279–282). At constant pressure, a dilute gas expands linearly with temperature, and at constant volume its pressure rises linearly (Gay-Lussac):

$$V=C_1T\quad(p\text{ const}),\qquad p=C_2T\quad(V\text{ const}).$$

Extrapolating the straight lines down, every gas reaches $V=0$ or $p=0$ at the same point, $-273.15\,^\circ\mathrm C$. That is absolute zero, and the Kelvin scale starts there: $T/\mathrm K=\vartheta/{}^\circ\mathrm C+273.15$, fixed by the triple point of water at $273.16\,\mathrm K$ (page 282). In every thermodynamics formula, $T$ is in kelvin. Only temperature differences are the same in both scales.

Heat capacity (section 10.3, page 284). The heat $\Delta Q$ needed to warm a body by $\Delta T$ is proportional to $\Delta T$:

$$\Delta Q=C\,\Delta T=m\,c_m\,\Delta T=\nu\,c_M\,\Delta T.$$

$C$ is the heat capacity of the body (in J/K), $c_m$ the specific heat per kilogram, $c_M$ per mole. Water has the famously large $c_m=4.19\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}$ (that is the old calorie, $1\,\mathrm{cal}=4.1868\,\mathrm J$). Most solids have about the same molar heat, $c_M\approx3R\approx25\,\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}$: the Dulong–Petit rule (page 286). We'll see in section 5 where the $3R$ comes from.

Mixing. Put two bodies together in an insulated box. Heat flows from hot to cold until both have the same final temperature $T_e$, and no heat is lost, so what one gives the other takes: $C_1(T_1-T_e)=C_2(T_e-T_2)$. Solved,

$$T_e=\frac{C_1T_1+C_2T_2}{C_1+C_2},$$

a weighted average, weighted with the heat capacities. The script does this with a metal and water (page 286).

The zeroth law (section 10.4, page 287): if A is in thermal equilibrium with C and B is in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in equilibrium with each other. It sounds obvious, but it is exactly what makes a thermometer (the C) meaningful.

3. The first law

A system has an internal energy $U$: all the kinetic and potential energy of its molecules. $U$ is a state function: it depends only on the present state (for example on $p$, $V$, $T$), not on how the system got there. It changes in exactly two ways (section 10.5, page 288):

$$dU=\delta W^\swarrow+\delta Q^\swarrow.$$

The script's arrows are the whole sign convention, so learn to read them: $\swarrow$ means into the system, $\nearrow$ means out of it. $\delta Q^\swarrow$ is the heat supplied to the gas, $\delta W^\swarrow$ the work done on the gas. Swapping the arrow swaps the sign: $W^\nearrow=-W^\swarrow$. Heat and work are not state functions (a gas has no “amount of heat”), which is why they get a $\delta$ instead of a $d$.

Work on a gas. A gas pushing a piston of area $A$ a distance $dx$ outward does the work $F\,dx=pA\,dx=p\,dV$ on the surroundings (page 295). So

$$\delta W^\nearrow=p\,dV,\qquad\delta W^\swarrow=-p\,dV.$$

Expanding ($dV>0$): the gas does work, $W^\swarrow<0$. Compressing: work is done on the gas, $W^\swarrow>0$. On a $p$–$V$ diagram, $|W|$ is the area under the curve.

Side note: many books (and some lecturers) write $\Delta U=Q-W$ with $W$ the work done by the gas. That is the same law with $W=W^\nearrow$. The exam uses the script's arrows and asks for “eine eindeutige Konvention” (FS25 problem 5): say which way your arrow points, and you get the points.

Recipe: first-law bookkeeping for an ideal gas

  1. Name the process: what is constant ($V$, $p$, $T$, or no heat)?
  2. $\Delta U=\nu C_V\Delta T$, always for an ideal gas, whatever the process (section 5 explains why).
  3. $W^\swarrow=-\int p\,dV$: zero at constant $V$, $-p\,\Delta V$ at constant $p$.
  4. $Q^\swarrow=\Delta U-W^\swarrow$.
  5. Check the signs with common sense: heated and expanding means $Q^\swarrow>0$, $W^\swarrow<0$.

4. The ideal gas

An ideal gas is the model of point-like molecules that don't attract each other and only collide elastically (section 10.6, page 289). Real gases come close when they are dilute (low pressure, high temperature). Combining Gay-Lussac with Boyle–Mariotte ($pV=$ const at constant $T$) and the fact that $pV$ doubles when you double the amount of gas gives the equation of state (pages 291–292):

$$pV=NkT=\nu RT.$$

Two versions of the same thing: count molecules ($N$, with the Boltzmann constant $k=1.381\cdot10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J/K}$) or count moles ($\nu=N/N_A$, with $R=N_Ak=8.314\,\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}$, $N_A=6.022\cdot10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}$). $kT$ is an energy: at room temperature $kT\approx4.1\cdot10^{-21}\,\mathrm J\approx\frac1{40}\,\mathrm{eV}$, the typical energy of one molecule. And $pV$ is an energy too (Pa·m³ = J).

Notice what is not in the equation: which gas it is. One mole of helium and one mole of nitrogen fill the same volume at the same $p$ and $T$. At $0\,^\circ\mathrm C$ and $1\,\mathrm{bar}$ that is about $22.7$ litres. (The script's $22.4\,\mathrm{m^3/kmol}$ is the value at $1\,\mathrm{atm}=1.013\,\mathrm{bar}$.)

Try it: a gas in a box

Set the temperature and the volume of one mole of gas. Press Go to watch the molecules: hotter means faster, and smaller means more hits on the walls per second. Both raise the pressure. The right panel shows the state on the $p$–$V$ diagram, on its isotherm.

Halve the volume at fixed $T$: the pressure doubles (Boyle–Mariotte), and the point slides along its isotherm, the hyperbola $p=\frac{\nu RT}V$. Raise $T$: the point jumps to a higher isotherm.

Worked example 1: a balloon of gas

One mole of gas at $0\,^\circ\mathrm C$ and $p=1\,\mathrm{bar}$ fills a balloon. (a) What is its volume? (b) The sun warms it to $27\,^\circ\mathrm C$ at the same pressure. What is the new volume? (c) What is $kT$ then?

  1. Which form of the equation of state, and in which units?

    Answer

    Moles are given, so $pV=\nu RT$ with $T$ in kelvin and $p$ in pascal: $V=\frac{\nu RT}p$ $=\frac{1\cdot8.314\cdot273}{10^5}\,\mathrm{m^3}$ $=22.7\,\mathrm L$.

  2. At constant $p$, how does $V$ change with $T$?

    Answer

    $V\propto T$ (Gay-Lussac), so $V_2=V_1\frac{T_2}{T_1}=22.7\cdot\frac{300}{273}\,\mathrm L=24.9\,\mathrm L$. Not $\frac{27}{0}$: in Celsius the ratio makes no sense.

  3. And $kT$?

    Answer

    $kT=1.381\cdot10^{-23}\cdot300\,\mathrm J$ $=4.1\cdot10^{-21}\,\mathrm J$. In electronvolts ($1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.6\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$): $0.026\,\mathrm{eV}$.

Result: $V_1=22.7\,\mathrm L$, $V_2=24.9\,\mathrm L$, $kT\approx4.1\cdot10^{-21}\,\mathrm J$. The trap in (b) is the Celsius scale: ratios of temperatures need kelvin.

5. The two specific heats and equipartition

For a gas it matters how you heat it (section 10.6.2, pages 293–295). Per mole:

$$\Delta Q_V=\nu C_V\,\Delta T\quad(V\text{ const}),\qquad\Delta Q_p=\nu C_p\,\Delta T\quad(p\text{ const}).$$

Why $C_p>C_V$. At constant volume no work is done, so all the heat goes into $U$, and $C_V=\frac1\nu\frac{dU}{dT}$. At constant pressure the gas expands and lifts the piston: $\delta W^\nearrow=p\,dV=\nu R\,dT$ (from $pV=\nu RT$ with $p$ fixed). The heat must pay for this work as well:

$$C_p=\frac1\nu\left(\frac{dU}{dT}+\frac{\delta W^\nearrow}{dT}\right)=C_V+R.$$

This is the alchemist's balloon: the swelling balloon pushes the air away and needs more firewood.

Equipartition (section 10.6.3, page 296). In thermal equilibrium every degree of freedom, every independent quadratic term in a molecule's energy, holds on average $\frac12kT$. A molecule with $f$ degrees of freedom has the mean energy $\frac f2kT$, and an ideal gas of $\nu$ moles has

$$U=\frac f2\nu RT,\qquad C_V=\frac f2R,\qquad C_p=\frac{f+2}2R,\qquad\kappa=\frac{C_p}{C_V}=\frac{f+2}f.$$

So for an ideal gas $U$ depends only on $T$: that is step 2 of the recipe, $\Delta U=\nu C_V\Delta T$ for every process. Counting $f$ (table 10.2, page 296, also on the formula sheet):

SystemTranslationRotation$f$$C_V$$C_p$$\kappa$
one atom (He, Ar)303$\frac32R$$\frac52R$$\frac53$
two atoms (N₂, O₂, H₂, air)325$\frac52R$$\frac72R$$\frac75$
three or more atoms, not in a line (H₂O)336$3R$$4R$$\frac43$
solid3 kinetic + 3 potential6$3R$$3R$–

A point-like atom can only move (3 translations). A two-atom molecule can also tumble about two axes; spinning about its own axis doesn't count, because there is (almost) no moment of inertia about it. An atom in a solid is an oscillator in three directions, with kinetic and potential energy in each: $6\cdot\frac12kT$ per atom, which is the Dulong–Petit $3R$.

Frozen degrees of freedom (pages 296–297). The vibration of a two-atom molecule would add two more ($f=7$), but at room temperature it is “frozen”: quantum mechanics needs a minimum energy to excite it, much larger than $kT$. That is why air has $f=5$, and why exams say “die Vibrationsfreiheitsgrade sind nicht angeregt”.

Try it: when degrees of freedom wake up

The molar heat $C_V$ of hydrogen against temperature (logarithmic axis, schematic like the script's figure 10.10). Move the temperature and watch which motions are active.

Below about $50\,\mathrm K$ hydrogen behaves like a one-atom gas. Around room temperature it has its textbook $C_V=\frac52R$. Only at a few thousand kelvin does the vibration join in.

Try it: heating at constant volume or constant pressure

The same heat $Q$ goes into one mole of gas in two cylinders: the left one has a fixed lid, the right one a free piston. Press Go.

The free piston rises, so the right gas does work and warms less. The fraction of the heat that turns into work is $\frac R{C_p}=\frac2{f+2}$: $40\,\%$ for a one-atom gas, $29\,\%$ for air.

Why it's cool

A single number, the count of ways a molecule can move, predicts how much energy a gas stores, how hard it is to heat, and (next lesson) how hot it gets when you compress it. And when the prediction fails at low temperature, it is one of the first clues, around 1900, that the world is quantum.

Worked example 2: heating air two ways

Two moles of air (two-atom, $f=5$) are heated from $300\,\mathrm K$ to $400\,\mathrm K$, once at constant volume and once at constant pressure. Find $\Delta U$, $W^\swarrow$ and $Q^\swarrow$ both times. Use $R\approx8.3\,\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}$.

  1. Step 2 of the recipe: what is $\Delta U$?

    Answer

    $\Delta U=\nu C_V\Delta T$ $=2\cdot\frac52\cdot8.3\cdot100\,\mathrm J$ $=4.15\,\mathrm{kJ}$, the same in both cases, because $U$ depends only on $T$.

  2. Constant volume: $W^\swarrow$ and $Q^\swarrow$?

    Answer

    $dV=0$, so $W^\swarrow=0$ and $Q^\swarrow=\Delta U=4.15\,\mathrm{kJ}$.

  3. Constant pressure?

    Answer

    $W^\swarrow=-p\,\Delta V=-\nu R\Delta T$ $=-2\cdot8.3\cdot100\,\mathrm J$ $=-1.66\,\mathrm{kJ}$ (the gas does work). $Q^\swarrow=\Delta U-W^\swarrow$ $=4.15+1.66=5.81\,\mathrm{kJ}$, which is $\nu C_p\Delta T=2\cdot\frac72\cdot8.3\cdot100\,\mathrm J$. ✓

Result: isochoric: $\Delta U=Q^\swarrow=4.15\,\mathrm{kJ}$, $W^\swarrow=0$. Isobaric: $\Delta U=4.15\,\mathrm{kJ}$, $W^\swarrow=-1.66\,\mathrm{kJ}$, $Q^\swarrow=5.81\,\mathrm{kJ}$.

6. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed. Exercises 1–7 are multiple choice, 8 is the start of a long problem, 9 and 10 are practice problems in exam style (not from an exam).

Exercise 1 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.5(a), 1 point, original)

For an ideal gas, the specific heat $c_v$ is (A) always $\frac12R$ (B) $\frac32c_p$ (C) proportional to the number of degrees of freedom (D) proportional to the pressure.

Solution

C. $C_V=\frac f2R$. (A) is the share of one degree of freedom, not the total; (B) is impossible since $c_p>c_v$; the pressure does not appear at all.

Exercise 2 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.5(c), and again HS25 1.5(c), 1 point, original)

Which statement about the heat capacity of an ideal gas is true? (A) It is higher at constant volume than at constant pressure. (B) It depends on the number of degrees of freedom of the gas particles. (C) It says how much work is needed to change the internal energy. (D) It says how the internal energy changes when the entropy rises.

Solution

B. $C_V=\frac f2R$, $C_p=\frac{f+2}2R$. (A) is backwards ($C_p=C_V+R$). (C) and (D) confuse it with work and entropy: the heat capacity is heat per kelvin, $C=\frac{\delta Q}{dT}$. The identical question came twice in two years.

Exercise 3 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.5(a), 1 point, original)

Which statement holds for an ideal gas with a fixed number of particles? (A) Above a certain pressure it liquefies. (B) The kinetic energy of the particles always rises with the pressure. (C) The density rises if the pressure rises at constant temperature. (D) The attractive forces between the particles grow with temperature.

Solution

C. At constant $T$, $V=\frac{\nu RT}p$ shrinks as $p$ grows, so the density $\frac mV$ rises. (A) and (D) need attraction between molecules, which an ideal gas does not have (that is a real gas, lesson 16). (B): the kinetic energy is $\frac f2kT$, set by the temperature, not the pressure; you can raise $p$ by compressing at constant $T$.

Exercise 4 (FS23, Aufgabe 1.4(d), 2 points, original)

What internal energy does one mole of an ideal one-atom gas have at $T=100\,\mathrm K$? Use $R=8\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$. (A) $400\,\mathrm J$ (B) $800\,\mathrm J$ (C) $1200\,\mathrm J$ (D) $1600\,\mathrm J$

Solution

C. One atom: $f=3$, so $U=\frac32\nu RT$ $=\frac32\cdot1\cdot8\cdot100\,\mathrm J=1200\,\mathrm J$.

Exercise 5 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.5(b), 1 point, original)

The pressure of an ideal gas is raised at constant volume. What holds for the heat supplied $Q^\swarrow$ and the change of internal energy $\Delta U$? (A) $Q^\swarrow=0$, $\Delta U=0$ (B) $Q^\swarrow=0$, $\Delta U\neq0$ (C) $Q^\swarrow\neq0$, $\Delta U=0$ (D) $Q^\swarrow\neq0$, $\Delta U\neq0$

Solution

D. At constant $V$, $p\propto T$, so $T$ rises and $\Delta U=\nu C_V\Delta T\neq0$. No work is done ($dV=0$), so by the first law $Q^\swarrow=\Delta U\neq0$.

Exercise 6 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.5(b), 1 point, original)

The volume of an ideal gas is increased at constant pressure. What holds for $Q^\swarrow$ and $\Delta U$? (A) $Q^\swarrow=0$, $\Delta U=0$ (B) $Q^\swarrow=0$, $\Delta U\neq0$ (C) $Q^\swarrow\neq0$, $\Delta U=0$ (D) $Q^\swarrow\neq0$, $\Delta U\neq0$

Solution

D. At constant $p$, $V\propto T$, so $T$ rises: $\Delta U=\nu C_V\Delta T\neq0$. And $Q^\swarrow=\nu C_p\Delta T\neq0$ (it pays for $\Delta U$ and the work of expanding).

Exercise 7 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.5(b), 1 point, original)

Which statement about changes of state is correct? (A) The work done in an isochoric process depends on the number of degrees of freedom. (B) The internal energy can only rise if heat is supplied to the gas. (C) In an adiabatic process the product of temperature and volume is constant. (D) For the same temperature rise, a gas needs more heat in an isobaric process than in an isochoric one.

Solution

D. $C_p>C_V$. (A): isochoric work is zero. (B): work done on the gas also raises $U$ (compress a gas quickly and it warms). (C): the adiabatic law is $TV^{\kappa-1}=$ const, not $TV$ (lesson 15).

Exercise 8 (FS24, problem 5(a)–(b), “Wasserstoffballon”, 3 points, original)

A balloon holds $n=20\,\mathrm{mol}$ of hydrogen, always at the pressure and temperature of the air around it: $p_1=1\,\mathrm{bar}=10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$, $T_1=300\,\mathrm K$. Use $R\approx10\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$ and $k_B\approx10^{-4}\,\mathrm{eV/K}$. (a) Compute the volume $V_1$. (b) Hydrogen is a two-atom molecule. Which degrees of freedom does the gas have, and how many? Then compute the mean energy of motion of one hydrogen molecule in millielectronvolts. (The vibrations cannot be excited at room temperature.)

Solution

(a) $V_1=\frac{nRT_1}{p_1}=\frac{20\cdot10\cdot300}{10^5}\,\mathrm{m^3}=0.6\,\mathrm{m^3}$.

(b) Three translations and two rotations (about the two axes perpendicular to the bond; the rotation about the bond has no moment of inertia, and the vibration is frozen): $f=5$. By equipartition the mean energy per molecule is $\frac f2k_BT=\frac52\cdot10^{-4}\cdot300\,\mathrm{eV}$ $=0.075\,\mathrm{eV}=75\,\mathrm{meV}$. (Of this, the translational part is $\frac32k_BT=45\,\mathrm{meV}$.)

Exercise 9 (practice, a horseshoe in a bucket, 3 points)

An aluminium block of $270\,\mathrm g$ (molar mass $27\,\mathrm{g/mol}$) at $100\,^\circ\mathrm C$ is dropped into $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm C$ in an insulated bucket. Water: $c_m=4.19\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}$. (a) Estimate the heat capacity of the block with the Dulong–Petit rule. (b) Find the final temperature. (c) Why may you calculate here in degrees Celsius?

Solution

(a) $\nu=\frac{270}{27}=10\,\mathrm{mol}$, $C_{\text{Al}}=\nu\cdot3R\approx10\cdot25\,\mathrm{J/K}=250\,\mathrm{J/K}$.

(b) $C_W=1\cdot4190\,\mathrm{J/K}$. The heat lost by the block equals the heat gained by the water: $T_e=\frac{C_{\text{Al}}T_{\text{Al}}+C_WT_W}{C_{\text{Al}}+C_W}$ $=\frac{250\cdot100+4190\cdot20}{4440}\,^\circ\mathrm C$ $\approx24.5\,^\circ\mathrm C$.

(c) Only differences $T_e-T$ enter the heat balance; adding $273.15$ to every temperature gives the same result (the weights add up to one). Ratios of temperatures, as in $pV=\nu RT$, need kelvin.

Exercise 10 (practice, the first law with arrows, 3 points)

One mole of helium is compressed at a constant pressure of $1\,\mathrm{bar}$ from $30\,\mathrm L$ to $20\,\mathrm L$. (a) Find $W^\swarrow$. (b) Find the temperatures before and after, and $\Delta U$. (c) Find $Q^\swarrow$, and say in words where the energy goes.

Solution

(a) $W^\swarrow=-p\,\Delta V$ $=-10^5\cdot(-0.01)\,\mathrm J$ $=+1000\,\mathrm J$: work is done on the gas.

(b) $T=\frac{pV}{\nu R}$: $T_a=\frac{10^5\cdot0.03}{8.314}\,\mathrm K=361\,\mathrm K$, $T_e=\frac{10^5\cdot0.02}{8.314}\,\mathrm K=241\,\mathrm K$. Helium: $f=3$, so $\Delta U=\frac32\nu R\Delta T=\frac32\,\Delta(pV)$ $=\frac32\cdot(-1000)\,\mathrm J=-1500\,\mathrm J$.

(c) $Q^\swarrow=\Delta U-W^\swarrow$ $=-1500-1000=-2500\,\mathrm J$. The gas gives off $2500\,\mathrm J$ of heat: the $1000\,\mathrm J$ of work pushed into it plus $1500\,\mathrm J$ of its own internal energy. (Check: $\nu C_p\Delta T=\frac52\cdot(-1000)\,\mathrm J$. ✓) A compression at constant pressure only works if the gas is cooled.

Scoring yourself: the exercises make 17 points. With 12 you're done. If you can do Exercise 10 without looking, you have the first law and its signs, which every problem 5 needs.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

How cold is cold? Absolute zero cannot be reached, only approached (the third law, lesson 16). Laboratories get to a billionth of a kelvin, colder than anywhere in nature; the darkest corners of space are at $2.7\,\mathrm K$, warmed by the afterglow of the Big Bang.

Why the sea keeps the coast mild. Water's specific heat is about five times that of rock and sand. In summer the sea soaks up heat with little warming, and in winter gives it back slowly. That is why Lisbon has mild winters and Madrid, inland at a similar latitude, has cold ones.
