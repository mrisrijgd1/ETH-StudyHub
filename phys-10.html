Why it matters for the exam

Statics is the friendliest long problem of the exam: a barbell on two supports (HS25, 8 points), a crate on a ramp that may slide or tip (FS25, 9 points), the counterweight of a crane (HS23). The method is always the same two lines, $\sum\vec F=0$ and $\sum\vec M=0$, and points come almost for free once you choose a clever reference point. The centre-of-mass integral is a standard first part of the rigid-body problems (FS26, the swing).

Recap of lesson 9 (3 min)

  1. What condition gives the radius of a circular orbit?

    Answer

    Gravity provides the centripetal force: $\frac{GMm}{r^2}=m\omega^2r$.

  2. How do you see from the energy whether an orbit is closed?

    Answer

    $E=\frac12mv^2-\frac{GMm}r<0$: bound, an ellipse (or circle). $E=0$: parabola. $E>0$: hyperbola.

  3. In lesson 7 you met the torque. What is it, and what does it change?

    Answer

    $\vec M=\vec r\times\vec F$ about a point O; it changes the angular momentum about O: $\dot{\vec L}=\vec M$.

1. The story: the drawbridge

The master builder is repairing the drawbridge. Her apprentice drags a long oak beam onto two trestles, and a sack of nails on one end makes it rock. “Where do I push so it stays?” he asks. She lays a finger under the beam, slides it until the beam balances on it, and marks the spot with chalk. “Here sits all its weight. From now on you can forget it is a beam and pretend all of it hangs at this mark.”

Then she leans a ladder against the smooth castle wall and climbs. Halfway up she calls down: “Hold the foot! The higher I go, the harder the wall pushes the ladder out, and only the floor's grip holds it.” A crate on the ramp of the bridge raises the last question: when the ramp tilts, does the crate slide, or does it tip over first?

In physics: a rigid body is a system of many particles whose distances never change. Its translation is governed by the total force acting at the centre of mass, its rotation by the total torque. A body at rest stays at rest exactly when both vanish: that is static equilibrium. The chalk mark is the centre of mass; the ladder and the crate are the two classic exam problems.

2. The centre of mass of a rigid body

For $N$ point masses you know it from lesson 7: $\vec r_S=\frac{\sum m_i\vec r_i}{\sum m_i}$. In a rigid body the mass is spread out, so cut it into small volume elements $\Delta V_i$ with density $\rho_i$ and mass $\Delta m_i=\rho_i\,\Delta V_i$. In the limit the sum becomes an integral (script, page 184):

$$\vec r_S=\frac1M\int\vec r\,dm=\frac1M\int_V\vec r\,\rho\,dV,\qquad M=\int_V\rho\,dV.$$

Read it as a weighted average of the positions, with weight $dm$. Two habits make every such integral short:

  • Symmetry first. If the body is symmetric under a mirror or a rotation, the centre of mass lies on the mirror plane or the axis. Only the remaining coordinate needs an integral.
  • Cut the body into slices whose mass you know: discs of a cone, rings of a disc, strips of a rod. In cylindrical coordinates $(r,\varphi,z)$ the volume element is $dV=r\,dr\,d\varphi\,dz$.

Worked example 1: the cone (script, pages 184–186)

A solid circular cone with constant density, mass $M$, height $h$ and base radius $R$ stands on its base, the $z$-axis along its axis. Where is its centre of mass?

  1. Which coordinates follow from symmetry alone?

    Answer

    The cone is symmetric about the $z$-axis, so $x_S=y_S=0$. Only $z_S$ is left.

  2. Slice the cone into discs of thickness $dz$. What is the radius $r(z)$ of the disc at height $z$, and its volume $dV$?

    Answer

    The radius shrinks linearly from $R$ at $z=0$ to $0$ at $z=h$: $r(z)=\frac Rh(h-z)$. A disc has $dV=\pi r(z)^2\,dz$.

  3. Compute the volume and the density $\rho_0$.

    Answer

    $V=\int_0^h\pi\frac{R^2}{h^2}(h-z)^2\,dz$ $=\pi\frac{R^2}{h^2}\cdot\frac{h^3}3=\frac13\pi R^2h$, so $\rho_0=\frac MV=\frac{3M}{\pi R^2h}$.

  4. Now $z_S=\frac1M\int\rho_0\,z\,dV$. Evaluate it.

    Answer

    $z_S=\frac{\rho_0}M\pi\frac{R^2}{h^2}\int_0^h(h-z)^2z\,dz$ $=\frac3{h^3}\int_0^h(h-z)^2z\,dz$.

    The integral: $\int_0^h(h^2z-2hz^2+z^3)\,dz$ $=\frac{h^4}2-\frac{2h^4}3+\frac{h^4}4=\frac{h^4}{12}$.

    So $z_S=\frac3{h^3}\cdot\frac{h^4}{12}=\frac h4$: a quarter of the height above the base. Most of the mass sits low, in the wide part.

3. Forces on a rigid body: line of action and torque

On a point mass it doesn't matter where a force “acts”. On a rigid body it does: a push at the edge of a door turns it, a push at the hinge doesn't. But you may slide a force along its own line of action (the line through the point of application in the direction of $\vec F$) without changing anything (script, page 186). The reason: write the position of the point of application as $\vec r=\vec r_\parallel+\vec r_\perp$, parallel and perpendicular to $\vec F$. Then

$$\vec M=\vec r\times\vec F=\vec r_\perp\times\vec F,$$

because $\vec r_\parallel\times\vec F=0$. Every point on the line of action has the same $\vec r_\perp$, so the same torque. The length $|\vec r_\perp|$ is the lever arm: the distance from the reference point O to the line of action. That gives the working formula

$$M=F\cdot(\text{lever arm})=rF\sin\varphi.$$

Four facts from the script (pages 189–193) that the exam uses:

  1. The torque depends on the reference point. Moving the reference point changes $\vec r$, and with it $\vec M$: $\vec M_2=\vec M_1+\vec b\times\vec F$. So always say “torque about O”.
  2. Torques add. Several forces give $\vec M=\sum_i\vec r_i\times\vec F_i$.
  3. A couple (Kräftepaar) is two forces of equal size and opposite direction on different lines of action. Its total force is zero, so it doesn't accelerate the body; its torque $\vec M=\vec r_{12}\times\vec F_1$ is the same about every point. It only turns.
  4. Reduction of a system of forces. Any set of forces on a rigid body acts like one total force $\vec F_{\text{res}}=\sum\vec F_i$ at a chosen point plus one torque $\vec M_{\text{res}}=\sum\vec r_i\times\vec F_i$ about that point.

Applied to gravity in a homogeneous field, the torque about the centre of mass is $\sum\Delta m_i\,\vec r_i^{\,*}\times\vec g=\left(\sum\Delta m_i\vec r_i^{\,*}\right)\times\vec g=0$, because the positions $\vec r_i^{\,*}$ relative to S average to zero. So the whole weight $M\vec g$ may be drawn at the centre of mass (script, page 193). This is the real reason the chalk mark works.

Notation: the script and the formula sheet write the torque as $\vec M$, the same letter as a mass, so read from context. Many books use $\vec\tau$, as FS26 problem 4 does. The torque is an axial vector: under a point reflection $\vec r\to-\vec r$, $\vec F\to-\vec F$, it stays $+\vec M$ (pages 189–190). The exam never asks about it.

4. Static equilibrium

A rigid body at rest stays at rest exactly when (script, page 194)

$$\sum_i\vec F_i=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_i\vec M_i=0.$$

The first condition stops translation, the second rotation. If the forces add to zero, the torque condition holds about every point as soon as it holds about one (by fact 1 above, $\vec b\times\vec F_{\text{res}}=0$). That freedom is the main trick: choose the reference point where an unknown force acts, and that force drops out of the torque equation.

Recipe: a statics problem

  1. Draw every force at its point of application: weights at the centres of mass, normal forces perpendicular to the surfaces, friction along them, rope forces along the ropes.
  2. Force balance in two directions (horizontal and vertical, or along and perpendicular to a slope).
  3. Torque balance about a point where an unknown force acts. Count counterclockwise positive; each torque is force times lever arm.
  4. Solve and check the signs: a support can only push. A negative support force means the body lifts off there and tips.
  5. On the verge of tipping, the normal force sits at the edge it tips over; on the verge of sliding, static friction is at its maximum $\mu_hN$.

Try it: the barbell on two supports

A bar of mass $m_H$ (here $m_H=1$) lies on supports at $\frac27L$ and $\frac57L$; weights $m_1$ at $\frac17L$ and $m_2$ at $\frac67L$. This is HS25 problem 3. The arrows show the support forces.

Make $m_1$ much larger than $m_2$: the right support force drops to zero, and the bar tips about the left support. With $m_H=\frac12m_1$ that happens when $m_1>16\,m_2$ (Exercise 4 (c)).

Worked example 2: the ladder

A ladder (length $\ell$, mass $m$) leans against a smooth wall at the angle $\theta$ to the floor; the floor is rough. A person of mass $M_P$ stands on it at distance $s$ from the foot. Find the forces, and see what happens as the person climbs. (This is FS26 Konzeptfrage 1.3(a), worked out.)

  1. Which forces act, and where?

    Answer

    The weights $mg$ (at the middle, $\frac\ell2$ along the ladder) and $M_Pg$ (at $s$); at the wall only a horizontal normal force $N_W$ (smooth wall, no friction); at the floor a vertical normal force $N_B$ and a horizontal static friction $F_R$ pointing toward the wall.

  2. Write the force balance.

    Answer

    Vertical: $N_B=(m+M_P)g$. Horizontal: $F_R=N_W$.

  3. About which point should you take torques, and what do you get?

    Answer

    About the foot: $N_B$ and $F_R$ act there and drop out. The lever arm of a vertical force at distance $d$ along the ladder is $d\cos\theta$; that of the horizontal wall force at the top is $\ell\sin\theta$. So

    $N_W\,\ell\sin\theta$ $=mg\,\frac\ell2\cos\theta+M_Pg\,s\cos\theta$, hence $N_W=\frac{g}{\tan\theta}\left(\frac m2+M_P\frac s\ell\right)$.

  4. What happens as the person climbs (larger $s$)? When does the ladder slip?

    Answer

    $N_W$ grows linearly with $s$, and with it the friction $F_R=N_W$ that the floor must supply, while $N_B$ stays the same. The ladder slips once $F_R$ would exceed $\mu_hN_B=\mu_h(m+M_P)g$. That's why ladders slip when you are near the top, and why a steeper ladder (larger $\tan\theta$) is safer.

5. Sliding or tipping?

A box of length $L$ (along the slope) and height $h$ rests on a slope with angle $\alpha$. Two things can go wrong.

  • Sliding: along the slope, gravity pulls with $mg\sin\alpha$, and static friction can hold at most $\mu_hmg\cos\alpha$. It slides when $\tan\alpha>\mu_h$, whatever its shape.
  • Tipping: on the verge of tipping, the normal force and the friction act at the lower edge Q only, so they have no torque about Q. The weight acts at the centre of mass; it turns the box back as long as the vertical through the centre of mass hits the base, that is, lies uphill of Q. It tips when that vertical passes Q: $\tan\alpha>\frac Lh$.

Whichever limit is smaller wins. A tall, narrow box ($\frac Lh<\mu_h$) tips first; a flat one slides first. The script's example (Tipler 12.14, pages 194–196) adds a rope that holds the block; the method is the same.

Try it: slide or tip

Tilt the slope. The dashed line is the vertical through the centre of mass; Q is the lower edge of the box.

Set $\frac Lh=0.5$ and $\mu_h=0.8$: the box tips at $\alpha\approx27^\circ$, long before it would slide at $39^\circ$.

Worked example 3: the crate pulled up the ramp

The crate from the slide-or-tip box, mass $m$, is pulled up the slope by a rope parallel to the slope, attached at half height $\frac h2$ on the uphill side. P is the uphill lower edge. How hard may you pull before the crate tips forward over P? (This is the core of FS25 problem 3 (d).)

  1. Which point do you take torques about, and which forces drop out?

    Answer

    About P. On the verge of tipping over P the normal force and friction act at P, so they have no torque there.

  2. Which torques remain? Split the weight along and perpendicular to the slope.

    Answer

    The rope force $F_S$ along the slope at height $\frac h2$ above P: lever arm $\frac h2$, tipping the crate forward.

    The weight at the centre of mass, which is $\frac L2$ downhill of P and $\frac h2$ above the slope. The perpendicular part $mg\cos\alpha$ has lever arm $\frac L2$; the downhill part $mg\sin\alpha$ has lever arm $\frac h2$. Both turn the crate back.

  3. Write the condition.

    Answer

    No tipping as long as $F_S\frac h2\le mg\cos\alpha\frac L2+mg\sin\alpha\frac h2$, that is

    $$F_S\le mg\left(\sin\alpha+\frac Lh\cos\alpha\right).$$

Why it's cool. The tipping rule “the vertical through the centre of mass must hit the base” is why the Leaning Tower of Pisa still stands (its top overhangs by about 4 m, but the vertical through its centre of mass still lands inside the base), why a racing car is built low and wide, and why you lean forward when you carry a heavy rucksack. Every statue, crane and tightrope walker obeys the same two lines, $\sum\vec F=0$ and $\sum\vec M=0$.

6. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed.

Exercise 1 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.1(e), 1 point, original)

A massless rod of length $l$ carries a mass $m$ at its left end and a mass $3m$ at its right end. Where must it be supported to balance? (A) In the middle. (B) $\frac l4$ left of the middle. (C) $\frac l4$ right of the middle. (D) $\frac l2$ right of the middle.

Solution

C. It balances under its centre of mass: from the left end, $x_S=\frac{m\cdot0+3m\cdot l}{4m}=\frac34l$, which is $\frac l4$ right of the middle. Check with torques about that point: $mg\cdot\frac34l=3mg\cdot\frac14l$.

Exercise 2 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.3(a), 1 point, original)

You climb a ladder that leans against a smooth (frictionless) vertical wall and stands on a rough horizontal floor. How do the normal force of the wall on the ladder, $F_{N,\text{Wand}}$, and the static friction needed at the floor, $F_R$, change as you climb higher? (A) Both stay constant, since the total weight doesn't change. (B) $F_{N,\text{Wand}}$ grows, $F_R$ stays constant. (C) $F_{N,\text{Wand}}$ decreases and $F_R$ grows. (D) Both grow.

Solution

D. Worked example 2: torques about the foot give $N_W=\frac g{\tan\theta}\left(\frac m2+M_P\frac s\ell\right)$, growing with $s$, and the horizontal balance gives $F_R=N_W$.

Exercise 3 (HS23, problem 3 “Kran”, part (a), 1 point, original)

A crane is modelled as a horizontal homogeneous rod of length $L$ and mass $m_K$, turning about a vertical axis at $\frac L4$ from one end. At the short end sits a concrete block of mass $M$ (a point mass) as counterweight. How large must $M$ be compared with $m_K$ so that no torque acts on the crane's foundation?

Solution

The rod's weight acts at its centre, $\frac L2$ from the end, which is $\frac L2-\frac L4=\frac L4$ from the axis on the long side. The block sits $\frac L4$ from the axis on the short side. Torques about the axis: $Mg\frac L4=m_Kg\frac L4$, so $M=m_K$.

Exercise 4 (HS25, problem 3 “Langhantel”, parts (a)–(e), 8 points, original)

A barbell bar of mass $m_H$ and length $L$ lies on two supports at $x=\frac27L$ and $x=\frac57L$. Point masses $m_1$ at $x=\frac17L$ and $m_2$ at $x=\frac67L$ are attached (the “Try it” above). (a) Compute the centre of mass of the system in terms of $m_1$, $m_2$, $m_H$. [1 point] (b) Compute the forces $F_1$ and $F_2$ on the two supports. [2 points] (c) Let $m_H=\frac{m_1}2$. For which ratios of $m_1$ and $m_2$ does the bar start to tip to the left? [2 points] (d) Now reduce $m_1$. For which $m_H$, as a function of $m_1$, can the bar never tip to the left, however large $m_2$ is? [1 point] (e) The bar is now slowly tipping to the left, rotating about the left support. Derive the angle $\varphi_{\text{krit}}$ at which $m_1$ starts to slide to the left; the coefficient of static friction between $m_1$ and the bar is $\mu$. [2 points]

Solution

(a) $x_S=\dfrac{m_1\frac L7+m_H\frac L2+m_2\frac{6L}7}{m_1+m_H+m_2}$ $=\dfrac{L\,(2m_1+7m_H+12m_2)}{14\,(m_1+m_H+m_2)}$.

(b) Torques about support 1 at $\frac27L$ (counterclockwise positive), so $F_1$ drops out. Lever arms: $m_1$ at $\frac L7$ to the left, $m_H$ at $\frac12L-\frac27L=\frac3{14}L$ to the right, $m_2$ at $\frac47L$ to the right, $F_2$ at $\frac37L$ to the right:

$$F_2\cdot\tfrac37L=-m_1g\tfrac L7+m_Hg\tfrac3{14}L+m_2g\tfrac47L,$$ $$F_2=\frac g6\left(-2m_1+3m_H+8m_2\right).$$

Force balance: $F_1=(m_1+m_H+m_2)g-F_2$ $=\frac g6\left(8m_1+3m_H-2m_2\right)$. (Check: swapping $m_1\leftrightarrow m_2$ swaps $F_1\leftrightarrow F_2$, as the symmetry demands.)

(c) The bar tips to the left (about support 1) when the right support would have to pull: $F_2<0$. With $m_H=\frac{m_1}2$: $-2m_1+\frac32m_1+8m_2<0$ $\iff m_1>16\,m_2$, so $\frac{m_1}{m_2}>16$.

(d) Never tipping left, even for $m_2=0$: $F_2\ge0$ needs $-2m_1+3m_H\ge0$, so $m_H\ge\frac23m_1$.

(e) $m_1$ now sits on the bar as on an inclined plane with angle $\varphi$: downhill force $m_1g\sin\varphi$, maximal static friction $\mu m_1g\cos\varphi$. It starts to slide when $\tan\varphi>\mu$, so $\varphi_{\text{krit}}=\arctan\mu$.

Exercise 5 (FS25, problem 3 “Kiste”, parts (a)–(f), 9 points, original)

A crate of mass $m$, length $L$ (along the ramp) and height $h$ sits on a ramp with angle $\alpha$. A rope parallel to the ramp is attached at half height on the uphill side and can pull it up the ramp. P is the lower uphill edge, Q the lower downhill edge. Static and kinetic friction coefficients $\mu_h>\mu_g$ are known. First the rope is slack. (a) Assume static friction holds the crate. How steep may the ramp be so that the crate doesn't tip over? [2 points] (b) Now assume it doesn't tip. How steep may the ramp be so that it doesn't start to slide? [1 point] Assume (a) and (b) hold, and pull on the rope. (c) How large must $F_S$ be at least to set the crate moving? [2 points] (d) How large may $F_S$ be at most so that the crate doesn't tip over P while you try to set it moving? [2 points] (e) What $F_S$ keeps it moving at constant velocity? [1 point] (f) The crate moves up at $v_0$ when the rope snaps. How long does it keep sliding up before it stops? [1 point]

Solution

(a) Tipping over Q (section 5): the vertical through the centre of mass must stay uphill of Q, so $\tan\alpha\le\frac Lh$, $\alpha_{\max}=\arctan\frac Lh$.

(b) $mg\sin\alpha\le\mu_hmg\cos\alpha$, so $\tan\alpha\le\mu_h$.

(c) Moving up, static friction points down the ramp at its maximum: $F_S\ge mg\sin\alpha+\mu_hmg\cos\alpha$.

(d) Worked example 3: torques about P give $F_S\le mg\left(\sin\alpha+\frac Lh\cos\alpha\right)$.

(e) Constant velocity, kinetic friction: $F_S=mg\left(\sin\alpha+\mu_g\cos\alpha\right)$.

(f) Without the rope, gravity and kinetic friction both point down the ramp: deceleration $a=g(\sin\alpha+\mu_g\cos\alpha)$, so $t=\dfrac{v_0}{g(\sin\alpha+\mu_g\cos\alpha)}$.

Exercise 6 (FS26, problem 4 “Die Schaukel”, parts (a) and (c), 3 points, original)

The seat of a swing is a homogeneous half-cylinder (mass $M$, radius $R$, length $H$, density $\rho$), its flat base in the $xz$-plane, the curved side on the $+y$ side, the axis along $z$. (a) Compute $M$ in terms of $\rho$, $R$, $H$. [1 point] (c) Compute the distance $y_S$ of the centre of mass from the flat base as a function of $R$, using $y_S=\frac1M\int_V\rho\,y\,dV$ in cylindrical coordinates with $y=r\sin\varphi$. [2 points]

Solution

(a) Half a cylinder: $M=\rho\,\frac12\pi R^2H$.

(c) With $dV=r\,dr\,d\varphi\,dz$, $0\le r\le R$, $0\le\varphi\le\pi$, $0\le z\le H$:

$$y_S=\frac\rho M\int_0^H\!\!\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_0^R r\sin\varphi\;r\,dr\,d\varphi\,dz=\frac\rho M\cdot H\cdot2\cdot\frac{R^3}3.$$

Insert $M$: $y_S=\dfrac{\frac23\rho HR^3}{\frac12\rho\pi R^2H}=\dfrac{4R}{3\pi}\approx0.42\,R$.

The other parts (the moment of inertia and the swinging) come in lessons 11 and 12.

Scoring yourself: the exercises make 23 points. With 15 you're done. If Exercise 4 (b) works without looking, you have the torque trick.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

The stack that leans past its edge. Stack identical bricks of length 1 at the edge of a table, each shifted as far as possible over the one below. The top brick may overhang by $\frac12$, the next by $\frac14$, then $\frac16$, $\frac18$, … : each time the centre of mass of everything above must sit exactly above the edge below. The total overhang is $\frac12\left(1+\frac12+\frac13+\cdots\right)$, half the harmonic series from Analysis, which diverges. So with enough bricks the top one hangs as far beyond the table as you like: with 4 bricks it already clears the table entirely ($\frac{25}{24}>1$). Statics and a divergent series in one picture.
