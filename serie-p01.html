Units, reading a $v$–$t$ diagram, braking with constant deceleration, and dimensional analysis. For every task: the idea in plain words, a sketch, up to three hints that each give away a little more, then the solution with every step explained next to what you write, and the trap people fall into.

How to use this page.

  2. Stuck on where to start? Read The idea and open You need.

  4. Only then open the solution. The left column says why each step is there, the right column is what you write.

Aufgabe 1 · Quantities and units

Convert to SI units: a) the TGV record speed, $574\,$km/h; b) yearly energies of $180\,$kWh (1 m² of solar panel), $35\,$kWh (coffee machine), $100\,$kWh (fridge), $200\,$kWh (dishwasher); c) the distance to Proxima Centauri, $4.2$ light years. Give the usual SI unit and its form in kg, m, s for d) energy and e) force.

The idea

Converting units is multiplying by $1$. Since $1\,\text h=3600\,\text s$, the fraction $\frac{3600\,\text s}{1\,\text h}$ equals $1$, so you may multiply by it as often as you like. Write every non-SI unit as a product of SI units, and the units cancel like numbers. For d) and e), start from a formula you know (force $=$ mass $\times$ acceleration, energy $=$ force $\times$ distance) and read off the units.

You need

$1\,\text h=3600\,\text s$, $1\,\text{km}=10^3\,\text m$, $1\,\text W=1\,\text J/\text s$, “kilo” $=10^3$. A light year is a distance: how far light travels in one year, with $c\approx2.998\cdot10^8\,$m/s and $1\,\text y\approx365.25\,$days. Units of formulas: Physics lesson 1.

Hint 1

a) Replace “km” by $10^3\,\text m$ and “h” by $3600\,\text s$, then simplify. b) “kWh” is kilowatt times hour, so it is power × time, which is energy. Replace kilo, watt and hour one by one.

Hint 2

b) $$\begin{aligned}1\,\text{kWh}&=10^3\,\text W\cdot3600\,\text s\\&=3.6\cdot10^6\,\text J.\end{aligned}$$ Do this once, then multiply by 35, 100, 180, 200. c) Distance $=$ speed × time: $4.2\,\text{ly}=c\cdot(4.2\text{ years in seconds})$.

Hint 3

e) $F=ma$ gives $[F]=\text{kg}\cdot\frac{\text m}{\text s^2}$. d) Work $=$ force × distance gives $[E]=\text N\cdot\text m$. Then write N in kg, m, s.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Swap each unit for its SI value. Dividing by $3.6$ is the km/h → m/s rule to remember.

$$\begin{aligned}574\,\frac{\text{km}}{\text h}&=574\cdot\frac{10^3\,\text m}{3600\,\text s}\\&=\frac{574}{3.6}\,\frac{\text m}{\text s}\approx160\,\frac{\text m}{\text s}.\end{aligned}$$

b) Convert one kWh once; the rest is multiplying. A watt-second is a joule.

$$\begin{aligned}1\,\text{kWh}&=10^3\,\text W\cdot3.6\cdot10^3\,\text s\\&=3.6\cdot10^6\,\text J\\&=3.6\cdot10^6\,\frac{\text{kg m}^2}{\text s^2}.\end{aligned}$$ Coffee machine $35\,\text{kWh}=1.26\cdot10^8\,\text J$; fridge $100\,\text{kWh}=3.6\cdot10^8\,\text J$; solar panel $180\,\text{kWh}=6.48\cdot10^8\,\text J$; dishwasher $200\,\text{kWh}=7.2\cdot10^8\,\text J$.

c) A light year is speed of light × one year. Write the year in seconds step by step so nothing gets lost.

$$\begin{aligned}4.2\,\text{ly}&=4.2\cdot(365.25\cdot24\cdot3600\,\text s)\\&\quad\cdot2.998\cdot10^8\,\tfrac{\text m}{\text s}\\&\approx4.0\cdot10^{16}\,\text m.\end{aligned}$$

d) Energy has three common names for the same unit: newton-metre, watt-second, joule.

$$\text J=\text{N m}=\text{W s}=\frac{\text{kg m}^2}{\text s^2}.$$

e) From $F=ma$.

$$\text N=\frac{\text{kg m}}{\text s^2}.$$

Sense check. One square metre of solar panel ($180\,$kWh a year) more than covers a fridge ($100\,$kWh), but not a dishwasher ($200\,$kWh). Comparing numbers like this is how you catch a wrong power of ten.

Trap

Multiplying by $3.6$ instead of dividing (km/h → m/s makes the number smaller: $574\,$km/h is not $2066\,$m/s). Also: kWh is an energy, not a power, and a light year is a distance, not a time.

Aufgabe 2 · Laser cutting (reading a $v$–$t$ diagram)

A laser cuts a part out of a square aluminium sheet. The beam moves with a constant speed along the path in the sketch on the sheet: from “Start” straight up, around three quarters of a circle, then straight right to “Ende”. Which of the four $v_x(t)$ diagrams a)–d) on the sheet fits the $x$-direction of the motion? Say briefly why each excluded diagram is wrong.

The idea

$v_x$ is the part of the velocity that points along $x$, like the shadow of the velocity arrow on the $x$-axis. The arrow always has the same length (constant speed), but it turns. On a vertical cut its shadow is $0$; on a horizontal cut to the right it is the full speed $+v$; on the circle it swings between $-v$ and $+v$. Walk along the path, note the sign of $v_x$ at a few points, and pick the graph that does the same.

Constant speed, turning arrow: the shadow on the $x$-axis is $0$ on the way up, jumps to $-v$ at the bottom of the circle, rises to $+v$ at the top, falls to $0$ on the right side, then jumps to $+v$ for the last cut.

You need

Velocity is a vector; $v_x$ is its $x$-component. For a vector of length $v$ at angle $\varphi$ to the $x$-axis, $v_x=v\cos\varphi$: full length when it points along $x$, zero when it points along $y$ (Physics lesson 1).

Hint 1

Cut the path into three pieces: the straight cut up, the circle, the straight cut right. What is $v_x$ on each straight piece? That already fixes the start and the end of the graph.

Hint 2

Follow the arrow on the circle. The laser enters at the bottom and goes round via the left side, the top, and the right side. At the bottom of a circle, which way does it move if it is heading for the left side next? At the top?

Hint 3

Sequence of $v_x$: $0$ (straight up) → jump to $-v$ (bottom of the circle, moving left) → $0$ (left side, moving up) → $+v$ (top, moving right) → $0$ (right side, moving down) → jump to $+v$ (straight right). Which diagram starts at $0$, goes negative first and ends at the maximum?

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

First piece: straight up, so the velocity has no $x$-part.

First cut vertical: $v_x=0$.

Circle, followed in the direction of the arrow: at the bottom it heads left, at the top right.

On the circle: $v_x$ jumps to $-v$ at the bottom, passes $0$ on the left side, reaches $+v$ at the top, and comes back to $0$ on the right side.

Last piece: straight right at full speed.

Last cut horizontal, in $+x$: $v_x=+v$ (the maximum).

Now compare and give one clear reason per wrong diagram.

Diagram b) is correct.
c) is wrong: it starts at a nonzero $v_x$, but the first cut is vertical, so $v_x(0)=0$.
d) is wrong: it ends at $v_x=0$, but the last cut is in the $x$-direction, so it must end at the maximal $v_x$.
a) is wrong: after the first straight cut the laser moves in the negative $x$-direction, but a) goes positive first.

Trap

Reading the diagram as a picture of the path. A $v_x(t)$ graph is not the shape of the cut and not $x(t)$: it is the $x$-velocity over time. A second trap is going round the circle the wrong way. Follow the arrow on the sheet, and you'll find that the first motion on the circle is to the left.

Aufgabe 3 · Braking at the zebra crossing

A car drives at $50\,$km/h towards a zebra crossing. The driver brakes with constant negative acceleration and stops after $4\,$s. a) Starting from a constant acceleration $a_0$, derive $v(t)$ and then $x(t)$ by integration (formulas first, no numbers). b) Find the magnitude of the braking acceleration in SI units. c) How many metres before the crossing must the driver start braking?

The idea

Constant acceleration means the velocity changes by the same amount every second, so $v(t)$ is a straight line. Integrate once more and the position is a parabola. “Comes to a stop after $4\,$s” is a condition, $v(4\,\text s)=0$, and that one condition fixes $a_0$. The braking distance is then $x(4\,\text s)$, or simply the area under the $v$–$t$ line.

The slope of the $v$–$t$ line is the acceleration; the area under it is the distance travelled.

You need

$v(t)=v_0+\int_0^t a(t')\,dt'$ and $x(t)=x_0+\int_0^t v(t')\,dt'$: integrating undoes the derivative, and the integration constant is the starting value (Physics lesson 1). Convert $50\,$km/h to m/s first.

Hint 1

a) Integrate the constant $a_0$ from $0$ to $t$: what do you get? Add the starting velocity $v_0$. Then integrate $v(t)$ in the same way and add $x_0$.

Hint 2

b) “Stops after $4\,$s” means $v(4\,\text s)=0$. Put that into $v(t)=a_0t+v_0$ and solve for $a_0$. Use $v_0=50/3.6\,$m/s $\approx13.9\,$m/s.

Hint 3

c) Put the origin where braking starts, so $x_0=0$. Then the distance is $x(4\,\text s)=\frac12a_0(4\,\text s)^2+v_0\cdot4\,\text s$ with the negative $a_0$ from b).

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Velocity is the integral of acceleration. A constant integrates to constant × time; the starting value $v_0$ is the integration constant.

$$v(t)-v_0=\int_0^t a_0\,dt'=a_0t,$$ so $v(t)=a_0t+v_0$.

Position is the integral of velocity. Same idea, one level up.

$$\begin{aligned}x(t)-x_0&=\int_0^t(a_0t'+v_0)\,dt'\\&=\tfrac12a_0t^2+v_0t,\end{aligned}$$ so $x(t)=\tfrac12a_0t^2+v_0t+x_0$.

b) Turn “stops after $4\,$s” into an equation, then solve for $a_0$.

$v(t)=0$ at $t=4\,$s: $0=a_0t+v_0$, so $$\begin{aligned}|a_0|&=\frac{v_0}{t}=\frac{13.89\,\text{m/s}}{4\,\text s}\\&=3.47\,\frac{\text m}{\text s^2}\approx3\,\frac{\text m}{\text s^2}.\end{aligned}$$ ($v_0=50/3.6\,$m/s.)

The official solution rounds to one significant figure because the least precise datum, “4 s”, has one.

(Rounded to the precision of the least precise datum, $4\,$s.)

c) Choose the origin at the point where braking starts. Then the distance is just $x$ at the moment the car stops. Keep the minus sign of $a_0$.

With $x_0=0$ and $a_0=-\frac{v_0}{4\,\text s}$: $$\begin{aligned}x(4\,\text s)&=-\tfrac12\cdot\frac{13.89\,\text{m/s}}{4\,\text s}\cdot16\,\text s^2\\&\quad+13.89\,\tfrac{\text m}{\text s}\cdot4\,\text s\\&=27.8\,\text m\approx30\,\text m.\end{aligned}$$

Another way: the area under the $v$–$t$ line

The distance is the area under $v(t)$. Here that is a triangle with height $v_0$ and width $4\,$s: $$\begin{aligned}x&=\tfrac12\,v_0\,t\\&=\tfrac12\cdot13.89\,\tfrac{\text m}{\text s}\cdot4\,\text s=27.8\,\text m.\end{aligned}$$ In words: with constant braking, the average speed is half the starting speed. The official solution notes that you could also set $x(t)=0$ at the crossing and solve for $x_0$: that gives the same distance.

Trap

Using $a_0=+3.47\,$m/s² in $x(t)$ (forgetting that braking means negative acceleration). That gives $83\,$m instead of $28\,$m. Quick check: the car can't travel further than if it kept its full speed for $4\,$s, which is $55.6\,$m. Second trap: putting $50$ (km/h) into a formula together with seconds.

Aufgabe 4 · Dimensional analysis rescues you from formula salad

Oscillations obey the differential equation $$m\,\ddot x+q\,\dot x+D\,x=0,$$ and one solution is the damped oscillation $$x(t)=x_0\,e^{-\delta t}\cos\!\Big(\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\delta^2}\;t+\alpha\Big).$$ You don't need to understand the equation yet: the point is to handle an unknown formula with dimensional analysis and a few tricks.

  1. $x$ is the position of a particle of mass $m$. Units of $x$, $\dot x$, $\ddot x$? Which units must $q$ and $D$ have?
  2. Which units must $x_0$, $\omega_0$, $\delta$, $\alpha$ have? Explain.
  3. Compute $\dot x$ and $\ddot x$, insert them into the equation and bring it to the form $A\cos(\omega t+\alpha)+B\sin(\omega t+\alpha)=0$. Find $A$ and $B$.
  4. Why must both $A=0$ and $B=0$? (A form of comparing coefficients.)
  5. Find $\omega_0$ and $\delta$ in terms of $m$, $q$, $D$.
The idea

Three rules do almost everything. (1) You can only add things with the same unit, so every term of the equation has the unit of $m\ddot x$, a force. (2) Whatever sits inside $e^{\dots}$ or $\cos(\dots)$ must have no unit at all. (3) If $A\cos(\cdot)+B\sin(\cdot)$ is zero at every moment, both numbers $A$ and $B$ must be zero, because cos and sin can't keep cancelling each other. The rest is careful differentiation.

What the solution looks like: $\delta$ (unit 1/s) sets how fast the swings shrink, $\omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\delta^2}$ (unit 1/s) how fast they repeat. You will meet this properly in Physics lesson 12.

You need

Units: $[x]=\text m$; each time derivative divides by s. Exponents and arguments of cos/sin are unitless. Derivatives: product rule and chain rule, $\frac{d}{dt}e^{-\delta t}=-\delta e^{-\delta t}$, $\frac{d}{dt}\cos(\omega t+\alpha)=-\omega\sin(\omega t+\alpha)$. Rupp writes $D$ for the spring constant.

Hint 1

a) Find the unit of the first term $m\ddot x$. Each of the other two terms must have exactly that unit; solve for $[q]$ and $[D]$. b) $e^{-\delta t}$ and $\cos(\dots)$ are pure numbers, so $x_0$ carries the whole unit of $x$. What must $[\delta]$ be so that $\delta t$ has no unit?

Hint 2

c) Write $\omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\delta^2}$ to keep it short. $\dot x$ has two terms (product rule). $\ddot x$ has four terms; two of them are equal sin-terms, so it collapses to one cos-term and one sin-term. Every term contains $x_0e^{-\delta t}$: cancel it.

Hint 3

d) The equation must hold for all $t$. Choose $t$ so that $\omega t+\alpha=0$: what's left? Then choose $\omega t+\alpha=\frac\pi2$. e) $B=(2m\delta-q)\,\omega=0$ gives $\delta$ straight away. Put it into $A=0$: the $\delta$-terms cancel.

Solution

a), b) Units.

Why this stepWhat you write

Position is a length; each derivative with respect to time divides by seconds. The first term is mass × acceleration, a force.

$[x]=\text m$, $[\dot x]=\frac{\text m}{\text s}$, $[\ddot x]=\frac{\text m}{\text s^2}$, and $\big[m\ddot x\big]=\frac{\text{kg m}}{\text s^2}$.

All summands must have the same unit as the first one. Solve for the unknown units.

$[q]\,\frac{\text m}{\text s}=\frac{\text{kg m}}{\text s^2}\Rightarrow[q]=\frac{\text{kg}}{\text s}$; $\quad[D]\,\text m=\frac{\text{kg m}}{\text s^2}\Rightarrow[D]=\frac{\text{kg}}{\text s^2}$.

b) Exponential and cosine are pure numbers, so the amplitude carries the metres.

$[x_0]=\text m$.

Exponents must be unitless, and so must the argument of cos and each summand in it. A square root only adds things of the same unit, so $\omega_0$ matches $\delta$.

$[\delta t]=1\Rightarrow[\delta]=\frac1{\text s}$; $\quad[\omega_0]=[\delta]=\frac1{\text s}$; $\quad[\alpha]=1$.

c) Insert the solution.

Short names, so the formulas stay readable.

Let $\omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\delta^2}$ and $\varphi=\omega t+\alpha$ (so $\dot\varphi=\omega$). Then $x=x_0e^{-\delta t}\cos\varphi$.

Product rule: differentiate the exponential, then the cosine.

$$\dot x=-x_0e^{-\delta t}\big(\delta\cos\varphi+\omega\sin\varphi\big).$$

Product rule on both terms gives four terms; the two sin-terms are equal and add up.

$$\begin{aligned}\ddot x=x_0e^{-\delta t}\big(&(\delta^2-\omega^2)\cos\varphi\\&+2\omega\delta\sin\varphi\big).\end{aligned}$$

Insert into $m\ddot x+q\dot x+Dx=0$, cancel the common factor $x_0e^{-\delta t}$ (never zero), and sort by cos and sin.

$$\begin{aligned}0&=\big[m(\delta^2-\omega^2)-q\delta+D\big]\cos\varphi\\&\quad+\big[2m\omega\delta-q\omega\big]\sin\varphi.\end{aligned}$$

Read off the brackets. Replace $\omega^2$ by $\omega_0^2-\delta^2$ in $A$.

$$\begin{aligned}A&=m(2\delta^2-\omega_0^2)-q\delta+D,\\B&=(2m\delta-q)\,\omega.\end{aligned}$$

d) Why both are zero.

The equation holds at every time. Pick two clever moments: one where sin vanishes, one where cos vanishes.

The equation holds for all $t$. At $t=-\frac\alpha\omega$ we have $\omega t+\alpha=0$, so $\sin=0$, $\cos=1$ and the equation reads $A=0$. At $\omega t+\alpha=\frac\pi2$ it reads $B=0$.

e) Solve for $\delta$ and $\omega_0$.

$B=0$ and $\omega\neq0$ (otherwise there is no oscillation), so the bracket is zero.

$0=(2m\delta-q)\,\omega\ \Rightarrow\ \delta=\dfrac{q}{2m}$.

Put it into $A=0$. Since $2m\delta=q$, the term $2m\delta^2$ equals $q\delta$ and cancels.

$$\begin{aligned}0&=2m\delta^2-m\omega_0^2-q\delta+D\\&=q\delta-m\omega_0^2-q\delta+D,\end{aligned}$$ so $m\omega_0^2=D$ and $\omega_0=\sqrt{\dfrac Dm}$.

Check the units against b): both come out as 1/s.

$\big[\frac q{2m}\big]=\frac{\text{kg/s}}{\text{kg}}=\frac1{\text s}$, $\ \big[\sqrt{D/m}\big]=\sqrt{\frac{\text{kg/s}^2}{\text{kg}}}=\frac1{\text s}$. ✓

Note (from the official solution). Strictly, $-\omega_0$ also solves the equations, but a different choice of $x_0$ and $\alpha$ then gives the same function, so nothing new comes out.

Trap

Forgetting the product rule: differentiating only the cosine and dropping the $-\delta$ from $e^{-\delta t}$. Then the $\sin$-terms come out wrong and $\delta$ can't be found. Also in d): “$A$ and $B$ are zero because sin and cos are different functions” is too vague. Say that the equation holds for all $t$, and give the two times.
