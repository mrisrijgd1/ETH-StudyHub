Serie 4, explained

Friction on the inclined plane, the water-shy tiger on the ice floe, a motorbike on a bend, a wall and a loop, and spring scales with ropes. For every task: the idea in plain words, a sketch, up to three hints that each give away a little more, then the solution with every step explained next to what you write, and the trap people fall into.

How to use this page.

  1. Read the task and try it on paper for 10 minutes. Draw a sketch first: in physics it is half the work.
  2. Stuck on where to start? Read The idea and open You need.
  3. Still stuck? Open Hint 1, try again, then Hint 2, then Hint 3. Hint 3 is almost the whole solution.
  4. Only then open the solution. The left column says why each step is there, the right column is what you write.

The solutions follow the official Lösung (Serie 4, Physik I HS26). The task texts are short English summaries: keep the official sheet next to you for the exact wording and the drawings in Aufgabe 4. With the StudyHub connector, your own AI can check your attempt before you look.

Aufgabe 1 · The inclined plane (lecture experiment)

A block sits on a plane whose angle $\varphi$ can be increased slowly.

  1. Sketch a block of mass $m$ that sticks (static friction) on the plane. Split its weight $F_G$ into the component parallel to the plane ($F_\parallel$) and perpendicular to it ($F_\perp$). Show that at the angle $\varphi_{\text{H}}$ where the block only just sticks, $\mu_0=\tan\varphi_{\text{H}}$.
  2. The block starts sliding at height $h=45\,$cm and horizontal base $l=75\,$cm. Find $\varphi_{\text{H}}$ and $\mu_0$.
  3. If you tap or shake the board while lifting it slowly, the block slides already at a clearly smaller height. Discuss why, using static and kinetic friction.
The idea

Turn your axes with the slope: one axis along the plane, one perpendicular. Then gravity splits into a part that pulls the block down the slope, $mg\sin\varphi$, and a part that presses it into the plane, $mg\cos\varphi$. The plane pushes back with the normal force, and static friction can hold up to $\mu_0$ times that. Tilting the plane makes the downhill pull grow and the pressing shrink. At the critical angle they meet, and $m$ and $g$ cancel: the angle depends only on the surfaces.

The weight splits into $F_\parallel=mg\sin\varphi$ down the slope and $F_\perp=mg\cos\varphi$ into the plane. The normal force $F_N$ balances $F_\perp$; static friction $F_R$ balances $F_\parallel$.

You need

Static friction: $F_R\le\mu_0F_N$ (it adjusts itself up to that maximum); kinetic friction $F_R=\mu F_N$ with usually $\mu<\mu_0$. Equilibrium: the forces add to zero in every direction. Inclined plane and friction: Physics lesson 3.

Hint 1

a) In the sketch, the angle between $F_G$ and $F_\perp$ is also $\varphi$ (the two angles have perpendicular sides). So $F_\parallel=mg\sin\varphi$ and $F_\perp=mg\cos\varphi$.

Hint 2

Equilibrium perpendicular to the plane gives $F_N$. Equilibrium along the plane gives the friction actually needed, $F_R=mg\sin\varphi$. “Only just sticks” means the needed friction equals the maximum, $\mu_0F_N$.

Hint 3

b) $\tan\varphi_{\text H}$ is opposite over adjacent in the triangle of the plane: $\frac hl$. c) Think about what a tap does to the normal force for a split second, and which friction coefficient applies once the block moves.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) List the forces (see the sketch).

Weight $\vec F_G=m\vec g$ (down); normal force $\vec F_N$ (perpendicular to the plane, outwards); static friction $\vec F_R$ (along the plane, uphill).

Split the weight in tilted axes.

$F_\parallel=mg\sin\varphi$ (down the slope), $\ F_\perp=mg\cos\varphi$ (into the plane).

At rest, both directions balance.

Perpendicular: $F_N=F_\perp=mg\cos\varphi$.
Along: $F_R=F_\parallel=mg\sin\varphi$.

Static friction can be at most $\mu_0F_N$. At the critical angle it is at its maximum.

$$mg\sin\varphi_{\text H}=\mu_0\,mg\cos\varphi_{\text H}.$$

Cancel $mg$ and divide by $\cos$.

$\mu_0=\dfrac{\sin\varphi_{\text H}}{\cos\varphi_{\text H}}=\tan\varphi_{\text H}$.

b) The plane is a right triangle with opposite side $h$ and adjacent side $l$.

$\tan\varphi_{\text H}=\dfrac hl=\dfrac{45}{75}=0.60$, so $\varphi_{\text H}=\arctan0.60\approx31^\circ$ and $\mu_0=0.60$.

c) Two effects. A tap shakes the surfaces apart for an instant, so the normal force (and with it the maximum friction) briefly drops; and once the block moves, the smaller kinetic friction takes over.

Tapping gives short jolts: the microscopic roughness of the two surfaces, which interlocks while at rest, is briefly lifted apart, so the effective normal force $F_N(t)$ (and with it $F_{R,\max}=\mu_0F_N$) drops for an instant. The block breaks loose at a smaller angle. Once it slides, only kinetic friction acts, and since $\mu<\mu_0$ it can't stop the block: it keeps sliding.

Another way to say c) (also in the official solution): a jolt is a short acceleration. The board must then supply the downhill pull plus the force for that acceleration, so the maximum static friction is exceeded earlier.

Trap

Swapping sin and cos: on a flat plane ($\varphi=0$) the downhill pull must be $0$, so it is $mg\sin\varphi$, not $mg\cos\varphi$. Check every decomposition with $\varphi=0$ and $\varphi=90^\circ$. Second trap: writing $F_R=\mu_0F_N$ for a block at rest below the critical angle. Static friction is only as large as needed; $\mu_0F_N$ is its maximum.

Aufgabe 2 · The water-shy tiger

A round ice floe, $4\,$m in diameter and $150\,$mm thick, floats in water. A tiger of mass $100\,$kg wants to step on it. $\rho_{\text{ice}}=930\,$kg/m³, $\rho_{\text{water}}=1000\,$kg/m³.

  1. How deep does the floe lie in the water before the tiger steps on it?
  2. Will the tiger get wet paws, or does the floe carry it?
  3. Later (in a zoo pool holding $200\,$m³) the floe melts, without the tiger. By how many micrometres does the water level rise, if at all? Explain.
The idea

A floating body sinks until the water it pushes aside weighs exactly as much as the body itself (Archimedes). For a flat slab the pushed-aside water is a slab of the same area, so you only compare thicknesses: the floe sinks to $\frac{930}{1000}$ of its thickness. Add the tiger, and the floe has to push aside $100\,$kg more water. For c): the floating ice already pushes aside exactly its own mass of water. When it melts it becomes exactly that much water, filling the same hole, so the level doesn't change.

Depths in mm (not to scale). Without the tiger $11\,$mm stick out; with the tiger only about $3\,$mm. Dry paws, just.

You need

Archimedes: buoyancy $F_A=\rho_{\text{water}}\,V_{\text{displaced}}\,g$. Floating: buoyancy $=$ weight. Mass $=$ density × volume; area of the floe $A=\pi R^2$ with $R=2\,$m. Buoyancy: Physics lesson 3.

Hint 1

a) Write the displaced volume as $A\cdot d_{\text w}$, where $d_{\text w}$ is the depth below the waterline, and the ice volume as $A\cdot d$. Set weight equal to buoyancy: the area $A$ cancels.

Hint 2

b) Now the weight is $(m_{\text{ice}}+m_{\text{tiger}})g$. Compute $m_{\text{ice}}=\rho_{\text{ice}}\pi R^2d$ first (about $1750\,$kg), then the new depth. Is it more or less than $150\,$mm?

Hint 3

c) Compare two volumes: the water the floating ice displaces, $\frac{m_{\text{ice}}}{\rho_{\text{water}}}$, and the volume of the meltwater, also $\frac{m_{\text{ice}}}{\rho_{\text{water}}}$ (mass is conserved).

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Floating: weight equals buoyancy (the weight of the displaced water).

$m_{\text{ice}}\,g=\rho_{\text w}V_{\text{displ}}\,g$, with $m_{\text{ice}}=\rho_{\text{ice}}Ad$ and $V_{\text{displ}}=A\,d_{\text w}$.

The area cancels: only densities and thickness matter.

$d_{\text w}=\dfrac{\rho_{\text{ice}}}{\rho_{\text w}}\,d=0.93\cdot0.150\,\text m=0.139\,\text m.$

So of $150\,$mm, $11\,$mm stick out. Is that enough for a $100\,$kg tiger?

$m_{\text{ice}}=930\cdot\pi\cdot2^2\cdot0.15\,\text{kg}\approx1753\,\text{kg}$.

b) Same balance with the tiger's mass added.

$$\begin{aligned}d_{\text w}&=\frac{m_{\text{ice}}+m_{\text{tiger}}}{\pi R^2\rho_{\text w}}\\&=\frac{1853\,\text{kg}}{12.57\,\text m^2\cdot1000\,\text{kg/m}^3}\\&=0.147\,\text m<0.150\,\text m.\end{aligned}$$

Compare with the thickness.

The floe carries the tiger, with about $3\,$mm to spare: dry paws, just.

c) Floating ice displaces its own mass of water. Melting turns it into exactly that mass of water, which fills exactly that volume.

Displaced volume $=\frac{m_{\text{ice}}}{\rho_{\text w}}=$ volume of the meltwater. So the level does not change: it rises by $0\,\mu$m. (The size of the pool doesn't matter.)

Trap

In c), computing a rise from the ice volume ($\pi R^2d\approx1.9\,$m³) divided by the pool. The ice is already floating and already displacing water. The $200\,$m³ is a decoy. (It would be different for ice resting on land, like a glacier: that does raise the level when it melts.)

Aufgabe 3 · Motorbike with limited grip

  1. A motorbike can just take a circular bend of radius $r_K=10\,$m at $v=20\,$km/h without sliding. What is the static friction coefficient $\mu_0$ between road and tyres?
  2. At what speed must it ride along the vertical inside wall of a cylinder of radius $r_Z=5\,$m (same material) to move on a horizontal circle without sliding down? $\mu_0$ does not depend on the speed.
  3. Now it rides through a loop of radius $r_L=5\,$m. Sketch the forces and the resulting force at the bottom A, the side B and the top C. What is the minimum speed at C so that it doesn't fall?
The idea

Going round a circle needs a net force towards the centre, $\frac{mv^2}r$ (centripetal force). It is not a new force: something real has to provide it. On the flat bend it is friction. On the wall it is the wall's normal force, and friction (up to $\mu_0$ times that) has to hold the weight, so faster means more grip. At the top of the loop, gravity itself points to the centre; if the bike is slow enough that gravity alone is enough, the track no longer has to push, and below that speed it falls.

The track always pushes towards the centre. At A it must lift the weight and supply the centripetal force; at C gravity helps, and at the minimum speed it does the whole job alone.

You need

Centripetal force $F_Z=\frac{mv^2}r$ for motion on a circle (Physics lesson 3). Static friction $F_R\le\mu_0F_N$. Convert km/h to m/s.

Hint 1

a) On a flat road $F_N=mg$. At the limit the maximum friction $\mu_0mg$ provides exactly $\frac{mv^2}{r_K}$. b) On the wall the roles swap: the wall's normal force provides $\frac{mv^2}{r_Z}$, and friction (vertical) must hold $mg$.

Hint 2

b) $mg=\mu_0F_N=\mu_0\frac{mv^2}{r_Z}$. Solve for $v$. c) At each point, add the forces that point towards the centre; they must give $\frac{mv^2}{r_L}$.

Hint 3

c) At C both $F_N$ and $mg$ point down (to the centre): $F_N+mg=\frac{mv^2}{r_L}$. The track can only push, so $F_N\ge0$. The limit is $F_N=0$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) Flat bend: friction is the only horizontal force, so it must supply the centripetal force. “Just” means friction is at its maximum.

$\mu_0\,mg=\dfrac{mv^2}{r_K}$, with $v=20\,\text{km/h}=5.56\,$m/s.

Mass cancels.

$\mu_0=\dfrac{v^2}{r_K\,g}=\dfrac{(5.56\,\text{m/s})^2}{10\,\text m\cdot9.81\,\text{m/s}^2}\approx0.3$.

b) Wall: the normal force (horizontal, towards the axis) is the centripetal force; friction (vertical) must carry the weight.

$F_N=\dfrac{mv^2}{r_Z}$ and $mg=F_R\le\mu_0F_N$.

At the limit, solve for $v$. Faster is fine: more speed, more normal force, more grip.

$$\begin{aligned}mg&=\mu_0\frac{mv^2}{r_Z}\\ \Rightarrow v&=\sqrt{\frac{g\,r_Z}{\mu_0}}\\&\approx12.5\,\frac{\text m}{\text s}\approx45\,\frac{\text{km}}{\text h}.\end{aligned}$$

c) At A the track pushes up and gravity pulls down; the net upward force is the centripetal force.

A (bottom): $F_N=mg+\frac{mv^2}{r_L}$; the resulting force points up, to the centre.

At B gravity is sideways to the centre direction, so the normal force alone is centripetal.

B (side): $F_N=\frac{mv^2}{r_L}$ towards the centre; $mg$ acts down, perpendicular to it. The resulting force points down and inwards.

At C both forces point down, to the centre.

C (top): $F_N+mg=\frac{mv^2}{r_L}$; the resulting force points down.

The track can only push ($F_N\ge0$). At the limit it doesn't push at all, and gravity alone is the centripetal force.

With $F_N=0$: $$\begin{aligned}mg&=\frac{mv^2}{r_L}\\ \Rightarrow v_{\min}&=\sqrt{g\,r_L}\\&\approx7.0\,\frac{\text m}{\text s}\approx25\,\frac{\text{km}}{\text h}.\end{aligned}$$

Trap

Drawing the centripetal force as an extra arrow next to $F_N$ and $mg$. It is the sum of the real forces, not another force. In the free-body sketch only real forces go in (weight, normal force, friction); $\frac{mv^2}r$ goes on the other side of the equation.

Aufgabe 4 · Weights, springs and massless ropes

In the drawings a)–d) on the sheet, $10\,$kg weights hang on spring scales calibrated in newtons; scales and ropes are massless. Give the reading of each scale. a) One weight hangs from a scale fixed to the ceiling. b) The scale is fixed to a wall; its rope runs over a pulley to one hanging weight. c) The scale lies between two pulleys, with a $10\,$kg weight hanging on each end. d) One weight hangs from a rope over a pulley at the ceiling, with a scale in each of the two rope sections.

e) A ball of weight $100\,$N hangs from two ropes: one horizontal to the wall, one slanted to the ceiling at $50^\circ$ to the ceiling. Find both rope tensions.

The idea

A spring scale shows the tension in its own rope, nothing else. A massless rope over a frictionless pulley has the same tension everywhere; the pulley only changes the direction. So in a), b) and c) the scale reads one weight, $98.1\,$N, even in c) with a weight on each side: one side holds the scale like the wall in b) does. In d) two rope sections share the load. For e) the knot is at rest, so the three forces on it add to zero: split the slanted tension into a vertical part (which carries the ball) and a horizontal part (which the horizontal rope balances).

Case c): the rope tension is $98.1\,$N everywhere, so the scale reads $98.1\,$N. The second weight plays the role of the wall in case b).

Left: the setup of e). Right: the three forces on the knot, drawn head to tail, close into a triangle because they add to zero.

You need

$F_G=mg$ with $g=9.81\,$m/s². A massless rope has the same tension everywhere; a frictionless pulley only turns the direction. Equilibrium of the knot: the vector sum of all forces on it is zero (Physics lesson 2).

Hint 1

a)–c) For each scale, ask: what is the tension in the rope attached to it? In c), why can't the tension be $196\,$N? (Look at one weight: it is at rest, so the rope above it pulls up with exactly its weight.)

Hint 2

d) The weight hangs from two rope sections, each with a scale. By symmetry, each carries half. e) The slanted rope makes $50^\circ$ with the horizontal ceiling, so its vertical part is $F_D\sin50^\circ$ and its horizontal part $F_D\cos50^\circ$.

Hint 3

e) Vertical balance: $F_D\sin50^\circ=100\,$N. Horizontal balance: $F_W=F_D\cos50^\circ$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

a) The scale carries the weight directly.

$F=mg=10\,\text{kg}\cdot9.81\,\text{m/s}^2=98.1\,$N.

b) The pulley turns the force sideways but doesn't change its size.

The rope pulls on the scale with $98.1\,$N.

c) Each weight is at rest, so its rope pulls up with exactly $mg$. The rope tension is $98.1\,$N, and the scale shows the tension.

$98.1\,$N. Same as b): there the wall held the scale with $98.1\,$N, here the second weight does.

d) Two symmetric rope sections share the load.

Each scale: $\dfrac{mg}{2}=49.1\,$N.

e) The knot is at rest: wall rope + ceiling rope + ball's pull $=0$. Write both components.

$x$: $F_W-F_D\cos50^\circ=0$.
$y$: $F_D\sin50^\circ-100\,\text N=0$.

The vertical equation has only one unknown: solve it first.

$F_D=\dfrac{100\,\text N}{\sin50^\circ}=130.5\,$N (slanted rope).

Then the horizontal one.

$F_W=F_D\cos50^\circ=\dfrac{100\,\text N}{\tan50^\circ}=83.9\,$N (horizontal rope).

Sense check. The slanted rope carries more than the ball's $100\,$N: it has to hold the ball up and balance the sideways pull of the wall rope. The flatter the slanted rope, the larger both tensions get.

Trap

Answering $196\,$N in c) by adding the two weights. A scale doesn't add the pulls on its two ends; it shows the tension, which is the pull on one end. (If only one end were pulled, the scale would accelerate.) In e), using $\sin$ and $\cos$ the wrong way round: check that the slanted rope's vertical part is larger when the rope is steeper.
