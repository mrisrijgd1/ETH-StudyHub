  1. What is $\arcsin'(x)$, and $\operatorname{arsinh}'(x)$?

    Answer

    $\frac1{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$ and $\frac1{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$. Read backwards, today they become integrals: $\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}=\arcsin x+C$.

  2. Two functions on an interval have the same derivative everywhere. What can you say?

    Answer

  3. Lesson 9: what does $\int_a^bf$ mean, and which functions are integrable?

    Answer

1. The story: the ledger and the hourglass

In the river town below the rope-makers' valley, two guilds have quarrelled for a hundred years. The Ledger-keepers stand at the toll bridge and add up: every hour they write down how much has crossed so far. The Hourglass-readers watch the bridge for a single moment and say how fast things are crossing right now. Each guild thinks its craft is the hard one.

A young apprentice settles it. Take the Ledger's running total, she says, and ask how fast that grows. In one short moment, the total grows by exactly what crosses in that moment: the rate times the moment. So the speed of the Ledger is the Hourglass. And the other way round: to know the total between morning and evening, you don't need to watch all day. Find any ledger whose speed is the Hourglass, and subtract its morning entry from its evening entry.

In maths: the Ledger is $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$, the area accumulated up to $x$, and the Hourglass is the rate $f$. The apprentice's two sentences are the fundamental theorem of calculus: $F'=f$, and $\int_a^bf=F(b)-F(a)$ for every antiderivative $F$. Areas, which needed sums of rectangles in lesson 9, become a matter of reading derivative tables backwards.

2. The fundamental theorem

For $f$ integrable on $[a,b]$, every $\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$ with $x\in[a,b]$ exists (an integral over a sub-interval, lesson 9). So we get a new function, the integral with variable upper limit $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$. The variable of integration is now called $t$, because $x$ is taken.

Proof A: the integral with variable upper limit is an antiderivative (Theorem 9.2, p. 417–418)

Theorem. Let $a<b$ and $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ Riemann integrable. If $f$ is continuous at $x_0\in[a,b]$, then $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$ is differentiable at $x_0$ with $F'(x_0)=f(x_0)$.

You'll need these (lesson 9)
  • Interval additivity: $\int_a^xf-\int_a^{x_0}f=\int_{x_0}^xf$ for $x_0<x$.
  • Integral of a constant: $\int_{x_0}^xc\,dt=c\,(x-x_0)$.
  • Triangle inequality and monotonicity: $\left|\int g\right|\le\int|g|$, and $|g|\le\varepsilon$ gives $\int_{x_0}^x|g|\le\varepsilon(x-x_0)$.

The idea. The difference quotient $\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}$ is the average of $f$ over the strip between $x_0$ and $x$. Continuity at $x_0$ says that, on a short enough strip, every value of $f$ is within $\varepsilon$ of $f(x_0)$. An average of numbers that are all within $\varepsilon$ of $f(x_0)$ is itself within $\varepsilon$ of $f(x_0)$. The one trick: write $f(x_0)$ itself as an average, $f(x_0)=\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^xf(x_0)\,dt$, so the two can be subtracted under one integral.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Goal: “for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ with $\left|\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}-f(x_0)\right|\le\varepsilon$ for all $x\in[a,b]$ with $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$.”
  • The only information: continuity at $x_0$ gives $\delta$ with $|f(t)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ for $|t-x_0|<\delta$.
  • The gap: turn the difference quotient into one integral of $f(t)-f(x_0)$. Two cases, $x>x_0$ and $x<x_0$.
The storyWhat you write

Choose a moment so short that the Hourglass barely changes during it.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $f$ is continuous at $x_0$, there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(t)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ for all $t\in[a,b]$ with $|t-x_0|<\delta$.

The Ledger's growth in that moment is just what crossed in it.

Case $x>x_0$, with $x\in[a,b]$ and $x-x_0<\delta$. By interval additivity, $F(x)-F(x_0)=\int_{x_0}^xf(t)\,dt$.

Pretend the rate had stayed frozen: that would have given exactly the frozen rate times the moment.

Since $f(x_0)=\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^xf(x_0)\,dt$,

$$\begin{aligned}&\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}-f(x_0)\\&\quad=\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^x\bigl(f(t)-f(x_0)\bigr)\,dt.\end{aligned}$$

The true crossing never differed from the frozen one by more than a hair per hour.

For $t\in[x_0,x]$ we have $|t-x_0|<\delta$, so $|f(t)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$. By the triangle inequality for integrals,

$$\begin{aligned}&\left|\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}-f(x_0)\right|\\&\quad\le \frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^x|f(t)-f(x_0)|\,dt\\&\quad\le \frac{\varepsilon\,(x-x_0)}{x-x_0}\\&\quad=\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$

Looking back into the past works the same way.

Case $x<x_0$, with $x_0-x<\delta$. Now $F(x_0)-F(x)=\int_x^{x_0}f(t)\,dt$, and $\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}=\frac{F(x_0)-F(x)}{x_0-x}$, so the same computation on $[x,x_0]$ gives the bound $\varepsilon$.

So the Ledger's speed at that moment is the Hourglass.

Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $\lim_{x\to x_0}\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}=f(x_0)$: $F$ is differentiable at $x_0$ with $F'(x_0)=f(x_0)$. (At an endpoint $x_0=a$ or $b$, only one case occurs.) ∎

Moves used: continuity with a chosen ε write a number as an average split into cases

The proof in one piece

Let $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $\delta>0$ with $|f(t)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ for $t\in[a,b]$, $|t-x_0|<\delta$. Let $x\in[a,b]$ with $0<x-x_0<\delta$. By interval additivity and $f(x_0)=\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^xf(x_0)\,dt$,

$$\begin{aligned}&\left|\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}-f(x_0)\right|\\&\quad=\left|\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^x\bigl(f(t)-f(x_0)\bigr)dt\right|\\&\quad\le \frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^x|f(t)-f(x_0)|\,dt\\&\quad\le \varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$

For $0<x_0-x<\delta$, the same estimate on $[x,x_0]$ holds, since $\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}=\frac1{x_0-x}\int_x^{x_0}f(t)\,dt$. Hence $\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}\to f(x_0)$, i.e. $F'(x_0)=f(x_0)$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. ε, δ from continuity at $x_0$.
  2. Additivity: $F(x)-F(x_0)=\int_{x_0}^xf$.
  3. Average trick: subtract $f(x_0)=\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^xf(x_0)\,dt$ under the same integral.
  4. Estimate: triangle inequality, each value $<\varepsilon$, so the average is $\le\varepsilon$.
  5. Other side: “analogously for $x<x_0$”, with the one-line reason.

In short: “the quotient is an average; continuity makes every value close; so the average is close.” This is the 2018 winter exam's question 12 (5 points), word for word.

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Which step uses that $f$ is integrable, and which uses that $f$ is continuous at $x_0$?

    Answer

    Integrable: $F$ exists at all, and interval additivity holds. Continuous at $x_0$: the bound $|f(t)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ near $x_0$. Away from $x_0$, $f$ may be as wild as integrability allows.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Let $f=0$ on $[-1,0]$ and $f=1$ on $(0,1]$. Compute $F(x)=\int_{-1}^xf$. Is $F$ differentiable at $0$? What does that show?

    Answer

    $F(x)=0$ for $x\le0$ and $F(x)=x$ for $x>0$, i.e. $F(x)=\max(x,0)$. Left derivative $0$, right derivative $1$: not differentiable at $0$. So continuity at $x_0$ can't be dropped. ($F$ is still continuous: integrals with variable limit always are, lesson 9.)

Worked example 1: derivatives of integrals

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

  1. $G(x)=\int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt$. What is $G'(x)$? Is $G$ increasing?

    Answer

    $e^{-t^2}$ is continuous everywhere, so by Proof A, $G'(x)=e^{-x^2}>0$, and $G$ is strictly increasing. (No formula for $G$ exists in terms of known functions, yet we know its derivative exactly.)

  2. $H(x)=\int_0^{x^2}\sin(t^2)\,dt$. Compute $H'(x)$.

    Answer

    $H=G\circ q$ with $G(u)=\int_0^u\sin(t^2)\,dt$ and $q(x)=x^2$. Chain rule: $$H'(x)=G'(x^2)\cdot2x=\sin(x^4)\cdot2x.$$

  3. $K(x)=\int_x^{2x}\frac{dt}t$ for $x>0$. Compute $K'(x)$ without integrating. Then check by integrating.

    Answer

    $K(x)=\int_1^{2x}\frac{dt}t-\int_1^x\frac{dt}t$, so $K'(x)=\frac1{2x}\cdot2-\frac1x=0$. Indeed $K(x)=\log(2x)-\log x=\log2$ is constant.

3. Computing integrals with antiderivatives

An antiderivative (Stammfunktion) of $f$ on an interval is a differentiable $F$ with $F'=f$. The indefinite integral $\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C$ is shorthand for “all antiderivatives”.

Proof B: all antiderivatives, and $\int_a^bf=F(b)-F(a)$ (Korollar 9.3–9.5, p. 418–419)

Theorem. Let $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ be continuous.

  1. $x\mapsto\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$ is an antiderivative of $f$, and every antiderivative has the form $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt+C$ for a constant $C$.
  2. For every antiderivative $F$ of $f$: $\displaystyle\int_a^bf(t)\,dt=F(b)-F(a)=:\bigl[F(x)\bigr]_a^b$.
  3. For $F$ continuously differentiable on $[a,b]$: $F(x)=F(a)+\int_a^xF'(t)\,dt$.

The idea. Proof A says the Ledger is an antiderivative. Any other antiderivative has the same derivative, so it differs by a constant. Find the constant by plugging in $x=a$, where the Ledger is $0$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Goal of 1: “$F_0'=f$” and “$F-F_0$ is constant.”
  • Goal of 2 and 3: an equation between numbers, so: name the constant $C$, then evaluate at $x=a$ and $x=b$.
  • The gap: none beyond Proof A and “derivative zero on an interval ⇒ constant”.
The storyWhat you write

The town's Ledger runs at the Hourglass's speed everywhere, since the rate never jumps.

1. $f$ is continuous, hence integrable (lesson 9), and continuous at every point. By Proof A, $F_0(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$ satisfies $F_0'=f$ on $[a,b]$.

Any other ledger with the same speed only started from a different entry.

If $F'=f$ too, then $(F-F_0)'=0$ on the interval $[a,b]$, so $F-F_0=C$ is constant (lesson 17, Proof H).

Read the difference of evening and morning entries: the starting entry cancels.

2. At $x=a$: $F(a)=F_0(a)+C=0+C$, so $C=F(a)$. At $x=b$: $\int_a^bf=F_0(b)=F(b)-C=F(b)-F(a)$.

A ledger is its morning entry plus everything that crossed since.

3. $F'$ is continuous and $F$ is an antiderivative of it, so by 2 on $[a,x]$: $\int_a^xF'(t)\,dt=F(x)-F(a)$. ∎

Moves used: name the constant, then plug in a point

The proof in one piece

As $f$ is continuous, it is integrable and, by Proof A, $F_0(x)=\int_a^xf$ satisfies $F_0'=f$. If also $F'=f$, then $(F-F_0)'=0$ on $[a,b]$, so $F=F_0+C$. Evaluating at $a$ gives $C=F(a)$, and at $b$ gives $\int_a^bf=F(b)-F(a)$. Applying this to $F'$ on $[a,x]$ gives $F(x)=F(a)+\int_a^xF'$. ∎

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why does it matter that $[a,b]$ is an interval? Find two functions on $(-1,0)\cup(0,1)$ with the same derivative that don't differ by a constant.

4. Differentiating power series

Lesson 16 integrated power series term by term (with the same radius). The fundamental theorem turns this into differentiation.

Proof C: power series can be differentiated term by term (Korollar 9.11, p. 420–421)

Theorem. Let $f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_nx^n$ have radius of convergence $R>0$. Then $f$ is differentiable on $(-R,R)$ and

$$f'(x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty nc_nx^{n-1},$$

and this series also has radius $R$. Repeating it: $f$ is infinitely often differentiable on $(-R,R)$.

You'll need these (lesson 16)
  • Term-by-term integration: a power series $g(x)=\sum b_kx^k$ with radius $S$ is continuous on $(-S,S)$, and $\int_0^xg=\sum\frac{b_k}{k+1}x^{k+1}$, again with radius $S$.

The idea. Don't differentiate $f$ directly. Take the series you want as the derivative, $g(x)=\sum nc_nx^{n-1}$, and integrate it term by term: you get back $f$ minus its constant term. So $f$ is an integral of the continuous $g$, and Proof A says its derivative is $g$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Goal: “$f$ is differentiable at each $x\in(-R,R)$ with $f'(x)=g(x)$”, and “the radius $S$ of $g$ equals $R$”.
  • The gap: write $f$ as $c_0+\int_0^xg$. That needs $g$'s radius first.
The storyWhat you write

Guess the spell's speed, word by word.

Let $$g(x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty nc_nx^{n-1}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty(k+1)c_{k+1}x^k,$$with radius $S$. On $(-S,S)$, $g$ is continuous (lesson 16).

Add up the guessed speed: the spell itself comes back, minus its first word.

Term-by-term integration gives, for $|x|<S$,

$$\begin{aligned}G(x):=\int_0^xg(t)\,dt&=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(k+1)c_{k+1}}{k+1}x^{k+1}\\&=\sum_{n=1}^\infty c_nx^n,\end{aligned}$$

and this series has radius $S$.

So the two spells reach equally far.

But $\sum_{n\ge1}c_nx^n$ is $f$ without its constant term, which has radius $R$. Hence $S=R$, and $f(x)=c_0+G(x)$ on $(-R,R)$.

And the Ledger's speed is the Hourglass.

$g$ is continuous on $(-R,R)$, so by Proof A, $G'=g$ there. Therefore $f'(x)=G'(x)=g(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}nc_nx^{n-1}$. Applying this to $f'$ again and again gives all higher derivatives. ∎

Moves used: go to partial sums (through term-by-term integration) name the constant, then plug in a point

The proof in one piece

Let $g(x)=\sum_{k\ge0}(k+1)c_{k+1}x^k$ with radius $S$; $g$ is continuous on $(-S,S)$. By term-by-term integration, $G(x)=\int_0^xg=\sum_{n\ge1}c_nx^n$ with radius $S$; since this is $f-c_0$, $S=R$. By Proof A, $G'=g$ on $(-R,R)$, so $f'=(c_0+G)'=g=\sum_{n\ge1}nc_nx^{n-1}$. Iterating gives smoothness. ∎

Worked example 3: values of famous series

  1. Show $\log(1+x)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}kx^k$ for $|x|<1$.

    Answer

    $$\log(1+x)'=\frac1{1+x}=\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^nx^n$$for $|x|<1$. By Proof B part 3 and term-by-term integration: $$\begin{aligned}\log(1+x)&=\log1+\int_0^x\sum(-1)^nt^n\,dt\\&=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n}{n+1}x^{n+1}.\end{aligned}$$(This is on the formula sheet.)

  2. Felder HS22 1.A6 (2 points): compute $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n\,2^n}$.

    Answer

    Put $x=-\frac12$ in the series for $-\log(1+x)$: $-\log(1+x)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(-1)^k}kx^k$, and with $x=-\frac12$, $(-1)^kx^k=\frac1{2^k}$. So the sum is $-\log\frac12=\log2$. (Equivalently: $-\log(1-y)=\sum\frac{y^n}n$ at $y=\frac12$.)

  3. Why does $1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\dots=\log2$ need one more step than item 1?

    Answer

    Item 1 holds only for $|x|<1$, and $x=1$ is on the boundary. The series converges at $x=1$ (Leibniz), so by Abel's theorem (lesson 16) the power series is continuous up to $x=1$, and $\log(1+x)$ is too. Letting $x\nearrow1$ gives $\log2$. The same with $$\arctan'(x)=\frac1{1+x^2}=\sum(-1)^nx^{2n}$$gives $1-\frac13+\frac15-\dots=\arctan1=\frac\pi4$.

Why it's cool. Three numbers that seem to come from different worlds meet here: $\frac\pi4$ (a circle), $\log2$ (exponential growth), and the plain fractions $1,\frac13,\frac15,\dots$. The fundamental theorem is the bridge: a fact about derivatives ($\arctan'=\frac1{1+x^2}$) becomes a fact about areas, and then about an infinite sum. Newton and Leibniz each found this bridge in the 1660s–1670s, and with it, problems that had taken Archimedes a whole book became exercises.

5. Integration by parts

Each rule of differentiation, read backwards, is a rule of integration. The product rule gives integration by parts.

Proof D: integration by parts (Section 9.2.1 and 9.2.6, p. 425 and 434)

Theorem. Let $u,v$ be continuously differentiable on an interval. Then

$$\begin{aligned}&\int u\,v'\,dx=u\,v-\int u'\,v\,dx\\&\int_a^bu\,v'\,dx=\bigl[u\,v\bigr]_a^b-\int_a^bu'\,v\,dx.\end{aligned}$$

The idea. The product rule $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$ says $uv$ is an antiderivative of $u'v+uv'$. Move one of the two terms to the other side. The point of the rule: you trade the integral of $uv'$ for the integral of $u'v$, which is easier when differentiating $u$ makes it simpler (like $x\mapsto1$) and integrating $v'$ doesn't make it worse (like $e^x$).

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Goal (indefinite): “$uv-W$ is an antiderivative of $uv'$, where $W$ is an antiderivative of $u'v$.” So: differentiate $uv-W$.
  • Goal (definite): an equation of numbers: use Proof B on both sides.
The storyWhat you write

The hard ledger is not written directly; it's a known ledger minus an easier one.

$u'v$ is continuous, so it has an antiderivative $W$ (Proof B). By the product rule, $(uv-W)'=u'v+uv'-u'v=uv'$. So $uv-W$ is an antiderivative of $uv'$, which is the indefinite formula.

Between morning and evening, read off each ledger.

By Proof B, $$\begin{aligned}\int_a^buv'&=\bigl[uv-W\bigr]_a^b\\&=\bigl[uv\bigr]_a^b-\bigl[W\bigr]_a^b\\&=\bigl[uv\bigr]_a^b-\int_a^bu'v.\end{aligned}$$∎

Moves used: check by differentiating

The proof in one piece

Let $W$ be an antiderivative of the continuous $u'v$. Then $(uv-W)'=u'v+uv'-u'v=uv'$, so $\int uv'=uv-\int u'v$. Evaluating with Proof B: $\int_a^buv'=[uv]_a^b-[W]_a^b=[uv]_a^b-\int_a^bu'v$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why do we need $u,v$ continuously differentiable, not just differentiable?

    Answer

  2. Rung 2 (1 min). Write the rule in the short form with $dv$ and $du$.

    Answer

    $\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du$, where $dv=v'dx$ and $du=u'dx$. It is only shorthand for the formula above.

How to choose $u$ and $v'$

  • $u$: the factor that gets simpler when differentiated: a polynomial $x^n$ (after $n$ steps it's a constant), $\log x$ (becomes $\frac1x$), $\arctan x$, $\arcsin x$.
  • $v'$: the factor you can integrate without it getting worse: $e^{x}$, $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $x^s$.
  • A single $\log x$ or $\arctan x$: take $v'=1$.
  • $e^{ax}\sin(bx)$: integrate by parts twice; the original integral comes back, and you solve for it.

Worked example 4: integration by parts

  1. $\int xe^x\,dx$.

    Answer

    $u=x$, $v'=e^x$, so $u'=1$, $v=e^x$: $$\begin{aligned}\int xe^x\,dx&=xe^x-\int e^x\,dx\\&=(x-1)e^x+C.\end{aligned}$$Check: $$\bigl((x-1)e^x\bigr)'=e^x+(x-1)e^x=xe^x.$$✓

    Answer

  3. $\int_0^\pi x\cos x\,dx$ (2019 winter exam, question 1a).

    Answer

    $u=x$, $v'=\cos x$: $$\begin{aligned}&\bigl[x\sin x\bigr]_0^\pi-\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx\\&\quad=0-\bigl[-\cos x\bigr]_0^\pi\\&\quad=0-2\\&\quad=-2.\end{aligned}$$

  4. $\int e^x\sin x\,dx$.

    Answer

    Call it $I$. With $u=\sin x$, $v'=e^x$: $I=e^x\sin x-\int e^x\cos x\,dx$. Again with $u=\cos x$: $$\begin{aligned}\int e^x\cos x\,dx&=e^x\cos x+\int e^x\sin x\,dx\\&=e^x\cos x+I.\end{aligned}$$So $I=e^x\sin x-e^x\cos x-I$, and $I=\frac12e^x(\sin x-\cos x)+C$.

6. Substitution

The chain rule, read backwards, is the substitution rule.

Proof E: the substitution rule (Section 9.2.2 and 9.2.6, p. 426 and 435)

Theorem. Let $\varphi:[a,b]\to I$ be continuously differentiable and $g:I\to\mathbb R$ continuous on an interval $I$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}&\int g\bigl(\varphi(x)\bigr)\varphi'(x)\,dx=G\bigl(\varphi(x)\bigr)+C\\&\int_a^bg\bigl(\varphi(x)\bigr)\varphi'(x)\,dx=\int_{\varphi(a)}^{\varphi(b)}g(u)\,du,\end{aligned}$$

where $G$ is any antiderivative of $g$.

The idea. By the chain rule, $(G\circ\varphi)'=g(\varphi)\cdot\varphi'$. So if the integrand has the form “$g$ of an inner function, times the inner function's derivative”, the antiderivative is $G$ of the inner function. In practice: name the inner function $u=\varphi(x)$, replace $\varphi'(x)\,dx$ by $du$, and in a definite integral also replace the limits by $\varphi(a)$ and $\varphi(b)$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Goal (indefinite): “$(G\circ\varphi)'=(g\circ\varphi)\cdot\varphi'$.”
  • Goal (definite): both sides equal $G(\varphi(b))-G(\varphi(a))$, by Proof B twice.
The storyWhat you write

Measure the crossing in a different clock: the inner clock's speed comes out as a factor.

$g$ is continuous, so it has an antiderivative $G$ on $I$ (Proof B). By the chain rule, $$(G\circ\varphi)'(x)=G'(\varphi(x))\varphi'(x)=g(\varphi(x))\varphi'(x):$$the indefinite formula.

Both ledgers show the same difference between morning and evening.

The integrand $(g\circ\varphi)\varphi'$ is continuous, so by Proof B, $$\begin{aligned}\int_a^bg(\varphi(x))\varphi'(x)\,dx&=G(\varphi(b))-G(\varphi(a))\\&=\int_{\varphi(a)}^{\varphi(b)}g(u)\,du.\end{aligned}$$∎

Moves used: check by differentiating

The proof in one piece

Let $G'=g$ on $I$. By the chain rule $(G\circ\varphi)'=(g\circ\varphi)\varphi'$, which is continuous; so by Proof B, $$\int_a^b(g\circ\varphi)\varphi'=G(\varphi(b))-G(\varphi(a))=\int_{\varphi(a)}^{\varphi(b)}g.$$∎

How to spot a substitution

  • An inner function whose derivative is (up to a constant) also a factor: $xe^{x^2}$, $\frac{x}{1+x^2}$, $x^{2017}\sin(x^{2018})$, $\frac{\cos x}{1+\sin x}$, $\frac{\log x}x$.
  • $\frac{\varphi'}{\varphi}$: $\int\frac{\varphi'(x)}{\varphi(x)}dx=\log|\varphi(x)|+C$. Very common: $\tan x=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}=-\frac{(\cos x)'}{\cos x}$.
  • A root or a strange inner function you want to get rid of: $u=\sqrt x$ (so $x=u^2$, $dx=2u\,du$), $u=\sqrt t+1$, $u=e^x$, $u=t^{1/6}$. Express everything in $u$ and hope for a rational function.
  • Linear inner functions are free: $\int f(ax+b)\,dx=\frac1aF(ax+b)+C$.

Worked example 5: substitution

  1. $\int\frac x{1+x^2}dx$ and $\int\tan x\,dx$.

    Answer

    $u=1+x^2$, $du=2x\,dx$: $\int\frac{du}{2u}=\frac12\log(1+x^2)+C$. And $u=\cos x$, $du=-\sin x\,dx$: $$\begin{aligned}\int\tan x\,dx&=-\int\frac{du}u\\&=-\log|\cos x|+C\end{aligned}$$on each interval where $\cos x\neq0$.

  2. $\int x^{2017}\sin\left(x^{2018}\right)dx$ (2018 summer exam, question 1a).

    Answer

    $u=x^{2018}$, $du=2018x^{2017}dx$: $$\begin{aligned}\frac1{2018}\int\sin u\,du&=-\frac1{2018}\cos\left(x^{2018}\right)+C\end{aligned}.$$

  3. $\int\sin\sqrt x\,dx$ for $x>0$.

    Answer

    $x=u^2$ with $u=\sqrt x>0$, $dx=2u\,du$: $\int2u\sin u\,du$. By parts ($u$ differentiated, $\sin u$ integrated): $2(-u\cos u+\sin u)$. So $$\begin{aligned}&\int\sin\sqrt x\,dx\\&\quad=2\sin\sqrt x-2\sqrt x\cos\sqrt x+C.\end{aligned}$$

  4. $\int_0^{\pi^2}\cos\sqrt x\,dx$ (Figalli HS23, MC6). (A) $0$ (B) $2$ (C) $-4$ (D) $-1$

    Answer

    $x=s^2$, $dx=2s\,ds$, limits $0$ and $\pi$: $2\int_0^\pi s\cos s\,ds=2\cdot(-2)=-4$, using Worked example 4, item 3. (C).

7. Rational functions and partial fractions

A rational function $\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ always has an elementary antiderivative, and there is a fixed recipe. It is the method behind most of the K1 questions.

The recipe for $\int\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}dx$

  1. Divide if $\deg p\ge\deg q$: $\frac pq=s+\frac{r}q$ with a polynomial $s$ and $\deg r<\deg q$ (polynomial division).
  2. Factor $q$ into linear factors $(x-a)$ and quadratic factors $x^2+bx+c$ without real zeros.
  3. Ansatz: one term $\frac{A}{x-a}$ per simple zero; $\frac{A_1}{x-a}+\frac{A_2}{(x-a)^2}+\dots+\frac{A_k}{(x-a)^k}$ for a $k$-fold zero; $\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+bx+c}$ per quadratic factor.
  4. Find the constants: multiply by $q$, then plug in the zeros of $q$ (the fastest way), and compare coefficients or plug in other values for the rest.
  5. Integrate each piece:
$$\begin{aligned}&\int\frac{dx}{x-a}=\log|x-a|\\&\int\frac{dx}{(x-a)^k}=\frac{(x-a)^{1-k}}{1-k}\ (k\ge2)\\&\int\frac{dx}{(x+\beta)^2+\gamma^2}=\frac1\gamma\arctan\frac{x+\beta}\gamma\\&\int\frac{2x+b}{x^2+bx+c}dx=\log(x^2+bx+c).\end{aligned}$$

For $\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+bx+c}$: split off a multiple of $2x+b$ (the derivative of the denominator), and complete the square in the rest: $x^2+bx+c=\left(x+\frac b2\right)^2+\left(c-\frac{b^2}4\right)$.

Worked example 6: partial fractions

  1. $\int\frac{dx}{x^2-x-2}$ (Einsiedler mock HS21, 1c).

    Answer

    $x^2-x-2=(x-2)(x+1)$. Ansatz $\frac1{(x-2)(x+1)}=\frac A{x-2}+\frac B{x+1}$, so $1=A(x+1)+B(x-2)$. $x=2$: $A=\frac13$. $x=-1$: $B=-\frac13$. Hence $$\begin{aligned}\int&=\frac13\log|x-2|-\frac13\log|x+1|+C\\&=\frac13\log\left|\frac{x-2}{x+1}\right|+C.\end{aligned}$$

  2. $\int\frac{2x+1}{x^2(x+1)}dx$ (Figalli FS26, K1).

    Answer

    Double zero at $0$: $\frac{2x+1}{x^2(x+1)}=\frac A{x^2}+\frac Bx+\frac C{x+1}$, so $$\begin{aligned}2x+1&=A(x+1)+Bx(x+1)+Cx^2\end{aligned}.$$$x=0$: $A=1$. $x=-1$: $C=-1$. Coefficient of $x^2$: $0=B+C$, so $B=1$. Hence $$\int=-\frac1x+\log|x|-\log|x+1|+C.$$

  3. $\int\frac{dx}{x(x^2+2x+2)}$ .

    Answer

    $x^2+2x+2=(x+1)^2+1$ has no real zeros. Ansatz $\frac Ax+\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+2x+2}$: $$1=A(x^2+2x+2)+(Bx+C)x.$$$x=0$: $A=\frac12$. Coefficients of $x^2$: $0=\frac12+B$, so $B=-\frac12$; of $x$: $0=1+C$, so $C=-1$. The second piece is $$\begin{aligned}&-\frac12\cdot\frac{x+2}{x^2+2x+2}\\&\quad=-\frac14\cdot\frac{2x+2}{x^2+2x+2}\\&\qquad-\frac12\cdot\frac1{(x+1)^2+1}.\end{aligned}$$So

    $$\begin{aligned}\int={}&\tfrac12\log|x|-\tfrac14\log\left(x^2+2x+2\right)\\&-\tfrac12\arctan(x+1)+C.\end{aligned}$$

  4. $\int\frac{3}{(1+x^2)(4+x^2)}dx$ (Figalli HS23, K1).

    Answer

    Treat $x^2$ as one variable $y$: $\frac3{(1+y)(4+y)}=\frac1{1+y}-\frac1{4+y}$ (check: $\frac{(4+y)-(1+y)}{(1+y)(4+y)}$). So $\int=\arctan x-\frac12\arctan\frac x2+C$.

Worked example 7: the area of a circle, and friends

  1. $\int\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx$ on $(-1,1)$.

    Answer

    $x=\sin\theta$, $dx=\cos\theta\,d\theta$, $\sqrt{1-x^2}=\cos\theta$ (positive on $\left(-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2\right)$).     Answer

9. Worked examples: choosing the method

A mixed set to revise the whole lesson before the exercises. In the exam the hard part is not the computation, but seeing which method fits. For each integral, first say which method and why, then open the answer.

The decision list (in this order)

  1. Is it in the table, maybe after simplifying or splitting the fraction?
  2. Is there an inner function whose derivative is also a factor? Then substitution. (Includes $\frac{\varphi'}\varphi$.)
  3. Is it a product of two different kinds (polynomial × exp/trig, or a single log/arctan)? Then by parts.
  4. Is it a rational function? Then partial fractions.
  5. Is there $\sqrt{a^2\pm x^2}$ or $\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$? Trigonometric or hyperbolic substitution. Only $\sin$ and $\cos$ in a fraction? Half-angle.
  6. A root or exponential you'd like to get rid of? Substitute it, $u=\sqrt x$, $u=e^x$, and see.

Worked example 8: which method?

  1. $\int\frac{\log x}x\,dx$.

    Answer

    Substitution: $u=\log x$, $du=\frac{dx}x$. $\int u\,du=\frac12(\log x)^2+C$. (By parts would also work, but takes longer.)

  2. $\int x^2e^{-x}\,dx$ (Einsiedler FS22, 1b).

    Answer

    By parts, twice ($u=x^2$, then $u=x$, with $v'=e^{-x}$, $v=-e^{-x}$): $$\begin{aligned}&-x^2e^{-x}+\int2xe^{-x}dx\\&\quad=-x^2e^{-x}-2xe^{-x}+\int2e^{-x}dx\\&\quad=-(x^2+2x+2)e^{-x}+C.\end{aligned}$$

  3. $\int\frac{dx}{x(x+3)}$ (2018 summer, 1d).

    Answer

    Partial fractions: $\frac1{x(x+3)}=\frac13\left(\frac1x-\frac1{x+3}\right)$. $\int=\frac13\log\left|\frac x{x+3}\right|+C$.

  4. $\int\frac{\sin x}{1+\cos x}dx$ (Figalli FS24, K2).

    Answer

    $\frac{\varphi'}\varphi$ with $\varphi=1+\cos x$, $\varphi'=-\sin x$: $\int=-\log(1+\cos x)+C$. No half-angle needed: the numerator is (minus) the derivative of the denominator.

  5. $\int\frac{dx}{1+e^x}$.

    Answer

    Get rid of $e^x$: $u=e^x$, $du=u\,dx$: $$\begin{aligned}\int\frac{du}{u(1+u)}&=\int\left(\frac1u-\frac1{1+u}\right)du\\&=\log u-\log(1+u).\end{aligned}$$So $x-\log(1+e^x)+C$.

  6. $\int_1^e x\left(\log x+\frac12\right)dx$ (2018 summer, 1b).

    Answer

    By parts on $x\log x$ ($u=\log x$, $v=\frac{x^2}2$): $\int x\log x=\frac{x^2}2\log x-\frac{x^2}4$. Adding $\int\frac x2=\frac{x^2}4$ gives the antiderivative $\frac{x^2}2\log x$. So the integral is $\frac{e^2}2-0=\frac{e^2}2$.

Solution

Frame: the numerator is the derivative of the denominator: $\frac{\varphi'}\varphi$.

$u=1+\sin x$, $du=\cos x\,dx$: $$\begin{aligned}\int\frac{du}u&=\log|1+\sin x|+C\\&=\log(1+\sin x)+C\end{aligned}$$(as $1+\sin x\ge0$, on each interval where it's $>0$). Figalli FS26 K2, $\int\frac{\cos x}{2+\sin x}dx=\log(2+\sin x)+C$, is the same question.

Exercise 4 (Einsiedler HS21, question 1b, 2 points)

Open the original: HS21 exam · solutions

Compute $\int_1^4\frac{\exp\left(\sqrt t+1\right)}{\sqrt t}dt$.

Solution

Frame: an inner function $\sqrt t+1$ whose derivative $\frac1{2\sqrt t}$ is also a factor: substitution, with new limits.

$u=\sqrt t+1$, $\frac{du}{dt}=\frac1{2\sqrt t}$, so $\frac{dt}{\sqrt t}=2\,du$. Limits: $t=1\mapsto u=2$, $t=4\mapsto u=3$.

$$\int_1^4\frac{e^{\sqrt t+1}}{\sqrt t}dt=2\int_2^3e^u\,du=2\left(e^3-e^2\right).$$

(Official grading: 1 point if the correct value appears but a “$+C$” is left in a definite integral.)

Exercise 5 (Figalli FS24 K1 and Felder FS23 1.A3, 2 points)

Open the original: FS23 exam with solutions · FS24 exam with solutions

(a) $\int\frac{2x+1}{x(x-1)}dx$. (b) $\int\frac{dx}{x(x^2+1)}$ for $x>0$. (Count it as 2 points if both are right, 1 if one is.)

Solution

(a) $2x+1=A(x-1)+Bx$. $x=0$: $A=-1$. $x=1$: $B=3$. So $\int=-\log|x|+3\log|x-1|+C$.

(b) $\frac1{x(x^2+1)}=\frac Ax+\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+1}$: $1=A(x^2+1)+(Bx+C)x$. $x=0$: $A=1$. $x^2$: $0=A+B$, so $B=-1$; $x$: $C=0$. So $$\begin{aligned}&\int\left(\frac1x-\frac x{x^2+1}\right)dx\\&\quad=\log x-\frac12\log(1+x^2)+C.\end{aligned}$$

Exercise 6 (Einsiedler HS21, question 1c, 3 points)

Open the original: HS21 exam · solutions

Compute $\int\frac{dt}{t^{2/3}+t^{1/2}}$ on $(0,\infty)$.

Solution

Frame: roots $t^{1/3}$ and $t^{1/2}$: substitute the smallest common root, $u=t^{1/6}$, to get a rational function.

$t=u^6$, $dt=6u^5\,du$, $t^{2/3}=u^4$, $t^{1/2}=u^3$:

$$\begin{aligned}&\int\frac{6u^5}{u^4+u^3}du\\&\quad=6\int\frac{u^2}{u+1}du\\&\quad=6\int\left(u-1+\frac1{u+1}\right)du\\&\quad=3u^2-6u+6\log(u+1)+C.\end{aligned}$$

(Polynomial division: $u^2=(u+1)(u-1)+1$.) Back: $3t^{1/3}-6t^{1/6}+6\log\left(t^{1/6}+1\right)+C$. The official grading splits the 3 points into steps, starting with a correct substitution.

Exercise 8 (Einsiedler FS22, question 1a and 1c)

Open the original: FS22 exam

(a) $\int_0^1\frac t{t^2-9}dt$ (2 points). (c) $\int\left(4-x^2\right)^{-3/2}dx$ on $(-2,2)$ (2 points). (Part (b) is Worked example 8, item 2.)

Solution

(a) $\frac{\varphi'}\varphi$ up to the factor $\frac12$: $$\begin{aligned}\int_0^1\frac t{t^2-9}dt&=\left[\frac12\log|t^2-9|\right]_0^1\\&=\frac12(\log8-\log9)\\&=\frac12\log\frac89.\end{aligned}$$(Fine on $[0,1]$: $t^2-9\neq0$ there.)

(c) $x=2\sin\theta$, $\theta\in\left(-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2\right)$: $dx=2\cos\theta\,d\theta$, $4-x^2=4\cos^2\theta$, so $(4-x^2)^{-3/2}=\frac1{8\cos^3\theta}$.

Exercise 10 (2018 winter exam, question 12, 5 points)

Open the original: 2018 winter exam

Let $[a,b]\subset\mathbb R$ with $a<b$, and let $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ be Riemann integrable and continuous at $x_0\in(a,b)$. Show that $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$ is differentiable at $x_0$ with $F'(x_0)=f(x_0)$.

Solution

Frame: Proof A. Moves: continuity with a chosen ε write a number as an average split into cases.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. By continuity at $x_0$ there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(t)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ for all $t\in[a,b]$ with $|t-x_0|<\delta$. For $x\in[a,b]$ with $0<x-x_0<\delta$, interval additivity gives $F(x)-F(x_0)=\int_{x_0}^xf(t)\,dt$, and $f(x_0)(x-x_0)=\int_{x_0}^xf(x_0)\,dt$. Hence

$$\begin{aligned}&\left|\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}-f(x_0)\right|\\&\quad\le \frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^x|f(t)-f(x_0)|\,dt\\&\quad\le \varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$

For $0<x_0-x<\delta$, $\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}=\frac1{x_0-x}\int_x^{x_0}f(t)\,dt$, and the same estimate on $[x,x_0]$ gives $\le\varepsilon$. As $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}\to f(x_0)$ for $x\to x_0$. ∎

Exercise 11 (Einsiedler HS21, question 10 (c) and (d), true or false)

Open the original: HS21 exam · solutions

Let $I\subseteq\mathbb R$ be a bounded interval and $f:I\to\mathbb R$. (c) If $f$ is continuous, then $f$ has an antiderivative. (d) If $f$ is monotone, then $f$ has an antiderivative.

Solution

(c) True. Fix $a\in I$. On every compact $[a,x]\subseteq I$ (or $[x,a]$), $f$ is continuous, hence integrable, and $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$ is an antiderivative by Proof A, at every point.

(d) False. Take $f=0$ on $[-1,0]$ and $f=1$ on $(0,1]$, which is monotone. Suppose $F'=f$ on $[-1,1]$. Derivatives have the intermediate value property (Darboux's theorem, lesson 17 Bonus): $F'$ takes the values $0$ and $1$, so it would also take the value $\frac12$. But $f$ never equals $\frac12$. So $f$ has no antiderivative. (Ladder rung 2 of Proof A shows the same thing concretely: $\int_{-1}^xf=\max(x,0)$ is not differentiable at $0$.)

Bonus: two one-point integrals with a twist (2017 summer exam, 1a and 1b)

Open the original: 2017 summer exam

(a) $\int_{-1}^1\frac{x-1}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}dx$. (b) $\int\frac{\log x}{x^{2017}}dx$ for $x>0$.

Solution

(a) Split: $\int_{-1}^1\frac x{\sqrt{x^2+1}}dx=0$, because the integrand is odd (substitute $x\mapsto-x$). The rest: $$\begin{aligned}-\int_{-1}^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}&=-\bigl[\operatorname{arsinh}x\bigr]_{-1}^1\\&=-2\operatorname{arsinh}1\\&=-2\log\left(1+\sqrt2\right),\end{aligned}$$using $\operatorname{arsinh}y=\log\left(y+\sqrt{y^2+1}\right)$.

(b) By parts with $u=\log x$, $v'=x^{-2017}$, $v=\frac{x^{-2016}}{-2016}$:

$$\begin{aligned}&\int\frac{\log x}{x^{2017}}dx\\&\quad=-\frac{\log x}{2016\,x^{2016}}+\frac1{2016}\int x^{-2017}dx\\&\quad=-\frac{\log x}{2016\,x^{2016}}-\frac1{2016^2x^{2016}}+C.\end{aligned}$$

11. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

A priority war. Newton found the fundamental theorem around 1666 but published little. Leibniz found it independently around 1675 and published in 1684, with the notation we still use: $\int$ is a stretched S for summa, and $dx$ the infinitely thin width. In 1712 a committee of the Royal Society, secretly led by Newton himself, declared Leibniz a plagiarist. The quarrel split British and continental mathematics for a century, and the British, loyal to Newton's clumsier dots, fell behind.

Integrals you can't write down. $\int e^{-x^2}dx$, $\int\frac{\sin x}x dx$ and $\int\frac{dx}{\log x}$ exist (the integrands are continuous), but Liouville proved in the 1830s that no combination of polynomials, roots, exp, log and trig functions is their antiderivative. They become new functions: the error function behind the bell curve of statistics, the sine integral of signal processing, and the logarithmic integral that counts prime numbers. Worked example 1 already differentiated one of them.

Archimedes' parabola. Around 250 BC Archimedes computed the area under a parabola with an ingenious exhaustion by triangles, a whole treatise of work. Today it is $\int_0^1x^2dx=\frac13$, one line.
