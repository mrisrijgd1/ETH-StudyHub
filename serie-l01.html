Serie 1, explained: Rings and fields

Four rings: subsets under symmetric difference and intersection, polynomials over $\mathbb Q$, the clock $\mathbb Z_m$ with multiplication, and a field with four elements built by hand. Which of them are fields, and why not the others?

You're done when
you can (1) list what a commutative ring needs beyond an abelian group (associative, commutative multiplication with a one, distributive law); (2) show that a ring is not a field by exhibiting one non-zero element without an inverse, or two non-zero elements with product $0$; (3) prove that $\mathbb Z_m$ is a field exactly when $m$ is prime; (4) check a small field from its addition and multiplication tables.

Aufgabe 6 · Subsets with $\triangle$ and $\cap$

As in Aufgabe 4, $\mathcal P(M)$ is the set of all subsets of $M$ and $x\mathbin\triangle y:=(x\setminus y)\cup(y\setminus x)$.

(a) Show: if $M$ is non-empty, then $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle,\cap)$ is a commutative ring.

(b) Show that $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle,\cap)$ is not a field as soon as $|M|\gt1$.

In short: Symmetric difference is the addition, intersection the multiplication. Zero is $\emptyset$, one is $M$. A proper non-empty subset has no inverse.

The idea

The addition is already done: Aufgabe 4 showed $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle)$ is an abelian group with zero $\emptyset$. Intersection is the multiplication. Its one is the whole set $M$, because $M\cap x=x$. The only real work is the distributive law, and it is proved like associativity in Aufgabe 4: fix an element $t$ and ask where it lies. For (b): intersecting can only make a set smaller, so a set $x\subsetneq M$ can never be multiplied up to $M$.

You need

Lesson 1, section 6: a commutative ring $(R,+,\cdot)$ is an abelian group $(R,+)$ with a second operation $\cdot$ that is associative and commutative, has a one ($1\cdot x=x$), and is distributive: $x\cdot(y+z)=x\cdot y+x\cdot z$. Lesson 1, section 7: a field is a commutative ring in which $(K\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is an abelian group. In particular $0\ne1$, every $x\ne0$ has an inverse, and a product of non-zero elements is non-zero. The membership rule from Serie 0, Aufgabe 4: $t\in u\mathbin\triangle v$ iff $t$ lies in exactly one of $u,v$.

Hint 1

Ring: (P(M),△) is a group by Aufgabe 4. For $\cap$ check closure, associativity, commutativity and the one ($M$). For the distributive law $x\cap(y\mathbin\triangle z)=(x\cap y)\mathbin\triangle(x\cap z)$, fix $t\in M$ and split into $t\notin x$ and $t\in x$.

Hint 2

If $t\in x$, then $t\in x\cap y$ iff $t\in y$, and $t\in x\cap z$ iff $t\in z$. So “exactly one of $x\cap y$, $x\cap z$” becomes “exactly one of $y,z$”.

Hint 3

(b) The one is $M$. Take $x=\{t_1\}$ with $t_1\ne t_2$ in $M$. What can $x\cap y$ be?

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

(a) The addition part is Aufgabe 4. Say what is left.

(a) By Aufgabe 4, $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle)$ is an abelian group with zero $\emptyset$. It remains to show that $\cap$ is an associative, commutative operation on $\mathcal P(M)$ with a one, and the distributive laws. We use: $t\in u\mathbin\triangle v$ iff $t$ lies in exactly one of $u,v$. $(*)$

Closure, associativity, commutativity of $\cap$: membership is “and”.

$x\cap y\subseteq x\subseteq M$, so $x\cap y\in\mathcal P(M)$. For $t\in M$: $t\in(x\cap y)\cap z$ iff $t\in x$ and $t\in y$ and $t\in z$ iff $t\in x\cap(y\cap z)$; and $t\in x\cap y$ iff $t\in y\cap x$.

The one: the whole set.

$M\in\mathcal P(M)$ and $M\cap x=x$ for every $x\subseteq M$.

Distributive law, case $t\notin x$: neither side contains $t$.

We show $x\cap(y\mathbin\triangle z)=(x\cap y)\mathbin\triangle(x\cap z)$. Let $t\in M$. If $t\notin x$: then $t\notin x\cap(y\mathbin\triangle z)$, and $t$ lies in neither $x\cap y$ nor $x\cap z$, so by $(*)$ $t\notin(x\cap y)\mathbin\triangle(x\cap z)$.

Case $t\in x$: intersecting with $x$ changes nothing for $t$.

If $t\in x$: then $t\in x\cap y\iff t\in y$ and $t\in x\cap z\iff t\in z$, so $$\begin{aligned}&t\in(x\cap y)\mathbin\triangle(x\cap z)\\\iff&t\text{ in exactly one of }y,z\\\iff&t\in y\mathbin\triangle z\\\iff&t\in x\cap(y\mathbin\triangle z).\end{aligned}$$

Conclude; the other distributive law follows by commutativity.

Both sides have the same elements, so they are equal. The law $(y\mathbin\triangle z)\cap x=(y\cap x)\mathbin\triangle(z\cap x)$ follows by commutativity of $\cap$. So $(\mathcal P(M),\triangle,\cap)$ is a commutative ring with zero $\emptyset$ and one $M$.

(b) In a field every non-zero element has an inverse. Find a non-zero element whose products never reach the one $M$.

(b) Let $t_1\ne t_2$ in $M$ and $x:=\{t_1\}$. Then $x\ne\emptyset$, and $x\ne M$ since $t_2\notin x$. For every $y\in\mathcal P(M)$: $x\cap y\subseteq x\subsetneq M$, so $x\cap y\ne M$. Thus $x$ has no multiplicative inverse, and the ring is not a field. ∎

Another way (also in the official solution): $\{t_1\}\cap\{t_2\}=\emptyset$ although both are non-zero. In a field a product of non-zero elements is never $0$. Remark: for $M=\{t\}$ the claim is false: $\mathcal P(M)=\{\emptyset,M\}$ is the field with two elements. That is why (b) needs $|M|\gt1$.

Trap

Taking $\emptyset$ as the one because it is “neutral” for $\triangle$. Each operation has its own neutral element: $\emptyset$ for $\triangle$ (the zero), $M$ for $\cap$ (the one). And in (b), showing only that $\emptyset$ has no inverse: zero never needs one.

Aufgabe 7 · Polynomials over $\mathbb Q$

Let $\mathbb Q[X]$ be the set of all polynomials $a_0+a_1X+\dots+a_nX^n$ with $n\in\mathbb N$ and $a_i\in\mathbb Q$.

(a) Show that $\mathbb Q[X]$ with the usual addition and multiplication of polynomials is a commutative ring.

(b) Show that $\mathbb Q[X]$ is not a field.

In short: Compute with coefficients: the $k$-th coefficient of $p\cdot q$ is $\sum_{i+j=k}a_ib_j$. Every ring law becomes a law in $\mathbb Q$. $X$ has no inverse.

The idea

A polynomial is just its list of coefficients, with only finitely many non-zero. Addition is coefficientwise, so the group laws come from $\mathbb Q$ exactly as for vectors. For multiplication, the $k$-th coefficient of $p\cdot q$ collects all products $a_ib_j$ with $i+j=k$. Every law then turns into a statement about such sums. For (b), look at the constant term: $X\cdot q$ always has constant term $0$, but $1$ has constant term $1$.

You need

Lesson 1, section 6: a commutative ring $(R,+,\cdot)$ is an abelian group $(R,+)$ with a second operation $\cdot$ that is associative and commutative, has a one ($1\cdot x=x$), and is distributive: $x\cdot(y+z)=x\cdot y+x\cdot z$. Lesson 1, section 7: a field is a commutative ring in which $(K\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is an abelian group. In particular $0\ne1$, every $x\ne0$ has an inverse, and a product of non-zero elements is non-zero. Polynomials and their coefficients: lesson 5, section 4.

Hint 1

Write $p=\sum_{i\ge0}a_iX^i$ with $a_i=0$ for all but finitely many $i$, and $(p)_k:=a_k$. Then $(p+q)_k=a_k+b_k$ and $(p\cdot q)_k=\sum_{i+j=k}a_ib_j$.

Hint 2

Associativity of $\cdot$: both $((p\cdot q)\cdot r)_k$ and $(p\cdot(q\cdot r))_k$ equal $\sum_{i+j+l=k}a_ib_jc_l$. (b): compute $(X\cdot q)_0$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Set up notation: a polynomial is its coefficient list, finitely many non-zero.

(a) Write $p=\sum_{i\ge0}a_iX^i$ with $a_i\in\mathbb Q$, $a_i=0$ for all but finitely many $i$, and $(p)_i:=a_i$. Two polynomials are equal iff all coefficients agree. For $q=\sum b_jX^j$: $$\begin{aligned}(p+q)_k&=a_k+b_k,\\(p\cdot q)_k&=\sum_{i+j=k}a_ib_j\\&=\sum_{i=0}^ka_ib_{k-i}.\end{aligned}$$

Closure: if $p$ stops at degree $n$ and $q$ at $l$, the sum stops at $\max$ and the product at $n+l$.

Let $a_i=0$ for $i\gt n$ and $b_j=0$ for $j\gt l$. For $k\gt\max\{n,l\}$, $a_k+b_k=0$. If $k\gt n+l$ and $i+j=k$, then $i\gt n$ or $j\gt l$, so $a_ib_j=0$ and $(p\cdot q)_k=0$. So $p+q,p\cdot q\in\mathbb Q[X]$.

$(\mathbb Q[X],+)$: coefficientwise, as for vectors.

For each $k$: $(a_k+b_k)+c_k=a_k+(b_k+c_k)$ and $a_k+b_k=b_k+a_k$. The zero polynomial is neutral, and $-p:=\sum(-a_i)X^i$ is the inverse of $p$.

Commutativity of $\cdot$: swapping $i$ and $j$ maps the index set to itself.

$(p\cdot q)_k=\sum_{i+j=k}a_ib_j=\sum_{i+j=k}b_ja_i=(q\cdot p)_k$.

Associativity of $\cdot$: both sides become one sum over triples.

For $r=\sum c_lX^l$, using the laws of $\mathbb Q$: $$\begin{aligned}&((p\cdot q)\cdot r)_k\\&=\sum_{s+l=k}\Big(\sum_{i+j=s}a_ib_j\Big)c_l\\&=\sum_{i+j+l=k}a_ib_jc_l,\\&(p\cdot(q\cdot r))_k\\&=\sum_{i+s=k}a_i\Big(\sum_{j+l=s}b_jc_l\Big)\\&=\sum_{i+j+l=k}a_ib_jc_l.\end{aligned}$$

The one: the constant polynomial $1$.

$1$ has $e_0=1$, $e_i=0$ for $i\ge1$, so $(1\cdot p)_k=\sum_{i+j=k}e_ia_j=e_0a_k=a_k$.

Distributive law, coefficientwise.

$(p\cdot(q+r))_k=\sum_{i+j=k}a_i(b_j+c_j)=\sum_{i+j=k}(a_ib_j+a_ic_j)=(p\cdot q+p\cdot r)_k$. The other law follows by commutativity. So $\mathbb Q[X]$ is a commutative ring.

(b) One non-zero element without an inverse is enough. Look at the constant coefficient.

(b) $X$ (with $a_1=1$, all other $a_i=0$) is not the zero polynomial. For any $q=\sum b_jX^j$: $(X\cdot q)_0=\sum_{i+j=0}a_ib_j=a_0b_0=0$, since $i+j=0$ forces $i=j=0$ and $a_0=0$. But $(1)_0=1\ne0$. So $X\cdot q\ne1$ for every $q$, and $\mathbb Q[X]$ is not a field. ∎

Another way (official remark): for non-zero $p,q$ with leading coefficients $a_n,b_l\ne0$, the coefficient of $X^{n+l}$ in $pq$ is $a_nb_l\ne0$, so $\deg(pq)=\deg p+\deg q$. Then $X\cdot q=1$ would give $0=1+\deg q\ge1$.

Trap

Saying “$\frac1X$ is the inverse”. $\frac1X$ is not a polynomial, and the question is exactly whether an inverse exists inside $\mathbb Q[X]$. Also: a statement like “$\mathbb Q[X]$ is not a field because it is infinite” is wrong ($\mathbb Q$ is infinite and a field).

Aufgabe 8 · The clock $\mathbb Z_m$ as a ring, and when it is a field

$(\mathbb Z_m,+_m)$ is as in Aufgabe 5. Define $\bar k\cdot_m\bar l:=\bar a$, where $0\le a\lt m$ and $mq+a=k\cdot l$ for some non-negative $q\in\mathbb Z$.

(a) Show that for any $\bar k,\bar l$ there is exactly one $a$ with $\bar k\cdot_m\bar l=\bar a$.

(b) Show that $(\mathbb Z_m,+_m,\cdot_m)$ is a commutative ring. Official hint: show $\bar n\cdot_m(\bar k+_m\bar l)=(\bar n\cdot_m\bar k)+_m(\bar n\cdot_m\bar l)$.

(c) Show: if $m$ is not prime, then $(\mathbb Z_m,+_m,\cdot_m)$ is not a field.

(d)* Show: if $m$ is prime, then $(\mathbb Z_m,+_m,\cdot_m)$ is a field. Official hint: the pigeonhole example in the notes of 23.09.2026.

In short: Multiply, then take the remainder. All laws come from $\mathbb Z$ through one helper: you may take remainders early. $\mathbb Z_6$ has $\bar2\cdot\bar3=\bar0$; for prime $m$, multiplying by $\bar k\ne\bar0$ is injective, hence hits $\bar1$.

The idea

Write $\rho(n)$ for the remainder of $n\ge0$ (Aufgabe 5). Then $\bar k\cdot_m\bar l=\overline{\rho(kl)}$, and everything follows from one helper: taking remainders early does not change the final remainder, for sums and for products. For (c): if $m=k\cdot l$ with $1\lt k,l\lt m$, then $\bar k\cdot\bar l=\bar0$, which never happens in a field. For (d): multiplying by a fixed $\bar k\ne\bar0$ is injective when $m$ is prime, and an injective map from a finite set to itself is surjective (pigeonhole), so something hits $\bar1$.

You need

Lesson 1, section 6: a commutative ring $(R,+,\cdot)$ is an abelian group $(R,+)$ with a second operation $\cdot$ that is associative and commutative, has a one ($1\cdot x=x$), and is distributive: $x\cdot(y+z)=x\cdot y+x\cdot z$. Lesson 1, section 7: a field is a commutative ring in which $(K\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is an abelian group. In particular $0\ne1$, every $x\ne0$ has an inverse, and a product of non-zero elements is non-zero. Serie 0, Aufgabe 5 (remainders). Lesson 4, section 5 (which clocks are fields) and lesson 3, section 7 (pigeonhole). A prime $p$ that divides a product $ab$ divides $a$ or $b$ (Euclid's lemma).

Hint 1

(a) is Aufgabe 5 (a) again, now with $n=kl\ge0$. Existence: among the numbers $n-mq\ge0$ take the smallest. (b) Helper: if $n=mQ+Y$ with $n,Y,Q\ge0$, then $\rho(n)=\rho(Y)$. Deduce $\rho(r+s)=\rho(\rho(r)+\rho(s))$ and $\rho(rs)=\rho(\rho(r)\rho(s))$.

Hint 2

(c) Two cases: $m=1$ ($\mathbb Z_1$ has only one element) and $m=kl$ with $1\lt k,l\lt m$. (d) Fix $\bar k\ne\bar0$ and look at $r_k:\bar x\mapsto\bar x\cdot_m\bar k$ on $\mathbb Z_m$. If $\bar x\bar k=\bar y\bar k$ with $x\ge y$, then $m$ divides $(x-y)k$.

Hint 3

(d) Since $m$ is prime and does not divide $k$, it divides $x-y$, and $0\le x-y\lt m$ forces $x=y$. So $r_k$ is injective on the finite set $\mathbb Z_m$, hence surjective; some $\bar c$ has $\bar c\cdot\bar k=\bar1$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

(a) Existence: the smallest non-negative number of the form $n-mq$ is the remainder.

(a) Let $0\le k,l\lt m$ and $n:=kl\ge0$. The set $A:=\{n-mq:q\in\mathbb Z,q\ge0,n-mq\ge0\}$ contains $n$, so it has a smallest element $a=n-mq_0$. If $a\ge m$, then $a-m=n-m(q_0+1)\in A$ is smaller, a contradiction. So $0\le a\lt m$ and $mq_0+a=n$.

Uniqueness exactly as in Aufgabe 5.

If $mq+a=n=mq'+a'$ with $0\le a,a'\lt m$, then $a-a'$ is a multiple of $m$ of absolute value $\lt m$, so $a=a'$ and $q=q'$. So $\cdot_m$ is a well-defined operation on $\mathbb Z_m$.

(b) Name the remainder $\rho(n)$ for any $n\ge0$; the proof of (a) used only $n\ge0$.

(b) For $n\ge0$ let $\rho(n)$ be the unique $a$ from (a). Then $\rho(n)=n$ for $0\le n\lt m$, and $\bar k+_m\bar l=\overline{\rho(k+l)}$, $\bar k\cdot_m\bar l=\overline{\rho(kl)}$.

Helper 1: adding multiples of $m$ does not change the remainder.

Helper 1. If $n=mQ+Y$ with $n,Y,Q\ge0$, then $\rho(n)=\rho(Y)$. Indeed $Y=mQ'+\rho(Y)$, so $n=m(Q+Q')+\rho(Y)$ with $0\le\rho(Y)\lt m$; uniqueness gives $\rho(n)=\rho(Y)$.

Helper 2: remainders may be taken early, in sums and products. Expand and apply Helper 1.

Helper 2. For $r,s\ge0$: $\rho(r+s)=\rho(\rho(r)+\rho(s))$ and $\rho(rs)=\rho(\rho(r)\rho(s))$. Write $r=mq_1+\rho(r)$, $s=mq_2+\rho(s)$. Then $r+s=m(q_1+q_2)+(\rho(r)+\rho(s))$ and $rs=m(mq_1q_2+q_1\rho(s)+q_2\rho(r))+\rho(r)\rho(s)$, all terms $\ge0$; apply Helper 1.

Commutativity and associativity of $\cdot_m$: both sides are the remainder of the same integer.

$\bar k\cdot_m\bar l=\overline{\rho(kl)}=\overline{\rho(lk)}=\bar l\cdot_m\bar k$. For $0\le k,l,n\lt m$: $(\bar k\cdot_m\bar l)\cdot_m\bar n=\overline{\rho(\rho(kl)\rho(n))}=\overline{\rho((kl)n)}$ and $\bar k\cdot_m(\bar l\cdot_m\bar n)=\overline{\rho(k(ln))}$, and $(kl)n=k(ln)$.

Distributive law (the official hint): both sides become $\rho(nk+nl)$.

$\bar n\cdot_m(\bar k+_m\bar l)=\overline{\rho(\rho(n)\rho(k+l))}=\overline{\rho(n(k+l))}=\overline{\rho(nk+nl)}$, and $(\bar n\cdot_m\bar k)+_m(\bar n\cdot_m\bar l)=\overline{\rho(\rho(nk)+\rho(nl))}=\overline{\rho(nk+nl)}$. The other law follows by commutativity.

The one. ($\mathbb Z_1=\{\bar0\}$ is the zero ring, where $\bar0$ is also the one.)

For $m\ge2$, $\bar1\cdot_m\bar k=\overline{\rho(k)}=\bar k$. For $m=1$, $\bar0$ is both zero and one. With Aufgabe 5, $(\mathbb Z_m,+_m,\cdot_m)$ is a commutative ring.

(c) A field has $0\ne1$ and no two non-zero elements with product $0$. Split $m=1$ and composite $m$.

(c) If $m=1$, $\mathbb Z_1=\{\bar0\}$ has zero $=$ one, but a field has at least two elements. If $m\ge2$ is not prime, $m=kl$ with $1\lt k,l\lt m$. Then $\bar k,\bar l\ne\bar0$, but $\bar k\cdot_m\bar l=\overline{\rho(m)}=\bar0$. Either way $\mathbb Z_m$ is not a field.

(d) What is left for a field: $\bar0\ne\bar1$, inverses, and closure of $\mathbb Z_m\setminus\{\bar0\}$. Start with inverses via pigeonhole (the official hint).

(d) Let $m$ be prime, so $m\ge2$ and $\bar0\ne\bar1$. Fix $\bar k\ne\bar0$, i.e. $1\le k\le m-1$, and let $r_k:\mathbb Z_m\to\mathbb Z_m$, $\bar x\mapsto\bar x\cdot_m\bar k$.

Injective: equal products mean $m$ divides $(x-y)k$; a prime that does not divide $k$ divides $x-y$.

If $\bar x\cdot_m\bar k=\bar y\cdot_m\bar k$ with $0\le y\le x\lt m$, then $xk$ and $yk$ have the same remainder, so $m$ divides $xk-yk=(x-y)k$. Since $m$ is prime and $m\nmid k$, $m\mid x-y$. With $0\le x-y\lt m$ this gives $x=y$. So $r_k$ is injective.

Pigeonhole: injective on a finite set means surjective. So $\bar1$ is hit.

An injective map from the finite set $\mathbb Z_m$ to itself is surjective (pigeonhole principle). So there is $\bar c$ with $\bar c\cdot_m\bar k=\bar1$: every $\bar k\ne\bar0$ has an inverse.

Closure: a product of non-zero elements is non-zero, else multiply by an inverse.

If $\bar k,\bar l\ne\bar0$ and $\bar k\cdot_m\bar l=\bar0$, then with $\bar c\bar k=\bar1$: $\bar l=(\bar c\bar k)\bar l=\bar c(\bar k\bar l)=\bar c\cdot_m\bar0=\bar0$, a contradiction. So $(\mathbb Z_m\setminus\{\bar0\},\cdot_m)$ is an abelian group, and $\mathbb Z_m$ is a field. ∎

The official route for (d): Bézout

The official solution builds the inverse directly. Let $d$ be the smallest positive number of the form $uk+vm$ ($u,v\in\mathbb Z$). Division with remainder shows that $d$ divides $k$ and $m$; since $m$ is prime and $m\nmid k$, $d=1$. So $uk+vm=1$, and after shifting $u$ by a multiple of $m$ to make it non-negative, $\overline{\rho(u)}$ is the inverse of $\bar k$. The official solution also mentions the pigeonhole route used above. Both are correct; the pigeonhole route is shorter and is the one the lecture hinted at.

Trap

In (d), using Euclid's lemma for $m$ but forgetting that it needs $m$ prime: in $\mathbb Z_6$, $\bar2\cdot\bar3=\bar0=\bar0\cdot\bar3$, so $r_3$ is not injective: $6$ divides $(2-0)\cdot3$ without dividing $2$. And in (c), forgetting the case $m=1$.

Aufgabe 9 · A field with four elements

Define on $K=\{0,1,a,b\}$ two operations $+$ and $\cdot$ such that $(K,+,\cdot)$ is a field with $0$ and $1$ as neutral elements for $+$ and $\cdot$.

In short: Not $\mathbb Z_4$! Addition: every element is its own negative. Multiplication: $a\cdot a=b$, $a\cdot b=1$, $b\cdot b=a$.

The idea

The obvious guess, the clock $\mathbb Z_4$, fails: $\bar2\cdot\bar2=\bar0$ (Aufgabe 8 (c)). The right field adds like two light switches ($\mathcal P(\{1,2\})$ under $\triangle$, so $x+x=0$) and multiplies like a three-hour clock on the non-zero elements ($1,a,b$ behave like $\bar0,\bar1,\bar2$ in $(\mathbb Z_3,+)$). Matching the tables to known groups proves the group axioms without checking all 64 triples; only the distributive law needs a short check by hand.

You need

Lesson 1, section 7: a field is a commutative ring in which $(K\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is an abelian group. In particular $0\ne1$, every $x\ne0$ has an inverse, and a product of non-zero elements is non-zero. Lesson 4, section 8 ($\mathbb F_4$). Serie 0, Aufgabe 4 and Aufgabe 5.

Hint 1

Not $\mathbb Z_4$: there $\bar2\cdot\bar2=\bar0$ (Aufgabe 8 (c)). Instead make every element its own negative, $x+x=0$ (a field with four elements must have characteristic 2, lesson 4, section 7). Then $1+a$ can only be $b$, and the addition table is fixed.

Hint 2

Multiplication on $\{1,a,b\}$ is a group with 3 elements, so it must be $a\cdot a=b$, $a\cdot b=1$, $b\cdot b=a$ (if $a\cdot a=1$, then $a\cdot b$ has nowhere to go). Then prove the group laws by matching with $\mathcal P(\{1,2\})$ and $\mathbb Z_3$, and check distributivity by cases.

Why this stepWhat you write

Define the two tables. Both are symmetric, so both operations are commutative; $0$ and $1$ are neutral by the first rows.

Define $+$ and $\cdot$ by the two tables above. Both tables are symmetric about the diagonal, so $+$ and $\cdot$ are commutative; $0$ is neutral for $+$ and $1$ for $\cdot$.

$(K,+)$: the table is the table of $\triangle$ on $\mathcal P(\{1,2\})$ under a renaming. Group laws transfer through a bijection.

Let $\varphi:K\to\mathcal P(\{1,2\})$, $\varphi(0)=\emptyset$, $\varphi(1)=\{1\}$, $\varphi(a)=\{2\}$, $\varphi(b)=\{1,2\}$. Comparing tables, $\varphi(x+y)=\varphi(x)\mathbin\triangle\varphi(y)$ for all $x,y$. Since $(\mathcal P(\{1,2\}),\triangle)$ is an abelian group (Serie 0, Aufgabe 4) and $\varphi$ is bijective, $(K,+)$ is one too; e.g. $\varphi((x+y)+z)=\varphi(x)\mathbin\triangle\varphi(y)\mathbin\triangle\varphi(z)=\varphi(x+(y+z))$, and $\varphi$ is injective.

$(K\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$: the table on $\{1,a,b\}$ is $(\mathbb Z_3,+_3)$ under a renaming.

Let $\psi:K\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb Z_3$, $\psi(1)=\bar0$, $\psi(a)=\bar1$, $\psi(b)=\bar2$. Then $\psi(x\cdot y)=\psi(x)+_3\psi(y)$ for all $x,y\ne0$ (e.g. $a\cdot b=1$ and $\bar1+\bar2=\bar0$). No $0$ appears in those rows, so $K\setminus\{0\}$ is closed, and as above $(K\setminus\{0\},\cdot)$ is an abelian group by Aufgabe 5.

Associativity of $\cdot$ on all of $K$: any product with a $0$ is $0$ on both sides.

Since $0\cdot x=x\cdot0=0$, if one of $x,y,z$ is $0$ then $(x\cdot y)\cdot z=0=x\cdot(y\cdot z)$.

Distributive law: reduce the cases, then check the six that are left.

We show $x(y+z)=xy+xz$. It holds if $x\in\{0,1\}$; if $y=z$ (both sides $0$, since $u+u=0$); if $y=0$ or $z=0$; and it is symmetric in $y,z$. Left: $x\in\{a,b\}$, $\{y,z\}\in\{\{1,a\},\{1,b\},\{a,b\}\}$:

$x$$y$$z$$x(y+z)$$xy+xz$
$a$$1$$a$$a\cdot b=1$$a+b=1$
$a$$1$$b$$a\cdot a=b$$a+1=b$
$a$$a$$b$$a\cdot1=a$$b+1=a$
$b$$1$$a$$b\cdot b=a$$b+1=a$
$b$$1$$b$$b\cdot a=1$$b+a=1$
$b$$a$$b$$b\cdot1=b$$1+a=b$

In each row the two results agree.

Finally $0\ne1$.

And $0\ne1$. So $(K,+,\cdot)$ is a field with four elements. ∎

Remark (official): $\{0,1\}$ is the field $\mathbb Z_2$, and $a\cdot a=b=a+1$, so $a$ is a zero of $X^2+X+1$. The field $\mathbb F_4$ is $\mathbb Z_2$ with such a zero added, like $\mathbb C$ is $\mathbb R$ with a zero of $X^2+1$ added.

Trap

Answering with $\mathbb Z_4$: it is a ring, but $\bar2\cdot\bar2=\bar0$, so $\bar2$ has no inverse. Also: giving tables without checking distributivity; the group laws alone do not make a field.
