Why it matters for the exam

Every recent exam's problem 2 is a throw or a motion along a track, and it starts exactly with this lesson: write $x(t)$ and $y(t)$, then use a condition (top of the flight, landing, meeting). The multiple-choice part has a block “Konzeptfragen: Kinematik”. No calculator is allowed, so answers are formulas, and numbers come out round (like $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$).

1. The story: the archers of the tower

On the wall of the old fortress stand the archers, and their captain has one rule: never guess where an arrow lands, predict it. Before each shot she writes two lines on her slate. One says how far the arrow travels along the ground as time passes, the other how high it is. The secret of the guild is that the two lines don't talk to each other. Along the ground the arrow keeps its pace forever; upwards, the earth pulls it down a little more every moment. Only the clock ties the two lines together: the arrow lands when the height line says zero, and at that moment the ground line tells you where.

The captain also trains the sling-throwers, who whirl stones in a circle before letting go. Their stones never fly in a straight line while whirling: something constantly pulls them towards the hand, and the faster they whirl, the harder the pull.

In physics: the arrow is a point mass, the two lines on the slate are the components $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ of the position vector, the earth's pull is the constant acceleration $g$, and the pull towards the hand is the centripetal acceleration.

2. Units and dimensions: the free check

Every physical quantity is a number times a unit, and its dimension (length, time, mass, …) is fixed (script, section 1.2). In the exam this is your free error check: both sides of every equation must have the same dimension. If your result for a height has the dimension of a time, there is a mistake upstream.

Worked example 1: check a formula in ten seconds

  1. Is $h=\dfrac{v_0^2\sin^2\alpha}{2g}$ a possible formula for a height?

    Answer

    $[v_0^2]=\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$, $[g]=\mathrm{m/s^2}$, and $\sin^2\alpha$ has no unit. So $[h]=\frac{\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}{\mathrm{m/s^2}}=\mathrm m$. A length: possible.

  2. Is $t=\dfrac{v_0^2}{g}$ a possible formula for a time?

    Answer

    $\frac{\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}{\mathrm{m/s^2}}=\mathrm m$, not $\mathrm s$. Impossible: the correct flight time of a throw is $\frac{2v_0\sin\alpha}g$, with dimension $\frac{\mathrm{m/s}}{\mathrm{m/s^2}}=\mathrm s$.

3. Position, velocity, acceleration

We describe a point mass by its position vector $\vec r(t)=\begin{pmatrix}x(t)\\y(t)\\z(t)\end{pmatrix}$ relative to an origin we choose (script, section 2.3). Then (sections 2.4–2.5):

  • mean velocity over $[t,t+\Delta t]$: $\langle\vec v\rangle=\dfrac{\Delta\vec r}{\Delta t}$; instantaneous velocity $\vec v(t)=\dot{\vec r}(t)=\dfrac{d\vec r}{dt}$;
  • acceleration $\vec a(t)=\dot{\vec v}(t)=\ddot{\vec r}(t)$.

The dot means the derivative with respect to time; you differentiate each component separately. Going backwards is integration: $\vec v(t)=\vec v(t_0)+\int_{t_0}^t\vec a(t')\,dt'$ and $\vec r(t)=\vec r(t_0)+\int_{t_0}^t\vec v(t')\,dt'$. This is lesson 19 of Analysis applied to each component.

Velocity and acceleration are vectors: a body accelerates when the size of its velocity changes, when its direction changes, or both. A car driving around a bend at a constant 50 km/h is accelerating.

4. Constant acceleration: free fall and the throw

If $\vec a(t)=\vec a_0$ is constant, integrating twice gives (script, equations (2.25)–(2.26)):

$$\vec v(t)=\vec v(t_0)+\vec a_0\,(t-t_0),\qquad \vec r(t)=\vec r(t_0)+\vec v(t_0)\,(t-t_0)+\tfrac12\vec a_0\,(t-t_0)^2.$$

This is a second-order differential equation in three dimensions, so the motion is fixed by $2\cdot3=6$ initial conditions: the three coordinates of $\vec r(t_0)$ and the three components of $\vec v(t_0)$. Free fall and the throw are the same formula with different initial conditions.

Near the earth's surface, with the $z$-axis (or $y$-axis) pointing up, $\vec a_0=\vec g=(0,0,-g)$ with $g\approx9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ (in exams usually $10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$).

The oblique throw (script, page 20). Thrown from height $z_0$ with speed $v$ at angle $\alpha$ to the horizontal, in the $xz$-plane, with $t_0=0$:

$$x(t)=v\cos\alpha\cdot t,\qquad z(t)=z_0+v\sin\alpha\cdot t-\tfrac12gt^2.$$

The two components are independent; only the time links them. Eliminating $t=\frac x{v\cos\alpha}$ gives the trajectory $z(x)=z_0+x\tan\alpha-\frac{g}{2v^2\cos^2\alpha}x^2$: a parabola, the Wurfparabel.

Recipe: any throw problem
  1. Draw axes and choose the origin (usually the launch point, $y$ upwards).
  2. Write the six initial conditions (in the plane: four) as components: $x_0,y_0,v_{x0}=v_0\cos\alpha,v_{y0}=v_0\sin\alpha$.
  3. Write $x(t)$, $y(t)$ and, if needed, $v_x(t)=v_{x0}$, $v_y(t)=v_{y0}-gt$.
  4. Turn the question into a condition: top of the flight $\Leftrightarrow v_y=0$; landing $\Leftrightarrow y=0$ (with $t>0$); two bodies meet $\Leftrightarrow$ same $x$ and same $y$ at the same $t$.
  5. Solve the condition for $t$, then put that $t$ into the other component.
  6. Check the dimension, and put numbers in only at the end.

In short: axes, components, condition, time, insert.

Worked example 2: height, flight time and range on flat ground

A stone is thrown from the ground ($x_0=y_0=0$) with speed $v_0$ at angle $\alpha$. Find the time to the top, the maximum height $h$, the flight time $T$ and the range $w$. Which angle gives the largest range?

  1. Write the components and the vertical velocity.

    Answer

    $x(t)=v_0\cos\alpha\,t$, $y(t)=v_0\sin\alpha\,t-\frac12gt^2$, $v_y(t)=v_0\sin\alpha-gt$.

  2. Top of the flight: which condition, and what are $t_{\text{top}}$ and $h$?

    Answer

    $v_y=0$ gives $t_{\text{top}}=\frac{v_0\sin\alpha}g$. Inserting into $y$:

    $$h=v_0\sin\alpha\cdot\frac{v_0\sin\alpha}g-\frac g2\cdot\frac{v_0^2\sin^2\alpha}{g^2}=\frac{v_0^2\sin^2\alpha}{2g}.$$

  3. Landing: which condition, and what are $T$ and $w$?

    Answer

    $y(T)=0$ with $T>0$: $T\left(v_0\sin\alpha-\frac g2T\right)=0$, so $T=\frac{2v_0\sin\alpha}g=2t_{\text{top}}$ (the flight is symmetric). Then

    $$w=x(T)=v_0\cos\alpha\cdot\frac{2v_0\sin\alpha}g=\frac{v_0^2\sin2\alpha}g,$$

    using $2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha=\sin2\alpha$.

  4. Which angle maximises $w$?

    Answer

    $\sin2\alpha\le1$ with equality for $2\alpha=90^\circ$, so $\alpha=45^\circ$ and $w_{\max}=\frac{v_0^2}g$. Angles $\alpha$ and $90^\circ-\alpha$ give the same range, since $\sin(180^\circ-2\alpha)=\sin2\alpha$.

5. Circular motion

A point mass moving on a circle of radius $r$ around the origin, in the $xy$-plane (script, section 2.5.3):

$$\vec r(t)=r\begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi(t)\\\sin\varphi(t)\\0\end{pmatrix}.$$
  • Angular velocity $\omega=\dot\varphi$. For uniform circular motion $\varphi(t)=\omega t+\varphi_0$, with period $T=\frac{2\pi}\omega$ and frequency $f=\frac1T$.
  • Differentiating: $\vec v(t)=r\omega\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\varphi\\\cos\varphi\\0\end{pmatrix}$, tangent to the circle, with speed $v=\omega r$.
  • Differentiating again (uniform case): $\vec a(t)=-\omega^2\vec r(t)$. It points to the centre, with size $a=\omega^2r=\frac{v^2}r$: the centripetal acceleration.
  • As a vector, $\vec\omega$ is perpendicular to the plane of the circle (right-hand rule), and $\vec v=\vec\omega\times\vec r$ (section 2.5.4).
  • If $\omega$ changes: angular acceleration $\alpha=\dot\omega=\ddot\varphi$ (section 2.5.5). For constant $\alpha$: $\varphi(t)=\varphi_0+\omega_0t+\frac12\alpha t^2$, exactly like the throw formula.

Any curved path (section 2.5.6). Write $\vec v=v\,\vec e_\parallel$ with the unit vector $\vec e_\parallel$ along the motion. Then

$$\vec a=\dot v\,\vec e_\parallel+\frac{v^2}\rho\,\vec e_\perp:$$

the tangential acceleration $\dot v$ changes the speed, the normal acceleration $\frac{v^2}\rho$ changes the direction, where $\rho$ is the radius of the circle that best fits the path at that point (the radius of curvature). On a circle, $\rho=r$.

Worked example 3: a speeding-up circle (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.2(c))

A point mass moves on $x(t)=R\cos(c_0t^2)$, $y(t)=R\sin(c_0t^2)$ with constants $R,c_0>0$. How does the speed $|\vec v(t)|$ depend on $t$, and what is its acceleration?

  1. What is $\varphi(t)$, and what is $\omega(t)$?

    Answer

    $\varphi(t)=c_0t^2$, so $\omega(t)=\dot\varphi=2c_0t$: the circle is run faster and faster.

  2. The speed?

    Answer

    $|\vec v(t)|=\omega(t)R=2Rc_0t$: a straight line through the origin in a $|v|$–$t$ graph. (Check by differentiating: $\dot x=-2Rc_0t\sin(c_0t^2)$, $\dot y=2Rc_0t\cos(c_0t^2)$, so $|\vec v|^2=4R^2c_0^2t^2$.)

  3. Tangential and normal acceleration?

    Answer

    Tangential: $\dot v=2Rc_0$, constant. Normal: $\frac{v^2}R=4Rc_0^2t^2$, towards the centre, growing with $t^2$.

Why it's cool. The two-line trick (components that ignore each other, tied only by time) is why a dropped bullet and a fired bullet hit level ground at the same moment, and why aiming straight at a falling target always works: gravity pulls the arrow and the target down by exactly the same amount. Exercise 3 below is an exam question built on this.

6. Exercises

Do them on paper. Numbers only at the end, $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, no calculator: leave $\sqrt2$ standing.

Exercise 1 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.1(a), 1 point)

You throw a stone on the earth and on the moon with identical initial values $v_{x0},v_{y0}>0$ and $x_0=y_0=0$, where $g_{\text{earth}}=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $g_{\text{moon}}=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Which is correct? (A) The impact speed is the same on the earth and the moon. (B) The maximum height is the same. (C) The range is the same. (D) The time in the air is the same.

Solution

A. On landing $y=0$, so by Worked example 2 the flight lasts $T=\frac{2v_{y0}}g$ and then $v_y(T)=v_{y0}-gT=-v_{y0}$, while $v_x=v_{x0}$ throughout. So the impact speed is $\sqrt{v_{x0}^2+v_{y0}^2}$, independent of $g$. The others all depend on $g$: $h=\frac{v_{y0}^2}{2g}$, $w=\frac{2v_{x0}v_{y0}}g$, $T=\frac{2v_{y0}}g$ are each 5 times larger on the moon.

Exercise 2 (HS23, problem 2 (a)–(c), 5 points)

Ball 1 is thrown from the ground ($y=0$) with $v_0=20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ at $\alpha=45^\circ$. Neglect air resistance; $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

(a) At horizontal distance $d=20\,\mathrm m$, ball 1 reaches its maximum height $h$, at time $\Delta t_1$ after the throw. Compute $h$ and $\Delta t_1$.

(b) Ball 2 is thrown straight up from the ground at $x=d$, with the same speed $v_0$, and is meant to hit ball 1 from below at the top of its flight. How long, $\Delta t_2$, is ball 2 in the air before it hits ball 1?

(c) Give the $x$- and $y$-components of both velocities just before the collision.

Solution

Frame: axes with origin at the launch point of ball 1, $y$ upwards; ball 1: $$v_{x0}=v_{y0}=v_0\cos45^\circ=\frac{v_0}{\sqrt2}=10\sqrt2\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

(a) Top of the flight: $v_y(\Delta t_1)=0$, so $\Delta t_1=\frac{v_0\sin\alpha}g=\frac{10\sqrt2}{10}\,\mathrm s=\sqrt2\,\mathrm s$. Height: $h=\frac{v_0^2\sin^2\alpha}{2g}=\frac{400\cdot\frac12}{20}\,\mathrm m=10\,\mathrm m$. (Check: $x(\Delta t_1)=10\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2\,\mathrm m=20\,\mathrm m=d$. ✓)

(b) Ball 2: $y_2(t)=v_0t-\frac12gt^2$, with $t$ measured from its throw. It must reach $y_2=h$ on the way up:

$$20t-5t^2=10\iff t^2-4t+2=0\iff t=2\pm\sqrt2.$$

On the way up means the earlier time, so $\Delta t_2=(2-\sqrt2)\,\mathrm s\approx0.59\,\mathrm s$. (Ball 2 must therefore be thrown $\Delta t_1-\Delta t_2=(2\sqrt2-2)\,\mathrm s$ after ball 1.)

(c) Ball 1 at the top: $v_{1x}=10\sqrt2\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $v_{1y}=0$. Ball 2: $v_{2x}=0$, $$v_{2y}=v_0-g\Delta t_2=20-10(2-\sqrt2)=10\sqrt2\,\mathrm{m/s},$$upwards.

The rest of the problem (the collision) comes in the lesson on momentum.

Exercise 3 (HS25, problem 2 (f)–(g), 4 points)

A clay disc is thrown from $x=0$, $y=0$ with speed $v_0$ at angle $\beta$ above the horizontal, towards positive $x$. From the other side, at $x=x_0$, $y=0$, in the same plane, you fire a projectile with speed $v_s$ at angle $\gamma$ above the horizontal, towards the disc. Assume both leave at $t=0$.

(f) Write $x_T(t)$, $y_T(t)$ for the disc and $x_S(t)$, $y_S(t)$ for the projectile. (g) Give the place $x$ and the time $t$ at which the projectile hits the disc.

Solution

(f) The disc moves towards $+x$, the projectile towards $-x$:

$$\begin{aligned}x_T(t)&=v_0\cos\beta\,t, & y_T(t)&=v_0\sin\beta\,t-\tfrac12gt^2,\\ x_S(t)&=x_0-v_s\cos\gamma\,t, & y_S(t)&=v_s\sin\gamma\,t-\tfrac12gt^2.\end{aligned}$$

(g) A hit means the same $x$ and the same $y$ at the same time. Same $x$:

$$v_0\cos\beta\,t=x_0-v_s\cos\gamma\,t\iff t=\frac{x_0}{v_0\cos\beta+v_s\cos\gamma},$$

and then $x=x_T(t)=\dfrac{v_0\cos\beta\;x_0}{v_0\cos\beta+v_s\cos\gamma}$. Same $y$: the $-\frac12gt^2$ terms cancel, leaving $v_0\sin\beta\,t=v_s\sin\gamma\,t$, so for $t>0$ the hit needs $v_0\sin\beta=v_s\sin\gamma$: both must start with the same vertical velocity. Gravity drops both by the same amount, so it doesn't matter at all. (Points come from setting both conditions up; the hit must also happen before either body lands, that is, $y_T(t)\ge0$.)

Exercise 4 (circular motion, a quick one)

A point on the rim of a wheel of radius $r=0.5\,\mathrm m$ turns 2 times per second. Find $\omega$, the speed $v$ and the centripetal acceleration $a$, and compare $a$ with $g$.

Solution

$f=2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $\omega=2\pi f=4\pi\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Speed $v=\omega r=2\pi\,\mathrm{m/s}\approx6.3\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Acceleration $$a=\omega^2r=16\pi^2\cdot0.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}=8\pi^2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\approx79\,\mathrm{m/s^2},$$about $8g$ (using $\pi^2\approx10$), pointing to the axle.

Scoring yourself: Exercise 1 (1), Exercise 2 (5) and Exercise 3 (4) make 10 points. With 7 of 10 you're done. Exercise 4 is a warm-up for the lesson on forces.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Why $45^\circ$ is not the real answer. With air resistance the best angle for a thrown ball drops below $45^\circ$, because a flatter flight spends less time fighting the air. Shot-putters also release lower, at roughly $35$–$40^\circ$: the ball starts above the ground, and their arms push harder at flatter angles. The clean $45^\circ$ is the answer of the idealised model, and much of physics is knowing which idealisation you're allowed.

Galileo's trick. Galileo was the first to treat the horizontal and vertical motion separately, around 1600. He couldn't time a falling stone accurately, so he rolled balls down gentle ramps, “diluting” gravity, and timed them with a water clock. The distances grew like $1:3:5:7$ in equal times: the odd numbers, which add up to the squares $1,4,9,16$, exactly $\frac12at^2$.
