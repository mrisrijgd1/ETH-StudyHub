1. Warm-up (5 min)

Three quick questions. Answer each in your head, then open it.

  1. Negate: “every dragon in the kingdom has a name”.

    Answer

    “There is a dragon in the kingdom without a name.” The “every” becomes “there is”, and only the last part is negated.

  2. $f:\mathbb N\to\mathbb N$, $f(n)=n+1$. Injective? Surjective?

    Answer

    Injective: $n+1=m+1$ gives $n=m$. Not surjective: $1$ has no preimage, since $n+1\ge2$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$.

  3. On the set of all people, is “has the same birthday as” an equivalence relation?

    Answer

    Yes: it is a “same ___” relation, so it is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. The classes are the 366 possible birthdays (the ones that occur).

2. Recap card (10 min)

Read this once, slowly. Everything in bold must come out exactly in the exam.

Lesson 1: quantifiers

  • $\forall$: the Doubter chooses; $\exists$: the Champion chooses; read left to right.
  • Negation: $\neg(\forall x:\ A(x))\iff\exists x:\ \neg A(x)$ and $\neg(\exists x:\ A(x))\iff\forall x:\ \neg A(x)$. Order and ranges stay; only the quantifier types and the final condition change.
  • $\exists y\,\forall x$ (uniform) is stronger than $\forall x\,\exists y$ (pointwise).
  • “$\forall x\in\emptyset:\ A(x)$” is always true.

Lesson 2: functions

  • Injective: $$\forall x_1,x_2\in X:\ f(x_1)=f(x_2)\Rightarrow x_1=x_2.$$Surjective: $\forall y\in Y\ \exists x\in X:\ f(x)=y$. Bijective: both.
  • Templates: “Let $x_1,x_2\in X$ with $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$. … Hence $x_1=x_2$.” and “Let $y\in Y$. Set $x:=\dots$ Then $f(x)=y$.”
  • $g\circ f$ injective $\Rightarrow$ $f$ injective; $g\circ f$ surjective $\Rightarrow$ $g$ surjective.
  • Image $f(A)=\{f(x)\mid x\in A\}$, preimage $f^{-1}(B)=\{x\in X\mid f(x)\in B\}$. The preimage respects $\cup$, $\cap$ and complements; the image only $\cup$ (and $\cap$ too if $f$ is injective).

Lesson 3: equivalence relations and countability

  • Equivalence relation: reflexive, symmetric, transitive (each with its $\forall$).
  • Class $[x]=\{y\in X\mid y\sim x\}$; quotient $X/\!\sim$; $$x\sim y\iff[x]=[y]\iff[x]\cap[y]\neq\emptyset,$$so the classes form a partition.
  • Countable: there is a bijection $\mathbb N\to X$. $\mathbb Z$, $\mathbb N\times\mathbb N$, $\mathbb Q$ are countable; $\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ is uncountable (diagonal argument).
  • Pigeonhole: if $|X|>|Y|$ (finite), no map $X\to Y$ is injective.

4. Write it on paper: three exam proofs (20 min)

For each proof: read the claim and the frame, close your eyes for ten seconds, then write the whole proof on paper without looking at the lesson. Only then open the fold and mark yourself with the grader's list.

Proof 1: if $g\circ f$ is injective, then $f$ is injective

Claim. Let $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to Z$ be maps. If $g\circ f$ is injective, then $f$ is injective. (Figalli FS26, A1(a), 2 points.)

What the definitions force

First line: “Let $x_1,x_2\in X$ with $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$.” Last line: “Hence $x_1=x_2$, so $f$ is injective.” The gap: reach the equation that the assumption on $g\circ f$ can use.

The proof and the grader's list

Let $x_1,x_2\in X$ with $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$. Applying $g$ to both sides gives $g(f(x_1))=g(f(x_2))$, that is $(g\circ f)(x_1)=(g\circ f)(x_2)$. Since $g\circ f$ is injective, $x_1=x_2$. Hence $f$ is injective. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • starting with the injectivity template for $f$ (not for $g$ or $g\circ f$);
  • the step $g(f(x_1))=g(f(x_2))$, which is allowed because $g$ is a map;
  • naming where the assumption is used (“since $g\circ f$ is injective”) and the concluding sentence.

Proof 2: same remainder mod $m$ is an equivalence relation

Claim. Let $m\in\mathbb N$. On $\mathbb Z$ define $a\sim b\iff m\mid a-b$, that is, $a-b=mk$ for some $k\in\mathbb Z$. Then $\sim$ is an equivalence relation, and it has exactly the $m$ classes $[0],[1],\dots,[m-1]$. (Lesson 3, Proof A with $3$ replaced by $m$; the class count was Rung 2 there and is on your skip list.)

What the definitions force

Three labelled bullets (reflexive, symmetric, transitive). Each starts with “Let …”, unfolds $\sim$ with a fresh letter, computes, and folds back. For the classes: every $a$ lies in one of $[0],\dots,[m-1]$, and these are pairwise different.

The proof and the grader's list

Reflexive. Let $a\in\mathbb Z$. Then $a-a=0=m\cdot0$, so $a\sim a$.

Symmetric. Let $a\sim b$, so $a-b=mk$ with $k\in\mathbb Z$. Then $b-a=m(-k)$ with $-k\in\mathbb Z$, so $b\sim a$.

Transitive. Let $a\sim b$ and $b\sim c$, so $a-b=mk$ and $b-c=ml$ with $k,l\in\mathbb Z$. Adding gives $a-c=m(k+l)$ with $k+l\in\mathbb Z$, so $a\sim c$.

The classes. Let $a\in\mathbb Z$. Division with remainder gives $a=mq+r$ with $q\in\mathbb Z$ and $r\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}$, so $a-r=mq$, $a\sim r$ and $a\in[r]$. So every integer lies in one of $[0],\dots,[m-1]$. These are pairwise different: for $0\le r<s\le m-1$ we have $0<s-r<m$, so $m\nmid s-r$, so $r\not\sim s$ and $[r]\neq[s]$. Hence $\mathbb Z/\!\sim$ has exactly $m$ elements. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • three separate, labelled bullets, each starting with “Let …”;
  • a fresh letter ($k$, $l$) for each unfolded assumption, with “$\in\mathbb Z$” checked when folding back;
  • for the classes: both halves (every $a$ is in some $[r]$, and the $[r]$ are different).

Proof 3: no list, even with repetitions, contains all A/B-sequences

Claim. There is no surjective map $f:\mathbb N\to\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$. In particular $\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ is uncountable. (Lesson 3, Proof C in its stronger form; this was Rung 2 there and is on your skip list.)

What the definitions force

“There is no” calls for a contradiction: “Suppose $f:\mathbb N\to\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ is surjective.” The goal: a sequence $s$ with $s\neq f(n)$ for every $n$, so $f$ is not surjective.

The proof and the grader's list

Suppose, for contradiction, that $f:\mathbb N\to\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ is surjective, and write $f(n)=(f(n)_1,f(n)_2,\dots)$. Define $s\in\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$ by $s_n=B$ if $f(n)_n=A$, and $s_n=A$ if $f(n)_n=B$. Then for every $n\in\mathbb N$ we have $s_n\neq f(n)_n$, so $s\neq f(n)$. Hence $s$ is not in the image of $f$, contradicting surjectivity. So no surjection exists.

In particular there is no bijection $\mathbb N\to\{A,B\}^{\mathbb N}$, and the set is infinite (it contains the pairwise different sequences $BAAA\dots$, $ABAA\dots$, $AABA\dots$, …), so it is uncountable. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • the contradiction assumption written out, with the notation $f(n)_k$ for the entries;
  • the definition of $s$ by the diagonal, case by case;
  • the sentence “$s_n\neq f(n)_n$, so $s\neq f(n)$ for every $n$” and the contradiction named.

5. Spot the mistake (5 min)

A student hands in this “proof”. The claim is false (Worked example 1 of lesson 2 shows it), so the proof must break somewhere. Where exactly?

“Claim.” If $g\circ f$ is injective, then $g$ is injective.

“Proof.” Let $y_1,y_2\in Y$ with $g(y_1)=g(y_2)$. Write $y_1=f(x_1)$ and $y_2=f(x_2)$ with $x_1,x_2\in X$. Then $(g\circ f)(x_1)=(g\circ f)(x_2)$, so $x_1=x_2$ because $g\circ f$ is injective. Hence $y_1=f(x_1)=f(x_2)=y_2$, so $g$ is injective. ∎

The mistake

The second sentence. Writing $y_1=f(x_1)$ assumes that $y_1$ has a preimage under $f$, that is, that $f$ is surjective (or at least that $y_1,y_2\in f(X)$). Nothing in the assumption gives that.

The counterexample shows exactly this gap: $f:\mathbb R_{\ge0}\to\mathbb R$, $f(x)=x$, and $g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, $g(y)=y^2$. Then $g\circ f$ is injective on $\mathbb R_{\ge0}$, but $g(1)=g(-1)$, and $-1$ is not of the form $f(x)$. The argument is correct if $f$ is surjective: then $g$ is injective too.

New exercise 1 (true/false, exam format)

True or false? Give a one-line reason or a counterexample. (a) $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, $f(x)=x^3$, is bijective. (b) For every map $f:X\to Y$ and every $B\subseteq Y$: $f(f^{-1}(B))=B$. (c) On $\mathbb R$, $x\sim y\iff x-y\in\mathbb Q$ is an equivalence relation. (d) The set of all finite A/B-strings is uncountable.

Solution

(a) True. Injective: $x\mapsto x^3$ is strictly increasing, so $x_1\neq x_2$ gives $x_1^3\neq x_2^3$. Surjective: for $y\in\mathbb R$ take $x=\sqrt[3]{y}$ (the real cube root, which exists by completeness; lesson 7 gives it via the intermediate value theorem).

(b) False. Counterexample: $f:\{1\}\to\{0,1\}$, $f(1)=0$, $B=\{0,1\}$. Then $f^{-1}(B)=\{1\}$ and $f(f^{-1}(B))=\{0\}\neq B$. In general $f(f^{-1}(B))=B\cap f(X)$, so equality holds exactly when $B\subseteq f(X)$.

(c) True. Reflexive: $x-x=0\in\mathbb Q$. Symmetric: $x-y\in\mathbb Q$ gives $y-x=-(x-y)\in\mathbb Q$. Transitive: $x-y,\ y-z\in\mathbb Q$ give $x-z=(x-y)+(y-z)\in\mathbb Q$.

(d) False. List the strings by length: first the empty string, then the 2 strings of length 1, then the 4 of length 2, and so on. Each length has finitely many strings, so every string gets a place in the list (the same idea as the diagonals of $\mathbb N\times\mathbb N$). The set is countable.

New exercise 2 (negation)

A sequence $(a_n)$ is bounded if $\exists M>0\ \forall n\in\mathbb N:\ |a_n|\le M$. Write the negation with $\neg$ pushed all the way to the right, say it in one sentence, and use it to show that $a_n=n$ is not bounded.

Solution

Negation: $\forall M>0\ \exists n\in\mathbb N:\ |a_n|>M$. In words: whatever bound you propose, some term exceeds it.

For $a_n=n$: let $M>0$. By the Archimedean principle there is $n\in\mathbb N$ with $n>M$, so $|a_n|=n>M$. Hence $(a_n)$ is not bounded.
