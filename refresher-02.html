1. Warm-up (5 min)

Three quick questions. Answer each in your head, then open it.

  1. Negate: “if a knight swears an oath, the oath is kept”.

    Answer

    “Some knight swears an oath, and the oath is not kept.” The negation of $A\Rightarrow B$ is $A\wedge\neg B$: an implication is false only when the promise is made and broken.

  2. Which proof method fits “there is no largest prime”?

    Answer

    Contradiction: “there is no …” almost always starts with “Suppose, for contradiction, that there is a largest prime $p$.”

  3. $X=(0,1]$. Is $\sup X$ a maximum? And is $\inf X$ a minimum?

    Answer

    $\sup X=1\in X$, so $1=\max X$. $\inf X=0\notin X$, so there is no minimum.

2. Recap card (10 min)

Read this once, slowly. Everything in bold must come out exactly in the exam.

Lesson 4: implication, proof methods, induction, axioms

  • Implication: $A\Rightarrow B$ is false only when $A$ is true and $B$ is false. So $\neg(A\Rightarrow B)\iff A\wedge\neg B$, and $(A\Rightarrow B)\iff(\neg B\Rightarrow\neg A)$ (contrapositive). The converse $B\Rightarrow A$ is a different statement.
  • Three weapons: direct; contrapositive (“Suppose $\neg B$. … Hence $\neg A$.”); contradiction (“Suppose, for contradiction, … This contradicts …”).
  • Induction: base case $A(1)$; induction step “Let $n\in\mathbb N$ and assume $A(n)$. … Hence $A(n+1)$.” Write the target $A(n+1)$ down before you start.
  • Completeness axiom (16): if $X,Y\subseteq\mathbb R$ are non-empty and $x\le y$ for all $x\in X$, $y\in Y$, then there is $c\in\mathbb R$ with $x\le c\le y$ for all $x\in X$, $y\in Y$. $\mathbb Q$ fails it (the gap at $\sqrt2$).

Lesson 5: complex numbers, absolute value, open and closed

  • $\bar z=x-yi$, $z\bar z=x^2+y^2=|z|^2$; $\overline{z+w}=\bar z+\bar w$, $\overline{zw}=\bar z\,\bar w$. To divide, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the bottom.
  • Rule (e): $|a|\le b\iff-b\le a\le b$. Triangle inequality: $|x+y|\le|x|+|y|$; reverse: $\big||x|-|y|\big|\le|x-y|$.
  • Open: $U\subseteq\mathbb R$ is open if $\forall x\in U\ \exists\varepsilon>0:\ (x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq U$. Closed: $\mathbb R\setminus A$ is open. Sets can be neither (e.g. $[0,1)$).

Lesson 6: supremum and completeness

  • Supremum: $s_0=\sup X$ means (1) $\forall x\in X:\ x\le s_0$ and (2′) $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists x\in X:\ x>s_0-\varepsilon$. Every non-empty set bounded above has one (Satz 2.59). It is a maximum exactly when $s_0\in X$.
  • Recipe: guess $s_0$; prove (1) for an arbitrary $x$; prove (2′) by naming an explicit $x$ (often via Archimedes); check whether $s_0\in X$.
  • Archimedes: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $n\in\mathbb N$ with $\frac1n<\varepsilon$. $\mathbb Q$ is dense: between any two reals $a<b$ there is a rational number.

Sums and binomial coefficients (script §3.1 and §3.3)

  • $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n k=\frac{n(n+1)}2$, and for $q\neq1$: $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^n q^k=\frac{1-q^{n+1}}{1-q}$. Both are proved by induction.
  • $\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$, and $\binom nk+\binom n{k+1}=\binom{n+1}{k+1}$ (Pascal's triangle).
  • Binomial theorem: $(x+y)^n=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk x^k y^{n-k}$.

4. Write it on paper: three exam proofs (20 min)

For each proof: read the claim and the frame, close your eyes for ten seconds, then write the whole proof on paper without looking at the lesson. Only then open the fold and mark yourself with the grader's list.

Proof 1: $\sqrt3$ is irrational

Claim. There is no $x\in\mathbb Q$ with $x^2=3$. (Lesson 4, Proof B with $2$ replaced by $3$; this was Rung 2 there and is a ★ row on your skip list.)

What the definitions force

“There is no” calls for a contradiction: “Suppose $\left(\frac pq\right)^2=3$ with $p,q\in\mathbb Z$, $q\neq0$, fully reduced.” You need a small lemma first, the replacement for “$n^2$ even $\Rightarrow$ $n$ even”: if $3\mid n^2$, then $3\mid n$. Prove it by contrapositive.

The proof and the grader's list

Lemma. Let $n\in\mathbb Z$. If $3\mid n^2$, then $3\mid n$.

Proof (contrapositive). Suppose $3\nmid n$. Then $n=3k+1$ or $n=3k+2$ with $k\in\mathbb Z$. In the first case $n^2=9k^2+6k+1=3(3k^2+2k)+1$; in the second $n^2=9k^2+12k+4$ $=3(3k^2+4k+1)+1$. In both cases $n^2$ leaves remainder $1$ when divided by $3$, so $3\nmid n^2$.

Claim. Suppose, for contradiction, that $\left(\frac pq\right)^2=3$ with $p,q\in\mathbb Z$, $q\neq0$, and $p,q$ without common factor. Then $p^2=3q^2$, so $3\mid p^2$, and by the lemma $3\mid p$: $p=3m$ with $m\in\mathbb Z$. Substituting gives $9m^2=3q^2$, so $q^2=3m^2$, so $3\mid q^2$ and by the lemma $3\mid q$. So $3$ divides both $p$ and $q$, contradicting that they have no common factor. Hence no rational $x$ has $x^2=3$. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • the lemma, with both cases $n=3k+1$ and $n=3k+2$ computed (not just “similarly”);
  • the contradiction assumption with “fully reduced” (no common factor) written explicitly;
  • using the lemma twice, and naming the contradiction at the end.

Proof 2: the triangle inequality

Claim. For all $x,y\in\mathbb R$: $|x+y|\le|x|+|y|$. (Lesson 5, Proof A; rule (g) of Definition 2.45 in the script.)

What the definitions force

The only tool for $|\cdot|$ is rule (e), $|a|\le b\iff-b\le a\le b$. So the goal is the same as $-(|x|+|y|)\le x+y\le|x|+|y|$. Two pieces of information ($x$ and $y$) must be combined into one about $x+y$: add inequalities.

The proof and the grader's list

Since $|x|\le|x|$, rule (e) with $a=x$, $b=|x|$ gives $-|x|\le x\le|x|$. In the same way $-|y|\le y\le|y|$. Adding the two chains (inequalities may be added, axiom (14)) gives $-(|x|+|y|)\le x+y\le|x|+|y|$. By rule (e) with $a=x+y$ and $b=|x|+|y|$, this means $|x+y|\le|x|+|y|$. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • the reason for $-|x|\le x\le|x|$ (rule (e)), not just “obviously”;
  • adding the inequalities, with the reason;
  • reading the sandwich back with rule (e) in the right direction.

Proof 3: compute $\sup X$ and prove it

Claim. Let $X=\left\{1-\tfrac1n : n\in\mathbb N\right\}$. Then $\sup X=1$, and $X$ has no maximum. (Lesson 6, Worked example 2; exactly the exam recipe.)

What the definitions force

Two labelled parts: (1) “Let $x\in X$, so $x=1-\frac1n$ for some $n\in\mathbb N$ … hence $x\le1$.” (2′) “Let $\varepsilon>0$. … So $x:=\dots\in X$ satisfies $x>1-\varepsilon$.” Then one sentence on the maximum: is $1\in X$?

The proof and the grader's list

(1) $1$ is an upper bound. Let $x\in X$, so $x=1-\frac1n$ with $n\in\mathbb N$. Since $\frac1n>0$, we get $x<1$, in particular $x\le1$.

(2′) Nothing smaller is an upper bound. Let $\varepsilon>0$. By the Archimedean principle there is $n\in\mathbb N$ with $\frac1n<\varepsilon$. Then $x:=1-\frac1n\in X$ and $x>1-\varepsilon$.

By (1) and (2′), $\sup X=1$. No maximum: $1\in X$ would need $1-\frac1n=1$, that is $\frac1n=0$, which is impossible. So $\sup X\notin X$, and $X$ has no maximum. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • both conditions, labelled, with (2′) starting “Let $\varepsilon>0$”;
  • an explicit element of $X$ in (2′), with Archimedes named as the reason;
  • the sentence on why $1\notin X$.

5. Spot the mistake (5 min)

A student hands in this “proof”. The claim is false ($n=1$ gives $1^2+1=2$), so the proof must break somewhere. Where exactly?

“Claim.” For every $n\in\mathbb N$, the number $n^2+n$ is odd.

“Proof.” Let $n\in\mathbb N$ and assume $n^2+n$ is odd. Then $(n+1)^2+(n+1)=(n^2+n)+2(n+1)$. This is an odd number plus an even number, so it is odd. By induction, $n^2+n$ is odd for every $n\in\mathbb N$. ∎

The mistake

The induction step is correct, but there is no base case. Induction needs both: the step only says “if $A(n)$, then $A(n+1)$”, and without $A(1)$ the chain never starts. Here $A(1)$ is false ($1^2+1=2$ is even), so nothing follows. In fact $n^2+n=n(n+1)$ is always even.

Exam lesson: always write the base case as its own labelled line, even when it looks trivial.
