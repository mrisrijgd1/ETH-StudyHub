The Wanderer's Return

The Cauchy criterion proof is a full exam question.

Recap of lesson 11 (5 min)

  • Sandwich lemma: $a_n\le b_n\le c_n$ and $a_n,c_n\to A$ ⇒ $b_n\to A$.
  • Monotone convergence: a monotone sequence converges ⇔ it is bounded; an increasing one tends to $\sup\{a_n\}$. Proof: $S-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound, so $S-\varepsilon<a_N\le a_n\le S$ for $n\ge N$.
  • Tail suprema $S_n=\sup_{k\ge n}a_k$ decrease, tail infima $I_n=\inf_{k\ge n}a_k$ increase; $\limsup a_n=\lim S_n$, $\liminf a_n=\lim I_n$.
  • Two properties of $S=\limsup a_n$: (P1) eventually $a_n\le S+\varepsilon$; (P2) infinitely often $a_n>S-\varepsilon$.
  • Convergent ⇔ $\liminf=\limsup$ (for bounded sequences).

  1. Why is $(S_n)_n$ decreasing?

    Answer

    $\{a_k:k\ge n+1\}\subseteq\{a_k:k\ge n\}$, and a smaller set has a smaller (or equal) supremum: $S_n$ is an upper bound of the smaller set, so $S_{n+1}\le S_n$.

  2. What are the five steps for a recursive sequence $a_{n+1}=f(a_n)$?

    Answer

    Guess from the first terms; find the candidate limit from $A=f(A)$; prove monotone and bounded by induction; apply monotone convergence; pass to the limit in the recursion.

  3. Compute $\limsup$ and $\liminf$ of $(-1)^n\left(1+\frac1n\right)$.

    Answer

    Even terms $1+\frac1n\to1$, odd terms $-1-\frac1n\to-1$. So $\limsup=1$ and $\liminf=-1$.

1. The story: the wanderer's return

A wanderer is bound by a curse to walk forever inside a walled valley, one camp each night, never leaving the walls. He may wander anywhere, back and forth, without any pattern. The valley's cartographer claims: there is a spot he will come back near again and again, forever. Her proof: split the valley map in two halves. He spends infinitely many nights in the valley, so at least one half holds infinitely many of his camps. Split that half again, and again. The halves shrink to a single point, and every one of the shrinking pieces holds infinitely many camps. That point is where he keeps returning. That's Bolzano–Weierstrass: a bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence.

Without the walls the curse is different. A wanderer who walks steadily away and eventually stays beyond every milestone, however far, is said to go to infinity. Not every wanderer without walls does that: one can stroll back home every other night.

Finally, a caravan of pilgrims. After a while, any two of their later camps are close to each other, closer and closer as time goes on. Nobody has told them where the pilgrimage ends. Yet in the land of the real numbers, the elders say, such a caravan always arrives somewhere: there are no holes in the ground for it to fall into. That's the Cauchy criterion, and it's what “the real numbers are complete” really means.

In maths: the camps are the terms $a_n$, the walls are a bound $|a_n|\le M$, the spot he keeps returning to is an accumulation point, going to infinity is $a_n\to\infty$, and the caravan is a Cauchy sequence: $|a_m-a_n|$ small for all large $m,n$.

2. Subsequences and Bolzano–Weierstrass

You'll need these (lesson 10)
  • Subsequence: $(a_{n_k})_k$ with $n_1<n_2<\dots$, so $n_k\ge k$. A subsequence of a convergent sequence has the same limit.
  • Accumulation point of $(a_n)_n$: the limit of some subsequence. Criterion (Proposition 5.42): $A$ is an accumulation point ⇔ for every $\varepsilon>0$ and every $N$ there is $n\ge N$ with $|a_n-A|<\varepsilon$.

Satz 6.15 (p. 259). Let $(a_n)_n$ be a bounded real sequence. (i) The limit of every convergent subsequence lies in $[\liminf a_n,\limsup a_n]$. (ii) There is a subsequence converging to $\limsup a_n$, and one converging to $\liminf a_n$.

Bolzano–Weierstrass (the consequence everyone uses). Every bounded real sequence has a convergent subsequence.

So $\limsup$ is the largest accumulation point and $\liminf$ the smallest. For example $(-1)^n+\frac1n$ has accumulation points $1$ and $-1$, exactly its $\limsup$ and $\liminf$. Part (i) follows from the two properties: eventually $I-\varepsilon\le a_n\le S+\varepsilon$, so the limit of a convergent subsequence lies in $[I-\varepsilon,S+\varepsilon]$ for every $\varepsilon>0$.

Without “bounded”, it fails: $a_n=n$ has no convergent subsequence, because every subsequence is unbounded. Sequentially compact (p. 261): a set $K$ is sequentially compact if every sequence in $K$ has a subsequence converging to a point of $K$. By Bolzano–Weierstrass and Proposition 6.1, every closed interval $[a,b]$ is sequentially compact. $(0,1]$ is not: $\frac1n$ has only the limit $0\notin(0,1]$.

Proof A: the limsup is an accumulation point (Satz 6.15 (ii))

The idea. We check the criterion: after any day $N$, the sequence must come within $\varepsilon$ of $S=\limsup a_n$. Pick a later high-water mark $S_M$ that's already within $\varepsilon$ of $S$ (they settle at $S$). The water comes within $\varepsilon$ of that mark after day $M$, and that brings it within $\varepsilon$ of $S$ from below, while the mark keeps it below $S+\varepsilon$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Goal (by the criterion): for every $\varepsilon>0$ and $N$, find $n\ge N$ with $S-\varepsilon<a_n<S+\varepsilon$.
  • Given: $S_n=\sup_{k\ge n}a_k$ decreases to $S=\inf_nS_n$.
  • The gap to fill: one index $M\ge N$ whose mark is close to $S$, then one term close to that mark.
The storyWhat you write

Fix a tolerance and a day.

Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $N\in\mathbb N$. Let $S=\limsup a_n$ and $S_m=\sup_{k\ge m}a_k$.

The marks settle at $S$, so some mark after day $N$ is already close to $S$.

Since $S_m\to S$ and $S_m\ge S$ for all $m$, there is $M\ge N$ with $S\le S_M<S+\varepsilon$.

The water really comes close to that mark after day $M$.

Since $S_M-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound of $\{a_k:k\ge M\}$, there is $n\ge M\ge N$ with $$S-\varepsilon\le S_M-\varepsilon<a_n\le S_M<S+\varepsilon.$$

So it comes back near $S$ after every day.

So $|a_n-S|<\varepsilon$ with $n\ge N$. By the accumulation-point criterion (every $\varepsilon$-window catches terms with arbitrarily large index), $S$ is an accumulation point, i.e. the limit of a subsequence. The same argument from below works for $\liminf$. ∎

Moves used: use convergence with a chosen ε approach a supremum

The proof in one piece

Let $S=\limsup a_n$, $S_m=\sup_{k\ge m}a_k$, $\varepsilon>0$ and $N\in\mathbb N$. Since $S_m$ decreases to $S$, there is $M\ge N$ with $S\le S_M<S+\varepsilon$. As $S_M-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound of $\{a_k:k\ge M\}$, there is $n\ge M$ with $S_M-\varepsilon<a_n\le S_M$, hence $S-\varepsilon<a_n<S+\varepsilon$. So for every $\varepsilon>0$ and $N$ there is $n\ge N$ with $|a_n-S|<\varepsilon$, and $S$ is an accumulation point by the accumulation-point criterion. In particular, every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Goal by the criterion: for all $\varepsilon,N$ some $n\ge N$ with $|a_n-S|<\varepsilon$.
  2. $S_M$ close to $S$ for some $M\ge N$ (the $S_m$ decrease to $S$).
  3. Approach the supremum $S_M$: $n\ge M$ with $S_M-\varepsilon<a_n\le S_M$.
  4. Chain: $S-\varepsilon<a_n<S+\varepsilon$.

In short: “a tail supremum $S_M$ close to $S$, a term close to $S_M$”. If an exam only needs Bolzano–Weierstrass as a step (as in the Cauchy criterion), you just cite it.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why do we need $M\ge N$ and not just any $M$ with $S_M<S+\varepsilon$?

    Answer

    The criterion needs an index $n\ge N$. We find $n\ge M$, so $M\ge N$ guarantees $n\ge N$. (Since the $S_m$ decrease, any $M$ that works can be replaced by $\max\{M,N\}$.)

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the chain $S-\varepsilon<a_n<S+\varepsilon$ and justify each inequality.

    Answer

    $S-\varepsilon\le S_M-\varepsilon$ since $S\le S_M$; $S_M-\varepsilon<a_n$ by the choice of $n$; $a_n\le S_M$ since $n\ge M$; $S_M<S+\varepsilon$ by the choice of $M$.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Second proof of Bolzano–Weierstrass: halving intervals (p. 260)

The idea is the cartographer's. Let $c_1\le a_n\le d_1$ for all $n$, and $n_1=1$.

  1. Split $[c_1,d_1]$ into two halves. At least one half contains $a_n$ for infinitely many $n$; call it $[c_2,d_2]$, and choose $n_2>n_1$ with $a_{n_2}\in[c_2,d_2]$.
  2. Repeat: $[c_{k+1},d_{k+1}]\subseteq[c_k,d_k]$ is a half containing infinitely many terms, and $n_{k+1}>n_k$ with $a_{n_{k+1}}\in[c_{k+1},d_{k+1}]$. (Infinitely many terms means we can always find a later index.)
  3. The lengths are $d_k-c_k=\frac{d_1-c_1}{2^{k-1}}$. By nested intervals (lesson 6) there is $A\in\bigcap_k[c_k,d_k]$.
  4. Both $A$ and $a_{n_k}$ lie in $[c_k,d_k]$, so $|a_{n_k}-A|\le\frac{d_1-c_1}{2^{k-1}}\to0$. So $a_{n_k}\to A$.

This proof doesn't tell you which accumulation point you get, but it generalises well (to $\mathbb R^d$ by halving in every coordinate).

3. Improper limits: going to infinity

Definition 6.19 (p. 262). A real sequence $(a_n)_n$ diverges to $\infty$, written $\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=\infty$ or $a_n\to\infty$, if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ with $a_n\ge\frac1\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$. It diverges to $-\infty$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ with $a_n\le-\frac1\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$. These are called improper limits.

Writing $K=\frac1\varepsilon$, this says: for every bound $K>0$ there is $N$ with $a_n\ge K$ for all $n\ge N$; the sequence eventually stays above every milestone. Such a sequence is still divergent (it has no limit in $\mathbb R$); “$\lim a_n=\infty$” is a precise way of saying how it diverges.

  • Example. $a_n=\frac{n^2}{n+1}\to\infty$: since $n+1\le2n$, we get $a_n\ge\frac{n^2}{2n}=\frac n2$. Given $K>0$, choose $N>2K$ (Archimedes); then $a_n\ge\frac n2>K$ for $n\ge N$.
  • Increasing and unbounded ⇒ $a_n\to\infty$. Given $K$, since $K$ is not an upper bound, some $a_N>K$, and then $a_n\ge a_N>K$ for all $n\ge N$. Together with lesson 11: every monotone sequence has a limit in $\mathbb R\cup\{\pm\infty\}$.
  • Reciprocals. If $a_n>0$ for all $n$, then $a_n\to\infty\iff\frac1{a_n}\to0$, because $a_n\ge\frac1\varepsilon\iff0<\frac1{a_n}\le\varepsilon$. (That's why the script writes the bound as $\frac1\varepsilon$.)
  • Improper sandwich (Übung 6.21). If $a_n\le b_n$ for all $n$ and $a_n\to\infty$, then $b_n\to\infty$: from $a_n\ge K$ follows $b_n\ge K$. Likewise $b_n\to-\infty$ implies $a_n\to-\infty$.
  • Unbounded is not the same as $\to\infty$. $a_n=1$ for even $n$ and $a_n=n$ for odd $n$ is unbounded, but doesn't diverge to $\infty$, since it returns to $1$ forever. Still, every unbounded sequence has a subsequence tending to $\infty$ or $-\infty$ (Übung 6.20, your Exercise 6).

4. Cauchy sequences

Definition 6.22 (p. 263). A sequence $(a_n)_n$ in a metric space $(X,d)$ is a Cauchy sequence if

$$\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\mathbb N\ \forall m,n\ge N:\ d(a_m,a_n)<\varepsilon.$$

In $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$: $|a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon$ for all $m,n\ge N$.

Compare with convergence, $|a_n-A|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. The Cauchy condition doesn't mention any limit $A$: it only compares terms of the sequence with each other. That's its power. To prove convergence from the definition you have to know the limit already; to prove the Cauchy property you don't. Note: both $m$ and $n$ are free, not just neighbours $m=n+1$ (see the warning at the end of the section).

Proof B: convergent sequences are Cauchy (Wichtige Übung 6.23)

The idea. If all late terms are within $\frac\varepsilon2$ of the limit $A$, then any two late terms are within $\varepsilon$ of each other, by going through $A$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” Last line: “so $|a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon$ for all $m,n\ge N$.”
  • The given: $a_n\to A$, used with a tolerance of our choice.
  • The gap: compare $a_m$ and $a_n$ through the middle point $A$.
The storyWhat you write

Late camps are all near the destination, within half the tolerance.

Let $a_n\to A$ and $\varepsilon>0$. There is $N$ with $|a_n-A|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $n\ge N$.

So two late camps are near each other, via the destination.

For $m,n\ge N$: $$|a_m-a_n|\le|a_m-A|+|A-a_n|<\tfrac\varepsilon2+\tfrac\varepsilon2=\varepsilon.$$ So $(a_n)_n$ is a Cauchy sequence. ∎

Moves used: ε/2 twice insert a middle point

The proof in one piece

Let $a_n\to A$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $|a_n-A|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $n\ge N$. For $m,n\ge N$: $|a_m-a_n|\le|a_m-A|+|A-a_n|<\varepsilon$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why $\frac\varepsilon2$ and not $\varepsilon$?

    Answer

    The triangle inequality adds two distances. With $\varepsilon$ we'd only get $|a_m-a_n|<2\varepsilon$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the proof from the frame alone. (It's three lines; this one appears inside the exam proof of the Cauchy criterion.)

Proof C: Cauchy sequences are bounded

The idea. Exactly lesson 10's “convergent ⇒ bounded”, with the term $a_N$ playing the role of the limit: from $N$ on, all terms are within $1$ of $a_N$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Last line: “so $|a_n|\le M$ for all $n$.” We must name $M$.
  • The given: the Cauchy condition with $\varepsilon=1$, and with $m=N$ fixed.
The storyWhat you write

From some night on, every camp is within $1$ of camp $N$.

With $\varepsilon=1$ there is $N$ with $|a_m-a_n|<1$ for $m,n\ge N$. With $m=N$: for $n\ge N$, $$|a_n|\le|a_n-a_N|+|a_N|<1+|a_N|.$$

The finitely many earlier camps have a farthest one.

Let $M=\max\{|a_1|,\dots,|a_{N-1}|,\ 1+|a_N|\}$. Then $|a_n|\le M$ for all $n$. ∎

Moves used: use the condition with a chosen ε insert a middle point

The proof in one piece

By the Cauchy condition with $\varepsilon=1$ there is $N$ with $|a_m-a_n|<1$ for $m,n\ge N$. For $n\ge N$, $|a_n|\le|a_n-a_N|+|a_N|<1+|a_N|$. So $$|a_n|\le M=\max\{|a_1|,\dots,|a_{N-1}|,1+|a_N|\}$$for all $n$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (2 min). Write this proof from the frame alone, and compare it with lesson 10's Proof C.

    Answer

    Same structure: $\varepsilon=1$, tail by the triangle inequality, head by a finite maximum. The only change: $a_N$ instead of the limit $A$.

Proof D: a Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges (Wichtige Übung 6.24)

The idea. Some subsequence arrives at $L$. Every late term of the whole sequence is close to the late terms of the subsequence (Cauchy), and those are close to $L$. So, going through one well-chosen subsequence term, every late term is close to $L$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Given: (1) Cauchy: $N$ with $|a_n-a_m|<\cdot$ for $m,n\ge N$; (2) $a_{n_k}\to L$: $K$ with $|a_{n_k}-L|<\cdot$ for $k\ge K$. Two ε-statements, so use $\frac\varepsilon2$ in each.
  • Last line: “so $|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.”
  • The gap: one subsequence term $a_{n_k}$ that is both “late” for the Cauchy condition ($n_k\ge N$) and “late” for the subsequence ($k\ge K$). Use $n_k\ge k$.
The storyWhat you write

Late camps are close to each other.

Let $(a_n)_n$ be Cauchy with $a_{n_k}\to L$, and let $\varepsilon>0$. There is $N$ with $|a_n-a_m|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $m,n\ge N$.

Late camps of the chosen group are close to $L$.

There is $K$ with $|a_{n_k}-L|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $k\ge K$.

Pick one camp of the group that is late in both senses.

Let $k=\max\{N,K\}$. Then $k\ge K$, and $n_k\ge k\ge N$.

Every late camp is near that one, and that one is near $L$.

For every $n\ge N$: $$\begin{aligned}|a_n-L|&\le |a_n-a_{n_k}|+|a_{n_k}-L|\\&<\tfrac\varepsilon2+\tfrac\varepsilon2\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$ So $a_n\to L$. ∎

Moves used: ε/2 twice take the larger N insert a middle point

The proof in one piece

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $|a_n-a_m|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $m,n\ge N$, and $K$ with $|a_{n_k}-L|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $k\ge K$. Fix $k=\max\{N,K\}$; then $n_k\ge k\ge N$. For all $n\ge N$: $|a_n-L|\le|a_n-a_{n_k}|+|a_{n_k}-L|<\varepsilon$. Hence $a_n\to L$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why does $n_k\ge N$ hold for $k=\max\{N,K\}$?

    Answer

    Indices of a subsequence satisfy $n_k\ge k$ (they're strictly increasing natural numbers starting at $n_1\ge1$), and $k\ge N$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the last line, with the triangle inequality, and say which given each half uses.

    Answer

    $$\begin{aligned}|a_n-L|&\le |a_n-a_{n_k}|+|a_{n_k}-L|\\&<\tfrac\varepsilon2+\tfrac\varepsilon2\end{aligned}.$$ The first term is small by the Cauchy condition ($n\ge N$ and $n_k\ge N$); the second by the subsequence's convergence ($k\ge K$).

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Proof E: the Cauchy criterion (Satz 6.26, p. 263)

Cauchy criterion (Satz 6.26). A real sequence converges if and only if it is a Cauchy sequence.

The idea. “⇒” is Proof B. For “⇐”, chain what we have: a Cauchy sequence is bounded (Proof C), so it has a convergent subsequence (Bolzano–Weierstrass), so it converges (Proof D). In an exam, you write Proof D out in full inside this proof.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Statement first: “A sequence $(a_n)_n$ in $\mathbb R$ converges ⇔ it is a Cauchy sequence”, with the definition of Cauchy written out.
  • Two directions. “⇒”: ε/2 through the limit. “⇐”: we have no candidate limit, so we must produce one, and the only tool that produces limits from boundedness alone is Bolzano–Weierstrass.
The storyWhat you write

“⇒” A caravan that arrives somewhere ends up camping close together.

“⇒”: Let $a_n\to A$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $|a_n-A|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $n\ge N$. For $m,n\ge N$: $|a_m-a_n|\le|a_m-A|+|A-a_n|<\varepsilon$.

“⇐” A caravan whose camps draw together stays inside some walls.

“⇐”: Let $(a_n)_n$ be Cauchy. With $\varepsilon=1$ there is $N_0$ with $|a_n-a_{N_0}|<1$ for $n\ge N_0$, so $|a_n|\le\max\{|a_1|,\dots,|a_{N_0-1}|,1+|a_{N_0}|\}$ for all $n$: the sequence is bounded.

Inside walls, the cartographer finds a spot it keeps returning to.

By Bolzano–Weierstrass there is a convergent subsequence $a_{n_k}\to L$.

Since the camps draw together, the whole caravan follows that group.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $|a_n-a_m|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $m,n\ge N$ and $K$ with $|a_{n_k}-L|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $k\ge K$. Let $k=\max\{N,K\}$, so $n_k\ge k\ge N$. For $n\ge N$: $$|a_n-L|\le|a_n-a_{n_k}|+|a_{n_k}-L|<\varepsilon.$$ Hence $a_n\to L$. ∎

Moves used: ε/2 twice insert a middle point take the larger N

The proof in one piece (exam version)

Claim. A sequence $(a_n)_n$ in $\mathbb R$ converges if and only if it is a Cauchy sequence, i.e. $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall m,n\ge N:\ |a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon$.

“⇒”: Let $a_n\to A$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $|a_n-A|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $n\ge N$. Then $|a_m-a_n|\le|a_m-A|+|A-a_n|<\varepsilon$ for $m,n\ge N$.

“⇐”: Let $(a_n)_n$ be Cauchy. For $\varepsilon=1$ choose $N_0$ with $|a_m-a_n|<1$ for $m,n\ge N_0$. Then $|a_n|<1+|a_{N_0}|$ for $n\ge N_0$, so $|a_n|\le\max\{|a_1|,\dots,|a_{N_0-1}|,1+|a_{N_0}|\}$ for all $n$, and $(a_n)_n$ is bounded. By Bolzano–Weierstrass it has a convergent subsequence $a_{n_k}\to L$. Let $\varepsilon>0$; choose $N$ with $|a_n-a_m|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $m,n\ge N$ and $K$ with $|a_{n_k}-L|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $k\ge K$. With $k=\max\{N,K\}$ we have $n_k\ge N$, so for all $n\ge N$: $|a_n-L|\le|a_n-a_{n_k}|+|a_{n_k}-L|<\varepsilon$. Hence $a_n\to L$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. State it, with the Cauchy definition in quantifiers.
  2. “⇒”: $\frac\varepsilon2$ through the limit $A$.
  3. “⇐” bounded: $\varepsilon=1$, tail within $1$ of $a_{N_0}$, head by a finite max.
  4. Bolzano–Weierstrass: a subsequence $a_{n_k}\to L$.
  5. Two ε/2's: $N$ from Cauchy, $K$ from the subsequence; $k=\max\{N,K\}$, $n_k\ge N$.
  6. Triangle through $a_{n_k}$: $|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$.

In short: “bounded, Bolzano–Weierstrass, then the whole sequence follows its subsequence”. Kobel-Keller's HS24 grading gave one point each for: bounded, convergent subsequence, the Cauchy ε/2, the subsequence ε/2, the max of the indices, and the final triangle inequality.

    Answer

    Cauchy ⇒ bounded; bounded ⇒ convergent subsequence (Bolzano–Weierstrass); Cauchy with a convergent subsequence ⇒ convergent.

  2. Rung 2 (3 min). Write the “⇐” direction, citing Bolzano–Weierstrass.

  3. Rung 3 (6 min). Write the whole proof, both directions, from the frame alone. This is Exercise 5.

Einsiedler's route for “⇐”, via limsup and liminf (p. 264)

The script proves “⇐” with lesson 11's tools. (Both versions are safe: the official HS21 grading gives the point for “using Bolzano–Weierstrass or limsup/liminf”.) A Cauchy sequence is bounded. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $|a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon$ for $m,n\ge N$, and fix $m=N$: then $a_N-\varepsilon<a_n<a_N+\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$. So every tail supremum and infimum from $N$ on lies in $[a_N-\varepsilon,a_N+\varepsilon]$, and in the limit $$a_N-\varepsilon\le\liminf a_n\le\limsup a_n\le a_N+\varepsilon.$$Hence $\limsup a_n-\liminf a_n\le2\varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon>0$, so they are equal, and the sequence converges by “convergent ⇔ $\liminf=\limsup$”. Both routes earn full marks; use the one you remember better.

The big picture

Warning: the “wrong Cauchy criterion” (Beispiel 6.27, p. 264). It is not enough that neighbouring terms get close, $|a_{n+1}-a_n|\to0$. Example: $a_n=\sqrt n$. Then $$\begin{aligned}a_{n+1}-a_n&=\frac{(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n)(\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n)}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n}\\&=\frac1{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n}\to0\end{aligned},$$ but $\sqrt n\to\infty$. Small steps can still add up to an infinite journey. The Cauchy condition controls all pairs $m,n\ge N$, however far apart.

5. Worked examples

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

Worked example 1: $a_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1{k^2}$ converges, without knowing the limit

  1. The sequence is increasing. To use the Cauchy criterion instead, what is $a_m-a_n$ for $m>n$?

    Answer

    $a_m-a_n=\sum_{k=n+1}^m\frac1{k^2}$, the terms from $n+1$ to $m$.

  2. For $k\ge2$, compare $\frac1{k^2}$ with $\frac1{k(k-1)}$, and write $\frac1{k(k-1)}$ as a difference.

    Answer

    $\frac1{k^2}\le\frac1{k(k-1)}=\frac1{k-1}-\frac1k$ (check: $\frac{k-(k-1)}{k(k-1)}$).

  3. Bound $|a_m-a_n|$ for $m>n\ge1$.

    Answer

    Telescoping: $$\begin{aligned}0&\le a_m-a_n\\&\le \sum_{k=n+1}^m\left(\frac1{k-1}-\frac1k\right)\\&=\frac1n-\frac1m\\&<\frac1n\end{aligned}.$$

  4. Finish: given $\varepsilon>0$, which $N$ works?

    Answer

    By Archimedes choose $N>\frac1\varepsilon$. For $m>n\ge N$: $|a_m-a_n|<\frac1n\le\frac1N<\varepsilon$ (and for $m=n$ it's $0$; for $m<n$ swap the roles). So $(a_n)_n$ is Cauchy and converges by the Cauchy criterion.

We never found the limit. (It is $\frac{\pi^2}6$; see For wonder.) This is exactly how chapter 7 will handle series.

Worked example 2: the harmonic numbers $H_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1k$ are not Cauchy

  1. Write the negation of “Cauchy”.

    Answer

    $$\begin{aligned}\exists\varepsilon&>0\ \forall N\ \exists m,n\ge N:\\&\quad|a_m-a_n|\ge \varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

  2. Estimate $H_{2n}-H_n$ from below.

    Answer

    $H_{2n}-H_n=\sum_{k=n+1}^{2n}\frac1k$ has $n$ terms, each $\ge\frac1{2n}$, so $H_{2n}-H_n\ge n\cdot\frac1{2n}=\frac12$.

  3. Conclude, and say what happens to $H_n$.

    Answer

    With $\varepsilon=\frac12$: for every $N$ take $n=N$ and $m=2N$; then $|H_m-H_n|\ge\frac12$. So $(H_n)_n$ is not Cauchy, hence divergent. Since it is increasing, it must be unbounded (bounded and monotone would converge), so $H_n\to\infty$.

Yet $H_{n+1}-H_n=\frac1{n+1}\to0$: another example of the wrong Cauchy criterion.

Why it's cool. The Cauchy criterion decides convergence from the terms alone, without knowing the limit. That is how $\mathbb R$ guarantees that limits exist (“no holes”), and it is why you can prove that $\sum\frac1{k^2}$ converges long before you learn that its value is $\frac{\pi^2}6$.

Exercise 1 (Figalli HS23, MC2)

Open the original: HS23 exam with solutions

Which statement is correct? (A) Every Cauchy sequence is monotone. (B) Every monotone sequence is bounded. (C) Every bounded and monotone sequence is a Cauchy sequence. (D) Every bounded sequence is convergent.

Solution

Answer (C). A bounded monotone sequence converges (monotone convergence theorem), and a convergent sequence is Cauchy (Proof B).

  • (A) False: $\frac{(-1)^n}n\to0$ is Cauchy but alternates in sign, so it is not monotone.
  • (B) False: $a_n=n$.
  • (D) False: $(-1)^n$.

Exercise 2 (Figalli FS24, MC8)

Open the original: FS24 exam with solutions

Let $(x_n)_n$ be a real sequence. Which statement is wrong? (A) If $(x_n)_n$ is bounded, then the $\limsup$ is an accumulation point. (B) If $(x_n)_n$ is bounded, then there is a convergent subsequence. (C) If $(x_n)_n$ has no accumulation points, then it is unbounded. (D) If $(x_n)_n$ is a Cauchy sequence, then it is monotone.

Solution

Answer (D) is wrong: $\frac{(-1)^n}n$ is Cauchy but not monotone.

  • (A) True: the limsup of a bounded sequence is an accumulation point (Proof A).
  • (B) True: Bolzano–Weierstrass.
  • (C) True: it's the contrapositive of Bolzano–Weierstrass. If the sequence were bounded, it would have a convergent subsequence, whose limit is an accumulation point.

Exercise 3 (Felder FS23, MC2)

Open the original: FS23 exam with solutions

Which statement about the real numbers is true? (A) $\mathbb R$ has no countably infinite subsets. (B) Every countably infinite subset of $\mathbb R$ is unbounded. (C) Every sequence in $\mathbb R$ has a convergent subsequence. (D) Every Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb R$ is bounded.

Solution

Answer (D). Proof C.

  • (A) False: $\mathbb N\subseteq\mathbb R$ is countably infinite.
  • (B) False: $\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]$ is countably infinite and bounded.
  • (C) False: $a_n=n$ has no convergent subsequence. Bolzano–Weierstrass needs “bounded”.

Exercise 4 (Felder HS22, MC2)

Open the original: HS22 exam

A sequence $(a_n)_n$ in $\mathbb C$ is called a Cauchy sequence if

  • (A) $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\mathbb N\ \forall n,m\in\mathbb N:$ $n,m\ge N\Rightarrow|a_n-a_m|<\varepsilon$;
  • (B) $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\mathbb N\ \forall n\in\mathbb N:$ $n\ge N\Rightarrow|a_n|<\varepsilon$;
  • (C) the series $\sum_{n=1}^\infty|a_n|$ converges;
  • (D) the limit $\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}$ exists.
Solution

Answer (A), the definition. (B) says $a_n\to0$, which is stronger than Cauchy ($a_n=1$ is Cauchy). (C) is the absolute convergence of a series (chapter 7) and fails for $a_n=1$. (D) is not the definition either: for $a_n=n$ the limit $\lim\sqrt[n]{n}=1$ exists (lesson 11), but $(n)_n$ is not Cauchy (it is unbounded).

Exercise 5 (Kobel-Keller HS24, question 16, 6 points)

Open the original: HS24 exam

Let $(a_n)_n$ be a Cauchy sequence of real numbers. Show that this sequence converges. (You may use Bolzano–Weierstrass.)

Solution

Frame: “converges” without a given limit, so first produce a candidate: bounded (Proof C), then Bolzano–Weierstrass gives a subsequence with limit $L$. Last line: “Hence $a_n\to L$.” The gap: from the subsequence to the whole sequence with $\frac\varepsilon2$ twice (Proof D).

Let $(a_n)_n$ be Cauchy.

Bounded. For $\varepsilon=1$ there is $N_0$ with $|a_m-a_n|<1$ for $m,n\ge N_0$. For $n\ge N_0$: $$|a_n|\le|a_n-a_{N_0}|+|a_{N_0}|<1+|a_{N_0}|.$$Hence for all $n$: $$\begin{aligned}|a_n|&\le M\\&=\max\{|a_1|,\dots,|a_{N_0-1}|,1+|a_{N_0}|\}\end{aligned}.$$

Convergent subsequence. By Bolzano–Weierstrass there is a subsequence $(a_{n_k})_k$ with $a_{n_k}\to L\in\mathbb R$.

The whole sequence converges to $L$. Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $(a_n)_n$ is Cauchy, there is $N$ with $|a_n-a_m|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $m,n\ge N$. Since $a_{n_k}\to L$, there is $K$ with $|a_{n_k}-L|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $k\ge K$. Let $k=\max\{N,K\}$; then $k\ge K$ and $n_k\ge k\ge N$. For all $n\ge N$:

$$\begin{aligned}|a_n-L|&\le |a_n-a_{n_k}|+|a_{n_k}-L|\\&<\tfrac\varepsilon2+\tfrac\varepsilon2\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

Hence $a_n\to L$, and the sequence converges. ∎

Moves used: Bolzano–Weierstrass for a candidate ε/2 twice take the larger N insert a middle point

Exercise 6 (improper limits)

(a) Show from the definition that $n-\sqrt n\to\infty$. (b) (Übung 6.20) Let $(a_n)_n$ be an unbounded real sequence. Show that it has a subsequence tending to $\infty$ or to $-\infty$.

Solution

(a) Frame: “$\to\infty$” means “Let $K>0$ … so $n-\sqrt n>K$ for $n\ge N$”. The gap: a simpler lower bound for $n-\sqrt n$, then choose $N$ by Archimedes. Moves: choose n by Archimedes.

For $n\ge4$ we have $\sqrt n\ge2$, so $n-\sqrt n=\sqrt n\left(\sqrt n-1\right)\ge\sqrt n$. Let $K>0$. By Archimedes choose $N\ge4$ with $N>K^2$. For $n\ge N$: $n-\sqrt n\ge\sqrt n\ge\sqrt N>K$. So $n-\sqrt n\to\infty$.

(b) Frame: a subsequence is built by choosing indices one at a time, $n_1<n_2<\dots$; the last line is “so $a_{n_k}\to\infty$” (or $-\infty$). The gap: show that each choice is always possible.

First, for every $K>0$ there are infinitely many $n$ with $|a_n|>K$: otherwise all $|a_n|$ with $n$ beyond the last such index are $\le K$, and the sequence would be bounded by the maximum of $K$ and finitely many values. So we can choose indices recursively: $n_1$ with $|a_{n_1}|>1$, and $n_{k+1}>n_k$ with $|a_{n_{k+1}}|>k+1$. Then $|a_{n_k}|>k$ for all $k$. Infinitely many of these $a_{n_k}$ are positive, or infinitely many are negative. In the first case, the positive ones form a subsequence with $a_{n_{k_j}}>k_j\ge j$, which tends to $\infty$ (improper sandwich). In the second case, the negative ones tend to $-\infty$ in the same way. ∎

Bonus (Übung 6.25)

Show that a sequence $(z_n)_n$ in $\mathbb C$ is Cauchy if and only if $(\operatorname{Re}z_n)_n$ and $(\operatorname{Im}z_n)_n$ are Cauchy. Conclude that the Cauchy criterion also holds in $\mathbb C$.

Solution

Frame: an “if and only if” is two directions; each one is “Let $\varepsilon>0$ … for $m,n\ge N$”. The gap: compare $|w|$ with its real and imaginary parts. Moves: insert a middle point ε/2 twice take the larger N.

The tool: three inequalities. Write $w=a+ib$ with $a=\operatorname{Re}w$ and $b=\operatorname{Im}w$. Then:

  • $|a|=\sqrt{a^2}\le\sqrt{a^2+b^2}=|w|$, because $b^2\ge0$ and the square root is increasing. In the same way, $|b|\le|w|$.
  • $$|w|=|a+ib|\le|a|+|ib|=|a|+|b|,$$by the triangle inequality in $\mathbb C$ and $|i|=1$.

We apply them to $w=z_m-z_n$. Then $\operatorname{Re}w=\operatorname{Re}z_m-\operatorname{Re}z_n$ and $\operatorname{Im}w=\operatorname{Im}z_m-\operatorname{Im}z_n$, because $\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$ are additive.

Part 1, “⇒”. Let $(z_n)_n$ be Cauchy, and let $\varepsilon>0$. There is $N$ with $|z_m-z_n|<\varepsilon$ for all $m,n\ge N$. For these $m,n$:

$$\begin{aligned}&|\operatorname{Re}z_m-\operatorname{Re}z_n|\le |z_m-z_n|<\varepsilon\\&\text{and}\\&|\operatorname{Im}z_m-\operatorname{Im}z_n|\le |z_m-z_n|<\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

The same $N$ works for both, so $(\operatorname{Re}z_n)_n$ and $(\operatorname{Im}z_n)_n$ are Cauchy.

Part 1, “⇐”. Let $(\operatorname{Re}z_n)_n$ and $(\operatorname{Im}z_n)_n$ be Cauchy, and let $\varepsilon>0$. Apply the definition with $\frac\varepsilon2$ to each: there is $N_1$ with $|\operatorname{Re}z_m-\operatorname{Re}z_n|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $m,n\ge N_1$, and there is $N_2$ with $|\operatorname{Im}z_m-\operatorname{Im}z_n|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $m,n\ge N_2$. Let $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$, so that both statements hold for $m,n\ge N$. For these $m,n$:

$$\begin{aligned}|z_m-z_n|&\le |\operatorname{Re}z_m-\operatorname{Re}z_n|+|\operatorname{Im}z_m-\operatorname{Im}z_n|\\&<\tfrac\varepsilon2+\tfrac\varepsilon2\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

So $(z_n)_n$ is Cauchy.

Part 2: the Cauchy criterion in $\mathbb C$. The claim is: a sequence $(z_n)_n$ in $\mathbb C$ converges if and only if it is a Cauchy sequence. Frame: again two directions. For “⇐” the first line is “Let $(z_n)_n$ be Cauchy”, and the last line is “so $|z_n-z|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$”, for a limit $z$ that we still have to find. The gap: get $z$ from the real criterion, applied to the real and imaginary parts.

“⇒” (convergent ⇒ Cauchy). Proof B works word for word in $\mathbb C$, because it only uses the triangle inequality for $|\cdot|$. In detail: let $z_n\to z$ and $\varepsilon>0$. There is $N$ with $|z_n-z|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $n\ge N$. For $m,n\ge N$:

$$\begin{aligned}|z_m-z_n|&\le |z_m-z|+|z-z_n|\\&<\tfrac\varepsilon2+\tfrac\varepsilon2\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

“⇐” (Cauchy ⇒ convergent). Let $(z_n)_n$ be Cauchy.

  1. The parts are Cauchy. By Part 1 “⇒”, $(\operatorname{Re}z_n)_n$ and $(\operatorname{Im}z_n)_n$ are Cauchy sequences of real numbers.
  2. The parts converge. By the Cauchy criterion in $\mathbb R$ (Proof E), there are $x,y\in\mathbb R$ with $\operatorname{Re}z_n\to x$ and $\operatorname{Im}z_n\to y$. This is the only place where completeness of $\mathbb R$ enters.
  3. The candidate. Let $z=x+iy$. Then $\operatorname{Re}(z_n-z)=\operatorname{Re}z_n-x$ and $\operatorname{Im}(z_n-z)=\operatorname{Im}z_n-y$.
  4. $z_n\to z$. Let $\varepsilon>0$. There is $N_1$ with $|\operatorname{Re}z_n-x|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $n\ge N_1$, and there is $N_2$ with $|\operatorname{Im}z_n-y|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $n\ge N_2$. Let $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$. For $n\ge N$, the third inequality applied to $w=z_n-z$ gives $$\begin{aligned}|z_n-z|&\le |\operatorname{Re}z_n-x|+|\operatorname{Im}z_n-y|\\&<\tfrac\varepsilon2+\tfrac\varepsilon2\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

Hence $z_n\to z$, and $(z_n)_n$ converges. Step 4 is exactly the statement from lesson 10 that convergence in $\mathbb C$ is convergence of the real and imaginary parts. In an exam, you may cite it instead of writing step 4. ∎

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

The Basel problem. Worked example 1 proves that $\sum\frac1{k^2}$ converges, but what is the limit? Mathematicians (the Bernoullis among them) tried for decades. In 1734 the 28-year-old Euler found it: $$1+\frac14+\frac19+\frac1{16}+\dots=\frac{\pi^2}6.$$ Why $\pi$, the number of circles, appears in a sum of squares of whole numbers is still one of the most surprising facts in mathematics.

Where the holes are. In $\mathbb Q$, the Cauchy criterion fails. The decimal truncations $1,\ 1.4,\ 1.41,\ 1.414,\dots$ of $\sqrt2$ are rationals and form a Cauchy sequence ($|a_m-a_n|<10^{-(N-1)}$ for $m,n\ge N$), but there is no rational limit. In 1872 Cantor turned this around and constructed $\mathbb R$ as Cauchy sequences of rationals, with two sequences identified when their difference tends to $0$. And Übung 6.28 shows that for an ordered field containing $\mathbb Q$ densely, “every Cauchy sequence converges” is equivalent to the completeness axiom you met in lesson 4. Completeness is one idea with three faces: suprema, nested intervals and Cauchy sequences.

The harmonic numbers grow, but barely. $H_n\to\infty$ (Worked example 2), yet so slowly that $H_{10^{6}}\approx14.39$. In fact $H_n-\ln n$ converges, to the Euler–Mascheroni constant $\gamma\approx0.5772$. Nobody knows whether $\gamma$ is rational.
