Why it matters for the exam

Linear Algebra I and II are examined together, and eigenvalues are in every combined exam. Typical problems: write a recursion as $v_{n+1}=Av_n$, find the characteristic polynomial, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and an explicit formula (HS18, problem 6, 15 points; Exercise 1); $AB$ and $BA$ are similar when $A$ is invertible, and have the same characteristic polynomial (FS20, problem 5, 15 points; Exercise 2); idempotent matrices are diagonalisable with eigenvalues in $\{0,1\}$ (FS19, problem 4 (a); Exercise 3). True/false: “invertible iff $n$ distinct eigenvalues” (HS24, MC2; Worked example 3).

1. The story: the roads that only stretch

A courier of the Free City carries every journey to a new place: that is a linear map $T:V\to V$. Most journeys are bent and turned on the way. But the surveyors notice that some roads are special: a journey along such a road arrives on the same road, only stretched (or shrunk, or reversed). On these roads the courier is as simple as a courier can be: multiply by a number.

If the city has enough such roads to form a basis, the whole courier is simple: in that basis its matrix is diagonal, and applying it a hundred times means raising a few numbers to the hundredth power. The shift on Fibonacci sequences (lessons 6 and 10) is exactly such a courier.

Two questions remain. Which stretching factors can occur, and on which roads? The determinant answers the first: $\lambda$ is a stretching factor exactly when $T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}$ squashes some road to nothing, i.e. when $\det(T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id})=0$. That is one polynomial equation. The second is a kernel, which lesson 17 taught us to compute.

In mathematics: $v\ne0$ is an eigenvector of $T$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ if $Tv=\lambda v$; the eigenvalues are the zeros of $p_A(x)=\det(A-xI_n)$; $T$ is diagonalisable iff $V$ has a basis of eigenvectors.

2. Eigenvectors, eigenvalues, diagonalisability

Definitions. Let $V$ be a $K$-vector space and $T\in\mathrm{End}(V)$. A vector $v\in V$ is an eigenvector of $T$ if $v\ne0$ and $Tv=\lambda v$ for some $\lambda\in K$; then $\lambda$ is the eigenvalue of $v$. The eigenspace of $\lambda\in K$ is $\mathrm{Eig}_T(\lambda):=\mathrm{Ker}(T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}_V)$. If $\dim V=n<\infty$, $T$ is diagonalisable if $V$ has a basis $B$ with $[T]_B$ diagonal. For $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ all these notions are those of $m_A:K^n\to K^n$, $x\mapsto Ax$.

Warnings. Eigenvectors are never $0$; eigenvalues may be $0$: $0$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ iff $\mathrm{Ker}\,T\ne\{0\}$, i.e. (for finite dimension) iff $T$ is not invertible. Eigenvalues depend on the field: the rotation by $90°$ has none over $\mathbb R$ (Exercise 4).

Examples. A diagonal matrix $\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$ has $Ae_i=\lambda_ie_i$. For $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$: $A\binom11=3\binom11$ and $A\binom1{-1}=1\cdot\binom1{-1}$, so $3$ and $1$ are eigenvalues.

Proof A: diagonalisable iff there is a basis of eigenvectors (script Lemmas 5.2.7 and 5.2.14, pp. 157 and 159)

Claim. Let $\dim V=n$. A basis $B=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ satisfies $[T]_B=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$ iff $Tv_i=\lambda_iv_i$ for all $i$. So $T$ is diagonalisable iff $V$ has a basis of eigenvectors. For a matrix $A$: if $v_1,\dots,v_n$ is a basis of eigenvectors of $K^n$ and $P$ is the matrix with columns $v_1,\dots,v_n$, then $P$ is invertible and $P^{-1}AP=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$.

The idea. Column $i$ of $[T]_B$ is $[Tv_i]_B$, the coordinates of the image of the $i$-th basis vector. It is $\lambda_ie_i$ exactly when $Tv_i=\lambda_iv_i$. For matrices, $AP$ has columns $Av_i=\lambda_iv_i$, which is $PD$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Column $i$ of $[T]_B$ is $[Tv_i]_B$ (lesson 11).”
  • Last line: “so $P^{-1}AP=D$.”
  • The gap: $[Tv_i]_B=\lambda_ie_i\iff Tv_i=\lambda_iv_i$; compare $AP$ and $PD$ column by column.
The storyWhat you write

The ledger of a courier records, road by road, where each basic road is sent.

Column $i$ of $[T]_B$ is $[Tv_i]_B$ (lesson 11). Since $[\cdot]_B$ is an isomorphism and $[v_i]_B=e_i$, we have $[Tv_i]_B=\lambda_ie_i$ iff $Tv_i=\lambda_iv_i$. So $[T]_B=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$ iff $Tv_i=\lambda_iv_i$ for all $i$; the $v_i$ are nonzero, being basis vectors, so they are eigenvectors.

For a matrix, put the special roads side by side.

Let $P=(v_1\,|\,\cdots\,|\,v_n)$. Its columns form a basis, so $P$ is invertible (lesson 17). Column $i$ of $AP$ is $Av_i=\lambda_iv_i$, and column $i$ of $PD$ is $P(\lambda_ie_i)=\lambda_iv_i$. So $AP=PD$, i.e. $P^{-1}AP=D$. ($P$ is the transition matrix from the eigenbasis to the standard basis, lesson 13.) ∎

Moves used: the matrix column by column

The proof in one piece

Column $i$ of $[T]_B$ is $[Tv_i]_B$, and $[Tv_i]_B=\lambda_ie_i$ iff $Tv_i=\lambda_iv_i$, because $[\cdot]_B$ is an isomorphism with $[v_i]_B=e_i$. So $[T]_B$ is diagonal iff every $v_i$ is an eigenvector. For a matrix with an eigenbasis $v_1,\dots,v_n$, let $P=(v_1|\cdots|v_n)$; it is invertible, and $AP$ and $PD$ both have columns $\lambda_iv_i$. Hence $P^{-1}AP=D$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Column $i$ of $[T]_B$ is $[Tv_i]_B$.
  2. Diagonal $\iff$ each $Tv_i$ is a multiple of $v_i$.
  3. For matrices: eigenvectors as the columns of $P$, eigenvalues on the diagonal of $D$, in the same order; check $AP=PD$.

In short: diagonal in a basis $=$ the basis consists of eigenvectors.

  1. Rung 1. For $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ give $P$ and $D$ with $P^{-1}AP=D$.
    Answer

    $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $D=\mathrm{diag}(3,1)$. Check: $AP=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\3&-1\end{pmatrix}=PD$ ✓.

  2. Rung 2. Why is $A^{100}=PD^{100}P^{-1}$, and why is that useful?
    Answer

    $A=PDP^{-1}$, and in $A^{100}=PDP^{-1}PDP^{-1}\cdots$ every $P^{-1}P$ cancels. $D^{100}$ is diagonal with entries $\lambda_i^{100}$: no matrix multiplication needed.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof A from the frame alone.

Proof B: eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent (script Proposition 5.2.16 and Corollary 5.2.17, pp. 159–160)

Claim. Let $v_1,\dots,v_m$ be eigenvectors of $T$ with pairwise distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_m$. Then $v_1,\dots,v_m$ are linearly independent. In particular, if $\dim V=n$ and $T$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues, then $T$ is diagonalisable.

The idea. Induction on $m$. Take a relation $\sum a_iv_i=0$. Apply $T$, and subtract $\lambda_m$ times the relation: $v_m$ disappears, and the other coefficients get multiplied by $\lambda_i-\lambda_m\ne0$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Induction on $m$. For $m=1$: $v_1\ne0$, so $\{v_1\}$ is independent.”
  • Step, first line: “Let $a_1v_1+\dots+a_mv_m=0$.” Last line: “so $a_1=\dots=a_m=0$.”
  • The gap: apply $T$; subtract $\lambda_m$ times the relation; use the hypothesis and $\lambda_i\ne\lambda_m$.
The storyWhat you write

One special road alone is never the zero road.

Induction on $m$. For $m=1$, $v_1\ne0$ (eigenvectors are nonzero), so $v_1$ is linearly independent.

If the roads with different stretching factors cancelled out, the courier would stretch the cancellation in two different ways at once.

Let $m\ge2$, assume the claim for $m-1$ vectors, and let $\sum_{i=1}^ma_iv_i=0$ (1). Applying $T$ gives $\sum_ia_i\lambda_iv_i=0$ (2). Subtracting $\lambda_m$ times (1) from (2): $\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}a_i(\lambda_i-\lambda_m)v_i=0$.

The shorter list is honest by induction, and the factors are nonzero, so every coefficient vanishes.

By the hypothesis, $a_i(\lambda_i-\lambda_m)=0$ for $i<m$, and $\lambda_i\ne\lambda_m$, so $a_i=0$ for $i<m$. Then (1) reads $a_mv_m=0$, and $v_m\ne0$ gives $a_m=0$. For the corollary: $n$ eigenvectors for $n$ distinct eigenvalues are $n$ independent vectors in an $n$-dimensional space, hence a basis (lesson 7), and Proof A applies. ∎

Moves used: induction: base, hypothesis, step · count first: n vectors in an n-dimensional space

The proof in one piece

Induction on $m$; $m=1$ holds as $v_1\ne0$. Let $\sum_{i=1}^ma_iv_i=0$. Applying $T$ and subtracting $\lambda_m$ times the relation gives $\sum_{i<m}a_i(\lambda_i-\lambda_m)v_i=0$, so $a_i(\lambda_i-\lambda_m)=0$ by the hypothesis, hence $a_i=0$ for $i<m$ as the eigenvalues are distinct. Then $a_mv_m=0$ and $a_m=0$. With $n$ distinct eigenvalues in dimension $n$ the eigenvectors form a basis, and $T$ is diagonalisable by Proof A. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Induction on the number of vectors.
  2. Relation; apply $T$; subtract $\lambda_m\cdot$relation.
  3. Hypothesis kills $a_1,\dots,a_{m-1}$; $v_m\ne0$ kills $a_m$.

In short: apply $T$ and subtract to remove one vector.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Is the converse of the corollary true: diagonalisable $\Rightarrow$ $n$ distinct eigenvalues?
    Answer

    No: $I_n$ is diagonal (so diagonalisable) with the single eigenvalue $1$.

  2. Rung 2. Show $\mathrm{Eig}_T(\lambda)\cap\mathrm{Eig}_T(\mu)=\{0\}$ for $\lambda\ne\mu$ directly.
    Answer

    If $v$ is in both, $\lambda v=Tv=\mu v$, so $(\lambda-\mu)v=0$ and $v=0$.

3. Finding eigenvalues: the characteristic polynomial

Proof C: eigenvalues are the zeros of $\det(A-\lambda I_n)$ (script Lemma 5.3.3 and Corollary 5.3.4, pp. 161–162)

Claim. Let $T\in\mathrm{End}(V)$, $\dim V=n$, and $\lambda\in K$. (1) $\mathrm{Eig}_T(\lambda)$ is a subspace, and its nonzero vectors are exactly the eigenvectors with eigenvalue $\lambda$. (2) $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ iff $\det(T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}_V)=0$; for a matrix, iff $\det(A-\lambda I_n)=0$.

The idea. $Tv=\lambda v$ is the same as $(T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id})v=0$. So the eigenvectors for $\lambda$ are the nonzero vectors of a kernel, and such vectors exist iff $T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}$ is not injective, iff its determinant vanishes.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “For $v\in V$: $Tv=\lambda v\iff(T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}_V)v=0$.”
  • Last line: “$\iff\det(T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}_V)=0$.”
  • The gap: kernels are subspaces; injective $\iff$ invertible in finite dimension; invertible $\iff\det\ne0$.
The storyWhat you write

A road is stretched by $\lambda$ exactly when the modified courier $T-\lambda$ squashes it.

(1) $Tv=\lambda v\iff Tv-\lambda v=0\iff(T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}_V)v=0$. So $\mathrm{Eig}_T(\lambda)=\mathrm{Ker}(T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}_V)$ consists of $0$ and the eigenvectors with eigenvalue $\lambda$, and it is a subspace as a kernel of a linear map (lesson 11).

Some road is squashed exactly when the modified courier has zero measure.

(2) $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue $\iff\mathrm{Ker}(T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}_V)\ne\{0\}$ $\iff T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}_V$ is not injective $\iff$ it is not invertible (finite dimension, lesson 15) $\iff$ its matrix $[T]_B-\lambda I_n$ in a basis $B$ is not invertible $\iff\det([T]_B-\lambda I_n)=0$ (lesson 18, Proof C). The determinant does not depend on $B$, since the matrices in two bases are similar (lesson 18, Exercise 7). ∎

Moves used: injective: check the kernel

The proof in one piece

$Tv=\lambda v\iff(T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}_V)v=0$, so $\mathrm{Eig}_T(\lambda)=\mathrm{Ker}(T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}_V)$ is a subspace whose nonzero vectors are the eigenvectors for $\lambda$. Hence $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue iff this kernel is nonzero, iff $T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}_V$ is not injective, iff (finite dimension) it is not invertible, iff $\det([T]_B-\lambda I_n)=0$. Similar matrices have equal determinants, so this does not depend on $B$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Rewrite $Tv=\lambda v$ as $(T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id})v=0$.
  2. Chain: kernel $\ne0$ $\iff$ not injective $\iff$ not invertible $\iff$ $\det=0$.

In short: eigenvalues are where $T-\lambda\,\mathrm{id}$ fails to be invertible.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Which step of the chain needs $\dim V<\infty$?
    Answer

    “Not injective $\iff$ not invertible”, and the use of a matrix. The shift $(a_0,a_1,\dots)\mapsto(a_1,a_2,\dots)$ on all sequences is surjective but not injective.

  2. Rung 2. Show: $0$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ iff $\det A=0$.
    Answer

    Take $\lambda=0$ in (2): $\det(A-0\cdot I_n)=\det A$.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof C from the frame alone.

Proof D: the characteristic polynomial (script Definition 5.3.5 and Lemma 5.3.6, pp. 162–163)

Claim. For $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ let $p_A(x):=\det(A-xI_n)$, computed with the Leibniz formula for the matrix $A-xI_n$ with entries in $K[x]$. Then:

  1. $p_A(x)=(-1)^nx^n+(-1)^{n-1}\mathrm{tr}(A)\,x^{n-1}+\dots+\det A$; in particular $\deg p_A=n$.
  2. For $\lambda\in K$, $p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I_n)$, so the eigenvalues of $A$ in $K$ are exactly the zeros of $p_A$ in $K$. There are at most $n$ of them.
  3. If $A$ is upper or lower triangular, $p_A(x)=\prod_i(a_{ii}-x)$.
  4. Similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial; so $p_T:=p_{[T]_B}$ is well defined for $T\in\mathrm{End}(V)$.

The idea. Only diagonal entries of $A-xI_n$ contain $x$. The identity permutation uses all $n$ of them; every other permutation moves at least two points, so it uses at most $n-2$ diagonal entries and contributes only to degrees $\le n-2$. For (4), $P^{-1}AP-xI_n=P^{-1}(A-xI_n)P$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “$p_A(x)=\sum_\sigma\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\prod_i(A-xI_n)_{i\sigma(i)}$, where $(A-xI_n)_{ii}=a_{ii}-x$ and $(A-xI_n)_{ij}=a_{ij}$ for $i\ne j$.”
  • Last line: “so $p_{P^{-1}AP}=p_A$.”
  • The gap: count fixed points; constant term $p_A(0)$; the product rule in the field of rational functions.
The storyWhat you write

The unknown stretch $x$ sits only on the diagonal, and only the seating that keeps everybody in place uses the whole diagonal.

(1) The term of $\sigma=\mathrm{Id}$ is $\prod_{i=1}^n(a_{ii}-x)=(-1)^nx^n+(-1)^{n-1}(a_{11}+\dots+a_{nn})x^{n-1}+(\text{lower terms})$. If $\sigma\ne\mathrm{Id}$, then $\sigma(i)\ne i$ for at least two $i$ (if only one $i$ moved, $\sigma(i)=j\ne i$ and $\sigma(j)=j$ would contradict injectivity). So its term has at most $n-2$ factors containing $x$, each of degree $1$, and has degree $\le n-2$. Hence the coefficients of $x^n$ and $x^{n-1}$ are as claimed. The constant term is $p_A(0)=\det A$.

Putting in a number for the stretch gives the measure of the modified courier.

(2) Substituting $x=\lambda$ in each term of the Leibniz formula gives the Leibniz formula for $A-\lambda I_n$, so $p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I_n)$. By Proof C the eigenvalues are the zeros, and a nonzero polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ zeros in a field. (3) $A-xI_n$ is triangular with diagonal $a_{ii}-x$; use lesson 18, Proof B, in the field $K(x)$ of rational functions.

Renaming the roads does not change which stretches occur.

(4) Work in the field $K(x)$, where lesson 18 applies. $P^{-1}AP-xI_n=P^{-1}AP-P^{-1}(xI_n)P=P^{-1}(A-xI_n)P$, so by the product rule $p_{P^{-1}AP}=\det(P)^{-1}\,p_A\,\det P=p_A$. Two matrices of $T$ in different bases are similar (lesson 13), so $p_T$ does not depend on the basis. ∎

Moves used: sandwich the map between identities

The proof in one piece

In the Leibniz formula for $A-xI_n$ only diagonal entries contain $x$. The identity contributes $\prod(a_{ii}-x)=(-1)^nx^n+(-1)^{n-1}\mathrm{tr}(A)x^{n-1}+\dots$; any $\sigma\ne\mathrm{Id}$ moves at least two points and contributes degree $\le n-2$. The constant term is $p_A(0)=\det A$. Substituting $\lambda$ commutes with the formula, so $p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I_n)$, and Proof C identifies the eigenvalues with the zeros, at most $n$ of them. Triangular: lesson 18, Proof B. Similar: in $K(x)$, $P^{-1}AP-xI_n=P^{-1}(A-xI_n)P$ and the product rule gives $p_{P^{-1}AP}=p_A$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Degree and top coefficients: only the identity permutation uses the whole diagonal.
  2. Constant term: put $x=0$.
  3. Similar: pull $P^{-1}$ and $P$ out of $P^{-1}AP-xI_n$.

In short: $p_A=(-1)^nx^n+(-1)^{n-1}\mathrm{tr}(A)x^{n-1}+\dots+\det A$, and it does not see a change of basis.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Write $p_A$ for a $2\times2$ matrix in terms of the trace and the determinant.
    Answer

    $p_A(x)=(a-x)(d-x)-bc=x^2-\mathrm{tr}(A)\,x+\det A$.

  2. Rung 2. If $A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb C)$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ (with repetitions, $p_A=\prod(\lambda_i-x)$), show $\mathrm{tr}A=\sum\lambda_i$ and $\det A=\prod\lambda_i$.
    Answer

    Expand $\prod_i(\lambda_i-x)$: the coefficient of $x^{n-1}$ is $(-1)^{n-1}\sum\lambda_i$ and the constant term is $\prod\lambda_i$. Compare with (1).

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof D from the frame alone.

The recipe: all eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a matrix (script, end of section 5.3.1, p. 165)

  1. Compute $p_A(x)=\det(A-xI_n)$ (Sarrus, expansion or elimination with care).
  2. Find its zeros $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_k$ in $K$. Check: their sum with multiplicity is $\mathrm{tr}A$ when $p_A$ splits completely.
  3. For each $\lambda_j$, solve $(A-\lambda_jI_n)v=0$ by elimination; the kernel is $\mathrm{Eig}_A(\lambda_j)$ (lesson 17, free variables).
  4. If the eigenspaces together give $n$ independent vectors, put them as the columns of $P$: $P^{-1}AP=D$. Check $AP=PD$.
Try it: the eigenvalues of a $2\times2$ matrix

Type integers. The explorer computes $p_A(x)=x^2-\mathrm{tr}(A)\,x+\det A$, its zeros, an eigenvector for each real eigenvalue, and whether $A$ is diagonalisable over $\mathbb R$.

Worked examples: a mixed set before the exercises

Worked example 1: the recipe for a $2\times2$ matrix

Diagonalise $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb R$ and compute $A^n$.

  1. Characteristic polynomial and eigenvalues.
    Answer

    $p_A(x)=(1-x)^2-4=x^2-2x-3=(x-3)(x+1)$. Eigenvalues $3$ and $-1$. Check: $3+(-1)=2=\mathrm{tr}A$, $3\cdot(-1)=-3=\det A$ ✓.

  2. Eigenvectors.
    Answer

    $A-3I=\begin{pmatrix}-2&2\\2&-2\end{pmatrix}$: kernel spanned by $(1,1)$. $A+I=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}$: kernel spanned by $(1,-1)$.

  3. $P$, $D$, and $A^n$.
    Answer

    $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $D=\mathrm{diag}(3,-1)$, $P^{-1}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$. Then $A^n=PD^nP^{-1}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}3^n+(-1)^n&3^n-(-1)^n\\3^n-(-1)^n&3^n+(-1)^n\end{pmatrix}$. Check $n=1$: $\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\4&2\end{pmatrix}=A$ ✓.

Worked example 2: a $3\times3$ matrix with a repeated eigenvalue

Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\1&2&1\\1&1&2\end{pmatrix}$ (lessons 18 and 20). Find all eigenvalues and eigenspaces. Is $A$ diagonalisable?

  1. A shortcut: $A=I_3+J$, where $J$ has all entries $1$. What does $J$ do?
    Answer

    $J(1,1,1)^T=3(1,1,1)^T$, and $Jv=0$ for every $v$ with $v_1+v_2+v_3=0$. So $A$ has eigenvalue $4$ on $(1,1,1)$ and eigenvalue $1$ on the plane $v_1+v_2+v_3=0$.

  2. Confirm with the characteristic polynomial.
    Answer

    $p_A(x)=-(x-1)^2(x-4)$: degree $3$, leading coefficient $-1=(-1)^3$; the coefficient of $x^2$ is $1+1+4=6=\mathrm{tr}A$; the constant term is $4=\det A$ ✓ (Proof D).

  3. Eigenspaces and diagonalisability.
    Answer

    $\mathrm{Eig}(4)=\mathrm{span}\{(1,1,1)\}$; $\mathrm{Eig}(1)=\mathrm{Ker}(J)=\mathrm{span}\{(1,-1,0),(1,0,-1)\}$, dimension $2$. Three independent eigenvectors (Proof B for different eigenvalues, a basis inside $\mathrm{Eig}(1)$), so $A$ is diagonalisable with $D=\mathrm{diag}(4,1,1)$, although it has only two distinct eigenvalues.

Worked example 3: quick exam items (HS24, and classics)

  1. HS24, MC2: $B\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ is invertible if and only if it has $n$ distinct eigenvalues in $K$.
    Answer

    False. $I_n$ ($n\ge2$) is invertible with the single eigenvalue $1$. And $\mathrm{diag}(0,1)$ has two distinct eigenvalues but is not invertible. The correct statement: invertible iff $0$ is not an eigenvalue (Proof C, Rung 2).

  2. True or false: similar matrices have the same eigenvalues and the same eigenvectors.
    Answer

    Same eigenvalues: true (Proof D (4)). Same eigenvectors: false: if $B=P^{-1}AP$ and $Av=\lambda v$, then $B(P^{-1}v)=\lambda P^{-1}v$, so the eigenvectors of $B$ are $P^{-1}v$, which differ in general.

  3. True or false: if $p_A=p_B$, then $A$ and $B$ are similar.
    Answer

    False. $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $I_2$ both have $p=(1-x)^2$, but only $I_2$ is similar to $I_2$.

Why it's cool

The Fibonacci recursion $f_{n+2}=f_{n+1}+f_n$ is $v_{n+1}=Av_n$ with $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $v_n=(f_n,f_{n+1})$. Its eigenvalues are the zeros of $x^2-x-1$: the golden ratio $\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2$ and $\psi=\frac{1-\sqrt5}2$. Diagonalising gives Binet's formula $f_n=\frac{\varphi^n-\psi^n}{\sqrt5}$, an exact formula with irrational numbers for a sequence of integers. Google's original PageRank was an eigenvector of a huge matrix of web links, and in quantum mechanics the possible energies of an atom are eigenvalues: the colours of a neon sign are differences of eigenvalues.

For wonder: a matrix is a zero of its own polynomial (optional, 5 min)

Take $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$ with $p_A(x)=x^2-2x-3$, and put the matrix itself into the polynomial: $A^2-2A-3I_2=\begin{pmatrix}5&4\\4&5\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\4&2\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}=0$. This is no accident: every square matrix satisfies $p_A(A)=0$ (the theorem of Cayley and Hamilton). For a diagonalisable matrix you can see why: $p_A(D)$ is diagonal with entries $p_A(\lambda_i)=0$. The general proof uses the adjugate of lesson 20 with entries in $K[x]$, and it is one of the first results of Linear Algebra II.

Exercise 1 (HS18, problem 6, 15 points)

Let $F_0=3$, $F_1=6$, $F_2=14$ and $F_{n+1}=6F_n-11F_{n-1}+6F_{n-2}$ for $n\ge2$. (a) With $v_n=(F_n,F_{n-1},F_{n-2})^T$, write the recursion as $v_{n+1}=Av_n$. (b) Find the characteristic polynomial, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A$ over $\mathbb C$. (c) Give an explicit formula for $F_n$.

Hint

(b) Expand $\det(A-xI_3)$ along the first row; try $x=1$. An eigenvector for $\lambda$ has the shape $(\lambda^2,\lambda,1)$. (c) $F_n=a+b\,2^n+c\,3^n$; use $F_0,F_1,F_2$.

Solution

Frame: companion matrix; recipe; the general solution is a combination of powers of the eigenvalues. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

(a) $A=\begin{pmatrix}6&-11&6\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$.

(b) Expanding along row 1: $p_A(x)=(6-x)x^2+11\cdot(-x)+6\cdot1=-x^3+6x^2-11x+6=-(x-1)(x-2)(x-3)$. Eigenvalues $1,2,3$; $A(\lambda^2,\lambda,1)^T=(6\lambda^2-11\lambda+6,\lambda^2,\lambda)^T=\lambda(\lambda^2,\lambda,1)^T$ because $\lambda^3=6\lambda^2-11\lambda+6$. Eigenvectors $(1,1,1)$, $(4,2,1)$, $(9,3,1)$ and their nonzero multiples.

(c) $v_2=(14,6,3)^T$ is a combination of the eigenvectors, so $v_n=A^{n-2}v_2$ is a combination of $1^n,2^n,3^n$ times them; hence $F_n=a+b\,2^n+c\,3^n$. From $F_0,F_1,F_2$: $a+b+c=3$, $a+2b+3c=6$, $a+4b+9c=14$, so $b+2c=3$, $2b+6c=8$, giving $c=1$, $b=1$, $a=1$. $F_n=1+2^n+3^n$. Check: $F_3=6\cdot14-11\cdot6+6\cdot3=36=1+8+27$ ✓.

Exercise 2 (FS20, problem 5 (a)–(b), 8 points)

Let $A,B\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb R)$. (a) Assume $A$ is invertible. Show that $AB$ and $BA$ are similar, and so have the same characteristic polynomial. (b) Are $AB$ and $BA$ similar for all $A$ and $B$?

Hint

(a) Conjugate $AB$ by $A$. (b) Try $2\times2$ matrices with one entry $1$.

Solution

Frame: one conjugation; one counterexample. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

(a) $A^{-1}(AB)A=BA$, so $AB$ and $BA$ are similar, and $p_{AB}=p_{BA}$ by Proof D (4).

(b) No. $A=E_{12}$, $B=E_{11}$: $AB=E_{12}E_{11}=0$, but $BA=E_{11}E_{12}=E_{12}\ne0$. The zero matrix is similar only to itself ($P^{-1}0P=0$). (Part (c) of the exam, $p_{AB}=p_{BA}$ always, uses the normal form of lesson 15 and is left for Linear Algebra II.)

Exercise 3 (FS19, problem 4 (a), 4 points: idempotent matrices)

A square matrix $A$ over $K$ is idempotent if $A^2=A$. Show that every idempotent matrix is diagonalisable with eigenvalues in $\{0,1\}$.

Hint

For an eigenvalue: $\lambda v=Av=A^2v=\lambda^2v$. For a basis of eigenvectors: write $v=(v-Av)+Av$.

Solution

Frame: eigenvalue equation; split every vector; Proof A. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

If $Av=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$: $\lambda v=Av=A^2v=\lambda^2v$, so $\lambda^2=\lambda$ and $\lambda\in\{0,1\}$.

Every $v\in K^n$ is $v=(v-Av)+Av$ with $A(v-Av)=Av-A^2v=0$ and $A(Av)=Av$. So $K^n=\mathrm{Eig}(0)+\mathrm{Eig}(1)$, and $\mathrm{Eig}(0)\cap\mathrm{Eig}(1)=\{0\}$ (Proof B, Rung 2). Take a basis of $\mathrm{Eig}(0)$ and a basis of $\mathrm{Eig}(1)$: together they span $K^n$ and are independent (a relation splits into a part in each eigenspace, and both parts must vanish since the intersection is $0$). This is a basis of eigenvectors, so $A$ is diagonalisable (Proof A). $\blacksquare$

Exercise 4 (diagonalisability depends on the field; script Example 5.3.28)

Let $R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, the rotation by $90°$. Show that $R$ has no eigenvalues in $\mathbb R$, and diagonalise it over $\mathbb C$.

Hint

$p_R(x)=x^2+1$.

Solution

Frame: recipe over two fields.

$p_R(x)=x^2+1$ has no real zero, so $R$ has no real eigenvalue (geometrically: no line is mapped to itself by a quarter turn) and is not diagonalisable over $\mathbb R$. Over $\mathbb C$: zeros $\pm i$. $R-iI=\begin{pmatrix}-i&-1\\1&-i\end{pmatrix}$ has kernel spanned by $(i,1)$; $R+iI$ has kernel spanned by $(-i,1)$. Check: $R(i,1)^T=(-1,i)^T=i(i,1)^T$ ✓. So $P=\begin{pmatrix}i&-i\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, $P^{-1}RP=\mathrm{diag}(i,-i)$.

Exercise 5 (a matrix that is not diagonalisable)

Show that $N=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ is not diagonalisable over any field $K$. Then decide whether $C=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&0\\1&3&0\\4&5&3\end{pmatrix}\in M_{3\times3}(\mathbb R)$ is diagonalisable.

Hint

Triangular matrices: $p$ is the product of the diagonal entries minus $x$ (Proof D (3)). Count independent eigenvectors.

Solution

Frame: eigenvalues from the diagonal; eigenspaces too small for a basis.

$p_N=(1-x)^2$, so $1$ is the only eigenvalue. $N-I=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ has kernel spanned by $e_1$: every eigenvector is a multiple of $e_1$, so there is no basis of eigenvectors (Proof A). Alternatively: if $P^{-1}NP=D$, then $D=I_2$ (its diagonal holds the only eigenvalue), so $N=PIP^{-1}=I_2$, a contradiction.

$p_C=(2-x)(3-x)^2$. $C-3I=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\1&0&0\\4&5&0\end{pmatrix}$: $v_1=0$, then $5v_2=0$, $v_3$ free, so $\mathrm{Eig}(3)=\mathrm{span}\{e_3\}$ has dimension $1$. With $\dim\mathrm{Eig}(2)=1$ there are at most $2$ independent eigenvectors in $\mathbb R^3$: not diagonalisable.

Exercise 6 (eigenvalues of related matrices)

Let $Av=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$. Show: (a) $A^kv=\lambda^kv$ for $k\ge1$; (b) if $A$ is invertible, $\lambda\ne0$ and $A^{-1}v=\lambda^{-1}v$; (c) $A^T$ has the same eigenvalues as $A$ (but in general other eigenvectors).

Hint

(a) Induction. (b) Apply $A^{-1}$ to $Av=\lambda v$. (c) $p_{A^T}=p_A$ by lesson 20, Proof A.

Solution

Frame: induction; apply the inverse; transpose inside the determinant.

(a) $A^{k+1}v=A(\lambda^kv)=\lambda^kAv=\lambda^{k+1}v$. (b) $\lambda\ne0$ because $0$ is not an eigenvalue of an invertible matrix (Proof C, Rung 2); applying $A^{-1}$ gives $v=\lambda A^{-1}v$, so $A^{-1}v=\lambda^{-1}v$. (c) $p_{A^T}(x)=\det(A^T-xI_n)=\det((A-xI_n)^T)=\det(A-xI_n)=p_A(x)$. Eigenvectors differ: $N=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvectors on $e_1$, $N^T$ on $e_2$.
