Recap of lesson 10 (5 min)

  • Metric: $d(x,y)\ge0$ with equality only for $x=y$, symmetry, triangle inequality. On $\mathbb{R}$: $d(x,y)=|x-y|$.
  • Convergence: $a_n\to A$ means $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall n\ge N:\ |a_n-A|<\varepsilon$. The limit is unique.
  • Convergent ⇒ bounded: $\varepsilon=1$ for the tail, a finite maximum for the head.
  • Limit rules: sums, multiples, products and quotients (with nonzero denominator limit) of convergent sequences converge to the expected limits. Non-strict inequalities survive limits; strict ones can become equalities.
  • Subsequence $(a_{n_k})_k$ with $n_1<n_2<\dots$; it has the same limit as a convergent sequence. An accumulation point of a sequence is a limit of some subsequence.
  • Sequential continuity: $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ ⇔ $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$ for every sequence $x_n\to x_0$.

  1. What is the key step in proving that a limit is unique?

    Answer

    Take $\varepsilon=\frac12d(A_1,A_2)$, take $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$, and use the triangle inequality through $a_N$: $$\begin{aligned}d(A_1,A_2)&\le d(A_1,a_N)+d(a_N,A_2)\\&<2\varepsilon\\&=d(A_1,A_2)\end{aligned},$$ a contradiction.

  2. Is $a_n=(-1)^n$ bounded? Convergent? What are its accumulation points?

    Answer

    Bounded by $1$, but divergent. Its accumulation points are $1$ (even terms) and $-1$ (odd terms).

  3. How do you show that $f$ is not continuous at $x_0$ with sequences?

    Answer

    Find one sequence $x_n\to x_0$ with $f(x_n)\not\to f(x_0)$.

1. The story: the climber and the tide

A climber goes up the endless stair of the Tower of Wardens. Every step is at least as high as the one before; sometimes she pauses, but she never goes down. Above her, however high she climbs, there is a stone ceiling she can never pass. The tower's riddle: must she come to rest somewhere? She can't climb forever, because the ceiling stops her, and she can't jump back down. The answer is yes, and the place she approaches is the lowest ceiling she never passes: the supremum of all her heights. That's the monotone convergence theorem.

In the harbour below, the tide rises and falls forever and never settles. The harbourmaster has an oracle. Each morning the oracle tells her the highest the water will ever reach from today on: the day's high-water mark. As the days pass, the high-water marks can only fall (the future gets shorter, so its highest point can't get higher). They form a falling sequence that never goes below the harbour floor, so by the climber's riddle, read upside down, they come to rest. Where they come to rest is the limit superior of the tide: the highest level the water keeps returning to forever. The low-water marks do the same from below and settle at the limit inferior. When the two marks meet, the tide has stopped moving: the sequence converges.

And at the gate, two guards escort a prisoner, one on each side. Where the two guards arrive, the prisoner arrives too. That's the sandwich lemma, and it's where we start.

In maths: the climber is an increasing bounded sequence, the ceiling is an upper bound, the high-water mark on day $n$ is $S_n=\sup\{a_k:k\ge n\}$, the low-water mark is $I_n=\inf\{a_k:k\ge n\}$, and the guards are two sequences that converge to the same limit.

2. Inequalities and the sandwich lemma

Proposition 6.1 (p. 253). Let $a_n\to a$ and $b_n\to b$ in $\mathbb{R}$. (i) If $a_n\le b_n$ for all $n$, then $a\le b$. (ii) If $a<b$, then there is $N$ with $a_n<b_n$ for all $n\ge N$.

You proved (i) in Exercise 6 of lesson 10 with $\varepsilon=\frac{a-b}2$; the script proves (ii) with $\varepsilon=\frac{b-a}2$ and gets (i) as the contrapositive. Remember the warning: $a_n<b_n$ for all $n$ only gives $a\le b$ (take $a_n=0<\frac1n=b_n$).

Sandwich lemma (Lemma 6.2, p. 254). Let $a_n\le b_n\le c_n$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$, and suppose $a_n\to A$ and $c_n\to A$ (the same limit). Then $(b_n)_n$ converges, and $b_n\to A$.

Note what's special: we don't assume $b_n$ converges; the lemma proves it. That's why it's more than Proposition 6.1 (i).

Proof A: the sandwich lemma (Wichtige Übung 6.3)

The idea. From some point on, both guards are within $\varepsilon$ of $A$: $A-\varepsilon<a_n$ and $c_n<A+\varepsilon$. The prisoner is between them, so it's within $\varepsilon$ of $A$ too.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” (We must show $b_n\to A$, which starts with “for every ε”.)
  • Last line: “…so $|b_n-A|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.” We must name $N$.
  • The given: an $N_1$ for $(a_n)$ and an $N_2$ for $(c_n)$, for the same $\varepsilon$.
  • The gap to fill: combine them, and turn $|b_n-A|<\varepsilon$ into two one-sided inequalities.
The storyWhat you write

Fix a tolerance.

Let $\varepsilon>0$.

Wait until both guards are near the gate.

Since $a_n\to A$ and $c_n\to A$, there are $N_1,N_2$ with $|a_n-A|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N_1$ and $|c_n-A|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N_2$. Let $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$.

We only need the outer side of each guard: the left guard is not too far left, the right guard not too far right.

For $n\ge N$: $$A-\varepsilon<a_n\le b_n\le c_n<A+\varepsilon.$$

So the prisoner is near the gate.

Hence $|b_n-A|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$, so $b_n\to A$. ∎

Moves used: take the larger N sandwich

The proof in one piece

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N_1,N_2$ with $|a_n-A|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N_1$ and $|c_n-A|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N_2$, and let $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$. For $n\ge N$ we get $A-\varepsilon<a_n\le b_n\le c_n<A+\varepsilon$, so $|b_n-A|<\varepsilon$. Hence $b_n\to A$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Let $\varepsilon>0$; get $N_1$ for $a_n$, $N_2$ for $c_n$.
  2. $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$.
  3. The chain $A-\varepsilon<a_n\le b_n\le c_n<A+\varepsilon$.
  4. Conclude $|b_n-A|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$.

In short: “max of the Ns, one chain, done”.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Which half of $|a_n-A|<\varepsilon$ do we actually use, and which half of $|c_n-A|<\varepsilon$?

    Answer

    From $|a_n-A|<\varepsilon$ only $a_n>A-\varepsilon$; from $|c_n-A|<\varepsilon$ only $c_n<A+\varepsilon$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). The sandwich also works if $a_n\le b_n\le c_n$ only holds for $n\ge n_0$. What changes in the proof?

    Answer

    Take $N=\max\{N_1,N_2,n_0\}$. Everything else stays the same.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Typical use: $\left|\frac{(-1)^n}{n}\right|\le\frac1n$, so $-\frac1n\le\frac{(-1)^n}{n}\le\frac1n$ and both outer sequences tend to $0$, hence $\frac{(-1)^n}n\to0$. More generally: if $|b_n|\le c_n$ and $c_n\to0$, then $b_n\to0$ (sandwich between $-c_n$ and $c_n$).

3. Monotone sequences

Definitions (as for functions in lesson 7). A real sequence $(a_n)_n$ is increasing if $a_{n+1}\ge a_n$ for all $n$, and strictly increasing if $a_{n+1}>a_n$ for all $n$. It is decreasing (strictly decreasing) if $a_{n+1}\le a_n$ ($a_{n+1}<a_n$) for all $n$. It is monotone if it is increasing or decreasing.

Monotone convergence theorem (Satz 6.5, p. 255). A monotone real sequence converges if and only if it is bounded. If $(a_n)_n$ is increasing, then $\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=\sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$; if it is decreasing, then $\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=\inf\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$.

This is the first time the completeness axiom (every bounded nonempty set has a supremum) produces a limit. It's also the first convergence test that doesn't need you to know the limit in advance: you only check “monotone” and “bounded”.

Proof B: bounded monotone sequences converge (Satz 6.5)

The idea. The candidate limit is the supremum $S$ of all terms. Just below $S$ there must be a term $a_N$ (otherwise there'd be a lower ceiling). After that, the sequence can't fall below $a_N$ (it's increasing) and can't rise above $S$ (it's an upper bound). So it's trapped in $(S-\varepsilon,S]$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Two directions. “Convergent ⇒ bounded” is lesson 10's Proof C. The work is “bounded and monotone ⇒ convergent”.
  • We need a candidate limit, and the only thing completeness gives us is $S=\sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$.
  • First line: “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” Last line: “so $|a_n-S|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.”
  • The gap to fill: find $N$, using the characterisation of the supremum (“$S-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound”).
The storyWhat you write

A convergent climber stays below some ceiling anyway.

If $(a_n)_n$ converges, it is bounded (lesson 10: convergent sequences are bounded). Conversely, let $(a_n)_n$ be monotone and bounded.

A falling climber is a rising one seen upside down.

Without loss of generality $(a_n)_n$ is increasing; otherwise replace $a_n$ by $-a_n$ (then $\sup\{-a_n\}=-\inf\{a_n\}$).

The lowest ceiling she never passes exists.

The set $\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$ is nonempty and bounded above, so $S=\sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$ exists by completeness.

Any lower ceiling would be passed at some step.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $S-\varepsilon<S$, it is not an upper bound, so there is $N$ with $a_N>S-\varepsilon$.

After that step she never goes down, and never through the ceiling.

For $n\ge N$, monotonicity and the upper bound give $$S-\varepsilon<a_N\le a_n\le S<S+\varepsilon,$$ so $|a_n-S|<\varepsilon$. Hence $a_n\to S$. ∎

Moves used: reduce to one case approach a supremum trap between two values

The proof in one piece

A convergent sequence is bounded (lesson 10). Conversely let $(a_n)_n$ be monotone and bounded; without loss of generality it is increasing (otherwise consider $(-a_n)_n$). By completeness, $S=\sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$ exists. Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $S-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound of $\{a_n\}$, there is $N$ with $a_N>S-\varepsilon$. For $n\ge N$ we get $S-\varepsilon<a_N\le a_n\le S$, so $|a_n-S|<\varepsilon$. Hence $a_n\to S$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. “⇒” is convergent ⇒ bounded. For “⇐”: oBdA increasing.
  2. $S=\sup\{a_n\}$ exists (completeness, bounded above).
  3. Let $\varepsilon>0$: $S-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound, so $a_N>S-\varepsilon$ for some $N$.
  4. Chain for $n\ge N$: $S-\varepsilon<a_N\le a_n\le S$.

In short: “sup exists, one term above S − ε, monotone carries it to all later terms”.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). In the chain $S-\varepsilon<a_N\le a_n\le S$, which fact justifies each of the three inequalities?

    Answer

    $S-\varepsilon<a_N$: the choice of $N$ (approach a supremum). $a_N\le a_n$: the sequence is increasing and $n\ge N$. $a_n\le S$: $S$ is an upper bound of all terms.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the proof for a decreasing bounded sequence directly, with $I=\inf\{a_n\}$.

    Answer

    Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $I+\varepsilon$ is not a lower bound, there is $N$ with $a_N<I+\varepsilon$. For $n\ge N$: $I\le a_n\le a_N<I+\varepsilon$, so $|a_n-I|<\varepsilon$.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Two things to remember for multiple choice. Monotone alone is not enough: $a_n=n$ is increasing and diverges. Bounded alone is not enough: $(-1)^n$ is bounded and diverges. An increasing sequence that is not bounded above diverges to $\infty$ (lesson 12 makes this precise).

4. Limit superior and limit inferior

A bounded sequence like $(-1)^n$ need not converge. But we can still ask: what's the highest level it keeps coming back to, and the lowest? Here's the harbourmaster's trick in formulas. Let $(a_n)_n$ be bounded, $|a_k|\le M$ for all $k$. For every $n$ define the tail supremum and the tail infimum:

$$S_n=\sup\{a_k:k\ge n\},\qquad I_n=\inf\{a_k:k\ge n\}.$$
  • $(S_n)_n$ is decreasing: $\{a_k:k\ge n+1\}\subseteq\{a_k:k\ge n\}$, and the supremum of a smaller set is at most the supremum of the larger set, so $S_{n+1}\le S_n$.
  • $(S_n)_n$ is bounded: $-M\le a_n\le S_n\le M$.
  • So by the monotone convergence theorem, $S_n$ converges, to $\inf_n S_n$. In the same way $(I_n)_n$ is increasing and bounded and converges to $\sup_n I_n$.

Definitions 6.8 and 6.9 (p. 257). For a bounded real sequence $(a_n)_n$, the limit superior and limit inferior are

$$\limsup_{n\to\infty}a_n=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{k\ge n}a_k=\inf_{n\in\mathbb N}\sup_{k\ge n}a_k,$$ $$\liminf_{n\to\infty}a_n=\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{k\ge n}a_k=\sup_{n\in\mathbb N}\inf_{k\ge n}a_k.$$

Since $I_n\le S_n$ for all $n$, always $\liminf a_n\le\limsup a_n$ (Proposition 6.1).

Example. For $a_n=(-1)^n$, every tail contains both $1$ and $-1$, so $S_n=1$ and $I_n=-1$ for all $n$: $\limsup(-1)^n=1$ and $\liminf(-1)^n=-1$.

Example 6.10 (p. 257). $a_n=(-1)^n+\frac1n$: the terms are $0,\ \frac32,\ -\frac23,\ \frac54,\ -\frac45,\ \frac76,\dots$ The even terms $1+\frac1n$ decrease towards $1$, the odd terms $-1+\frac1n$ decrease towards $-1$. The largest term in a tail is its first even term, so $S_n=1+\frac1n$ for $n$ even and $S_n=1+\frac1{n+1}$ for $n$ odd, and $S_n\to1$. Every tail contains odd terms arbitrarily close to $-1$ but none below $-1$, so $I_n=-1$ for all $n$. Hence $\limsup a_n=1$ and $\liminf a_n=-1$.

Unbounded sequences (p. 262). If $(a_n)_n$ is not bounded above, then every $S_n=\infty$ and we set $\limsup a_n=\infty$. If it is bounded above but not below, $\limsup a_n=\lim S_n$, which may be $-\infty$ (for example $\limsup(-n)=-\infty$). The same conventions hold for $\liminf$ with signs swapped.

The two properties of the limit superior

Satz 6.11 (p. 257). Let $(a_n)_n$ be bounded and $S=\limsup a_n$. For every $\varepsilon>0$:

  • (P1) only finitely many $n$ satisfy $a_n>S+\varepsilon$ (equivalently: there is $N$ with $a_n\le S+\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$);
  • (P2) infinitely many $n$ satisfy $a_n>S-\varepsilon$.

The mirror statement for $I=\liminf a_n$: only finitely many $a_n<I-\varepsilon$, and infinitely many $a_n<I+\varepsilon$.

In words: the sequence eventually stays below any level above $S$, and keeps coming back above any level below $S$. Conversely, a number with both properties is the $\limsup$ (Übung 6.12, which we use without proof).

Proof C: the two properties (Satz 6.11)

The idea. (P1): the high-water marks $S_n$ settle at $S$, so some mark $S_N$ is below $S+\varepsilon$, and every later term is below its mark. (P2): every high-water mark $S_N$ is at least $S$, and the water really comes within $\varepsilon$ of the mark, after any day $N$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Given: $S_n=\sup_{k\ge n}a_k$ decreases to $S=\inf_n S_n$. Two facts about $S$: “$S_n\to S$” and “$S\le S_n$ for every $n$”.
  • (P1) needs one $N$ after which $a_k\le S+\varepsilon$.
  • (P2), “infinitely many”, is best shown as: for every $N$ there is $k\ge N$ with $a_k>S-\varepsilon$. (If there were only finitely many such $k$, take $N$ beyond the largest one.)
The storyWhat you write

(P1) The marks settle at $S$, so one of them is already below $S+\varepsilon$.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $S_n\to S$, there is $N$ with $S_N<S+\varepsilon$.

The water never rises above that day's mark afterwards.

For $k\ge N$: $a_k\le S_N<S+\varepsilon$. So $a_k>S+\varepsilon$ can only happen for $k<N$: finitely many indices.

(P2) Pick any day $N$. Its mark is at least $S$.

Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $N\in\mathbb N$. Since $S=\inf_n S_n$, we have $S_N\ge S$.

The mark is the true highest point, so the water comes within $\varepsilon$ of it after day $N$.

$S_N-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound of $\{a_k:k\ge N\}$, so there is $k\ge N$ with $$a_k>S_N-\varepsilon\ge S-\varepsilon.$$

That happens after every day, so it happens infinitely often.

Since $N$ was arbitrary, there are infinitely many $k$ with $a_k>S-\varepsilon$. ∎

Moves used: use convergence with a chosen ε approach a supremum

The proof in one piece

Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $S_n=\sup_{k\ge n}a_k$. (P1): since $S_n\to S$, there is $N$ with $S_N<S+\varepsilon$; then $a_k\le S_N<S+\varepsilon$ for all $k\ge N$, so $a_k>S+\varepsilon$ holds for at most the $N-1$ indices $k<N$. (P2): let $N\in\mathbb N$. Then $S_N\ge\inf_n S_n=S$, and since $S_N-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound of $\{a_k:k\ge N\}$, there is $k\ge N$ with $a_k>S_N-\varepsilon\ge S-\varepsilon$. As $N$ was arbitrary, infinitely many $k$ satisfy $a_k>S-\varepsilon$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

    Answer

    Above $\frac54$: only $a_2=\frac32$ (since $a_4=\frac54$ is not strictly above). Finitely many. Above $\frac34$: all even terms $1+\frac1n$. Infinitely many.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). State (P1) and (P2) in quantifiers (Übung 6.12).

    Answer

    (P1) $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall n\ge N:\ a_n\le S+\varepsilon$. (P2) $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \forall N\ \exists n\ge N:\ a_n>S-\varepsilon$.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the proof of (P2) from the frame alone.

Proof D: convergent ⇔ liminf = limsup (Korollar 6.14, p. 258)

The idea. “⇐”: if the high- and low-water marks settle at the same level $A$, the water is squeezed: eventually below $A+\varepsilon$ by (P1), and above $A-\varepsilon$ by the mirror of (P1). “⇒”: if $a_n\to A$, every tail lies in $(A-\varepsilon,A+\varepsilon)$, so its supremum and infimum lie in $[A-\varepsilon,A+\varepsilon]$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Two directions, for a bounded sequence $(a_n)_n$.
  • “⇐” ends with “so $|a_n-A|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$”. The tools: (P1) for $\limsup$ and its mirror for $\liminf$.
  • “⇒” ends with “$\liminf a_n=\limsup a_n=A$”: an equality of numbers, so show $|\cdot|\le\varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon$.
The storyWhat you write

“⇐” Both marks settle at $A$. Use half the tolerance on each side.

Let $A=\liminf a_n=\limsup a_n$ and $\varepsilon>0$. By (P1) there is $N_1$ with $a_n\le A+\frac\varepsilon2$ for $n\ge N_1$; by its mirror for $\liminf$ there is $N_2$ with $a_n\ge A-\frac\varepsilon2$ for $n\ge N_2$.

After both days, the water is pinned near $A$.

For $n\ge N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$: $|a_n-A|\le\frac\varepsilon2<\varepsilon$. So $a_n\to A$.

“⇒” The water settles at $A$, so from some day on it stays in a narrow band.

Let $a_n\to A$ and $\varepsilon>0$. There is $N$ with $A-\varepsilon<a_k<A+\varepsilon$ for all $k\ge N$.

So every later high-water mark and low-water mark is inside the band (or on its edge).

For $n\ge N$, $A+\varepsilon$ is an upper bound and $A-\varepsilon$ a lower bound of $\{a_k:k\ge n\}$, so $$A-\varepsilon\le I_n\le S_n\le A+\varepsilon.$$

The marks settle inside the band too, and the band can be made as thin as we like.

Letting $n\to\infty$ (limits preserve $\le$): $A-\varepsilon\le\liminf a_n\le\limsup a_n\le A+\varepsilon$. Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $\liminf a_n=\limsup a_n=A$. ∎

Moves used: ε/2 twice take the larger N sup ≤ an upper bound smaller than every ε

The proof in one piece

“⇐”: Let $A=\liminf a_n=\limsup a_n$ and $\varepsilon>0$. By (P1) of Proof C and its mirror, there are $N_1,N_2$ with $a_n\le A+\frac\varepsilon2$ for $n\ge N_1$ and $a_n\ge A-\frac\varepsilon2$ for $n\ge N_2$. For $n\ge\max\{N_1,N_2\}$ we get $|a_n-A|\le\frac\varepsilon2<\varepsilon$, so $a_n\to A$.

“⇒”: Let $a_n\to A$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $A-\varepsilon<a_k<A+\varepsilon$ for $k\ge N$. For $n\ge N$, $A\pm\varepsilon$ are bounds for $\{a_k:k\ge n\}$, so $A-\varepsilon\le I_n\le S_n\le A+\varepsilon$. Letting $n\to\infty$ gives $A-\varepsilon\le\liminf a_n\le\limsup a_n\le A+\varepsilon$. Since $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $\liminf a_n=\limsup a_n=A$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. “⇐”: (P1) for $\limsup$ gives $a_n\le A+\frac\varepsilon2$ eventually; the mirror for $\liminf$ gives $a_n\ge A-\frac\varepsilon2$ eventually.
  2. Max of the two Ns, then $|a_n-A|<\varepsilon$.
  3. “⇒”: tail in $(A-\varepsilon,A+\varepsilon)$ ⇒ $A-\varepsilon\le I_n\le S_n\le A+\varepsilon$.
  4. Limit $n\to\infty$, then ε arbitrary ⇒ equality.

In short: “⇐ two properties and a max; ⇒ the band traps the tail sup and inf”.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why is $S_n\le A+\varepsilon$ (not $<$) in the “⇒” part, although every $a_k<A+\varepsilon$?

    Answer

    A supremum of numbers that are all $<c$ can equal $c$; for example $\sup\{1-\frac1k\}=1$. We only know $A+\varepsilon$ is an upper bound, which gives $S_n\le A+\varepsilon$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the “⇐” direction.

    Answer

    See the first paragraph of the one-piece proof.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write both directions from the frame alone.

A practical rule for computing limsup and liminf

If a sequence splits into finitely many subsequences that together contain every term (for example the even terms and the odd terms) and each of them converges, then $\limsup a_n$ is the largest of their limits and $\liminf a_n$ the smallest.

Why: say the even terms tend to $L_1$ and the odd terms to $L_2$, with $L_1\ge L_2$. For $\varepsilon>0$, eventually all even terms are $<L_1+\varepsilon$ and all odd terms are $<L_2+\varepsilon\le L_1+\varepsilon$ (take the larger $N$), which is (P1) for $L_1$. And infinitely many terms (the even ones, from some point on) are $>L_1-\varepsilon$, which is (P2) for $L_1$. By Übung 6.12, $L_1=\limsup a_n$. The same argument from below gives $\liminf a_n=L_2$. This is the rule you use in Exercise 6.

5. Worked examples

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

Worked example 1: $\sqrt[n]{a}\to1$ for every $a>0$ (Beispiel 6.4, p. 254)

  1. Start with $a>1$. Then $\sqrt[n]a>1$. What should we show about $b_n=\sqrt[n]a-1\ge0$?

    Answer

    That $b_n\to0$. Then $\sqrt[n]a=1+b_n\to1$ by the sum rule.

  2. We have $a=(1+b_n)^n$. Which inequality from lesson 4 gives a lower bound for $(1+b_n)^n$ that is linear in $b_n$?

    Answer

    Bernoulli's inequality: $(1+x)^n\ge1+nx$ for $x\ge-1$. So $a=(1+b_n)^n\ge1+nb_n\ge nb_n$.

  3. Finish with the sandwich lemma.

    Answer

    From $a\ge nb_n$: $0\le b_n\le\frac an$. Both $0\to0$ and $\frac an\to0$, so $b_n\to0$ by the sandwich lemma, and $\sqrt[n]a=1+b_n\to1$.

  4. Now $0<a<1$. Reduce to the first case.

    Answer

    $\frac1a>1$, so $\sqrt[n]{1/a}\to1$ by the first case, and $\sqrt[n]a=\frac1{\sqrt[n]{1/a}}\to\frac11=1$ by the quotient rule. The case $a=1$ is trivial.

The same idea with the binomial theorem instead of Bernoulli shows $\sqrt[n]n\to1$: with $b_n=\sqrt[n]n-1$, $n=(1+b_n)^n\ge\binom n2b_n^2=\frac{n(n-1)}2b_n^2$, so $0\le b_n\le\sqrt{\frac2{n-1}}$ for $n\ge2$.

Worked example 2: a recursive sequence (Übung 6.6, p. 256)

Let $a_1=1$ and $a_{n+1}=\frac23\left(a_n+\frac1{a_n}\right)$ for $n\ge1$. Show that $(a_n)_n$ converges and find the limit.

  1. Compute $a_2$ and $a_3$. What do you guess?

    Answer

    $a_2=\frac23\cdot2=\frac43\approx1.333$ and $a_3=\frac23\left(\frac43+\frac34\right)=\frac{25}{18}\approx1.389$. Guess: increasing, bounded above.

  2. Find the only possible limit first. If $a_n\to A$ with $A>0$, what equation must $A$ satisfy?

    Answer

    Since $a_{n+1}\to A$ too (shifted index), the limit rules give $A=\frac23\left(A+\frac1A\right)$, so $3A^2=2A^2+2$, $A^2=2$, $A=\sqrt2$. This is only a candidate: we still have to prove convergence.

  3. Let $f(x)=\frac23\left(x+\frac1x\right)$. Show: for $1\le x\le\sqrt2$ we have $x\le f(x)\le\sqrt2$.

    Answer

    $$\begin{aligned}f(x)\ge x&\iff\tfrac23x+\tfrac2{3x}\ge x\\&\iff\tfrac2{3x}\ge\tfrac x3\iff x^2\le2,\end{aligned}$$ which is true for $0<x\le\sqrt2$.

    $$\begin{aligned}f(x)\le\sqrt2&\iff x^2+1\le\tfrac{3}{\sqrt2}x\\&\iff x^2-\tfrac3{\sqrt2}x+1\le0\\&\iff\left(x-\sqrt2\right)\left(x-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\right)\le0,\end{aligned}$$ which is true for $\frac1{\sqrt2}\le x\le\sqrt2$, so in particular for $1\le x\le\sqrt2$. (Multiplying out: $\sqrt2\cdot\frac1{\sqrt2}=1$ and $\sqrt2+\frac1{\sqrt2}=\frac3{\sqrt2}$.)

  4. Prove by induction that $1\le a_n\le\sqrt2$ for all $n$, and conclude that $(a_n)_n$ is increasing.

    Answer

    Base: $a_1=1\in[1,\sqrt2]$. Step: if $1\le a_n\le\sqrt2$, then by question 3, $1\le a_n\le f(a_n)=a_{n+1}\le\sqrt2$. So $1\le a_n\le\sqrt2$ for all $n$, and then question 3 gives $a_n\le f(a_n)=a_{n+1}$ for all $n$.

  5. Conclude.

    Answer

    $(a_n)_n$ is increasing and bounded above by $\sqrt2$, so it converges by the monotone convergence theorem, to some $A\in[1,\sqrt2]$. Since $A\ge1>0$, question 2 applies and $A=\sqrt2$.

The recipe for recursive sequences: (1) guess from the first terms, (2) find the candidate limit from the fixed-point equation $A=f(A)$, (3) prove monotone and bounded by induction, (4) monotone convergence, (5) pass to the limit in the recursion. Step (2) alone is not a proof: $a_{n+1}=2a_n$, $a_1=1$ gives the “candidate” $A=0$, but $a_n=2^{n-1}\to\infty$.

Worked example 3: limsup and liminf by splitting

Compute $\limsup$ and $\liminf$ of $a_n=(-1)^n\frac n{n+1}$.

Solution. The even terms are $a_{2m}=\frac{2m}{2m+1}\to1$, and the odd terms are $a_{2m+1}=-\frac{2m+1}{2m+2}\to-1$. Every term is even or odd, so by the practical rule, $\limsup a_n=1$ and $\liminf a_n=-1$. Since they differ, $(a_n)_n$ diverges (Korollar 6.14).

Why it's cool. Monotone convergence proves that a limit exists without knowing it, for example for a recursive sequence (Worked example 2), and then the limit can be found from the recursion. limsup and liminf give every bounded sequence two honest “limits”, even when it oscillates forever.

Exercise 1 (Kobel-Keller HS24, single choice 4)

Open the original: HS24 exam

Let $(x_n)_n$ be a monotone real sequence. Which statement is correct? (a) The sequence may have no accumulation point. (b) It has at least one accumulation point. (c) It has exactly one accumulation point. (d) It has at least two accumulation points.

Solution

Answer (a). The sequence $x_n=n$ is increasing and has no accumulation point: every subsequence is unbounded, so none converges. This also rules out (b), (c) and (d).

In fact a monotone sequence has at most one accumulation point: if it is bounded, it converges (monotone convergence theorem), so its only accumulation point is the limit; if it is unbounded, every subsequence is unbounded (oBdA increasing: from some index on all terms exceed any given bound), so it has none.

Exercise 2 (Kobel-Keller HS24, single choice 1)

Open the original: HS24 exam

Let $(x_n)_n$ be a real sequence and $M=\{x_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$. Which statement is correct? (a) $\min M$ is always an accumulation point of the sequence. (b) If the sequence is monotone, it converges and its limit is $\inf M$ or $\sup M$. (c) It may happen that $\sup M$ or $\inf M$ does not exist. (d) The sequence converges if $\inf M=\sup M$.

Solution

Answer (d). If $\inf M=\sup M$, then every $x\in M$ satisfies $\inf M\le x\le\sup M$, so $M$ has exactly one element. The sequence is constant and converges.

  • (a) False: $x_1=0$ and $x_n=1$ for $n\ge2$. Then $\min M=0$, but the only accumulation point is $1$.
  • (b) False: $x_n=n$ is monotone and diverges.
  • (c) False in this course's convention: every nonempty subset of $\mathbb R$ has a supremum and an infimum in $\mathbb R\cup\{\pm\infty\}$ (script section 2.5.3, p. 108). For $x_n=n$, $\sup M=\infty$ exists in this extended sense. That's why the intended single answer is (d).

Exercise 3 (Figalli FS26, MC2)

Open the original: FS26 exam

Let $(x_n)_n$ be a real sequence. Which statement is false? (A) If $(x_n)_n$ is bounded, then $\limsup x_n$ is finite. (B) If $\limsup x_n$ is finite, then $(x_n)_n$ is bounded. (C) If $x_n\to L$, then $\liminf x_n=L$. (D) If $\limsup x_n=\liminf x_n$ and both are finite, then $(x_n)_n$ converges.

Solution

Answer (B). Counterexample: $x_n=0$ for even $n$ and $x_n=-n$ for odd $n$. Every tail contains $0$ and no positive terms, so $S_n=0$ and $\limsup x_n=0$ is finite, but the sequence is not bounded below.

  • (A) True: if $|x_n|\le M$, then $-M\le S_n\le M$, so the limit of $S_n$ lies in $[-M,M]$.
  • (C) True: a convergent sequence has $\limsup=\liminf=$ its limit (Proof D, “⇒”).
  • (D) True: $\limsup x_n<\infty$ means the sequence is bounded above (otherwise $\limsup=\infty$), and $\liminf x_n>-\infty$ means it is bounded below. So it is bounded, and Proof D (“⇐”: a bounded sequence with $\limsup=\liminf$ converges) applies.

Exercise 4 (sandwich)

Compute the limits, with justification: (a) $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(-1)^n\,n}{n^2+1}$, (b) $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{2^n+3^n}$.

Solution

Frame: for each part, find two simple sequences with the same limit, one below and one above, valid for all $n$; then the sandwich lemma.

(a) $0$. For all $n$: $\left|\frac{(-1)^nn}{n^2+1}\right|=\frac n{n^2+1}\le\frac n{n^2}=\frac1n$. So $-\frac1n\le\frac{(-1)^nn}{n^2+1}\le\frac1n$, both bounds tend to $0$, and the sandwich lemma gives the limit $0$.

(b) $3$. For all $n$: $3^n\le2^n+3^n\le2\cdot3^n$. The $n$-th root is increasing on $[0,\infty)$ (lesson 8), so $$3\le\sqrt[n]{2^n+3^n}\le3\sqrt[n]2.$$ By Worked example 1 and the multiple rule, $3\sqrt[n]2\to3$. By the sandwich lemma, $\sqrt[n]{2^n+3^n}\to3$.

Moves used: sandwich

Exercise 5 (Übung 6.7, p. 256)

Let $(a_n)_n$ be increasing, $(b_n)_n$ decreasing, and $a_n\le b_n$ for all $n$. Show that both sequences converge and that $\lim a_n\le\lim b_n$.

Solution

Frame: “converges” with no limit given: show monotone and bounded, then the monotone convergence theorem; the inequality comes from limits preserving $\le$. Moves: trap between two values.

For all $n$: $a_1\le a_n\le b_n\le b_1$, using that $(a_n)_n$ is increasing and $(b_n)_n$ is decreasing. So $(a_n)_n$ is increasing and bounded above by $b_1$, and $(b_n)_n$ is decreasing and bounded below by $a_1$. Both are bounded (by $\max\{|a_1|,|b_1|\}$). By the monotone convergence theorem, $a_n\to A$ and $b_n\to B$ for some $A,B\in\mathbb R$. Since $a_n\le b_n$ for all $n$, and limits preserve $\le$ (section 2), $A\le B$. ∎

Exercise 6 (in the style of Einsiedler FS22, 2 points)

Open the original: FS22 exam

Let $a_n=\frac{2n^2+1}{n^2+3}$ and $b_n=\frac{n+5}{n+1}$. Compute $\lim a_n$, $\lim b_n$, and $\limsup$ and $\liminf$ of $c_n=a_n+(-1)^nb_n$. Does $(c_n)_n$ converge?

Solution

Dividing numerator and denominator by the highest power: $a_n=\frac{2+1/n^2}{1+3/n^2}\to\frac21=2$ and $b_n=\frac{1+5/n}{1+1/n}\to1$, by the limit rules.

Even terms: $c_{2m}=a_{2m}+b_{2m}\to2+1=3$. Odd terms: $$c_{2m+1}=a_{2m+1}-b_{2m+1}\to2-1=1$$(subsequences of convergent sequences have the same limits, then the sum rule). Every term is even or odd, so by the practical rule $\limsup c_n=3$ and $\liminf c_n=1$. Since $1\neq3$, $(c_n)_n$ diverges (a convergent sequence would have $\limsup=\liminf$, Proof D).

Bonus (Übung 6.13 (i))

Prove that $\limsup(-a_n)=-\liminf a_n$ for every bounded real sequence $(a_n)_n$.

Solution

Frame: prove the identity for each tail first ($\sup$ of the negatives is minus the $\inf$), then let $n\to\infty$ with the limit rules.

For every $n$: $$\sup\{-a_k:k\ge n\}=-\inf\{a_k:k\ge n\},$$because $c$ is an upper bound of $\{-a_k\}$ exactly when $-c$ is a lower bound of $\{a_k\}$ (lesson 6). So the tail suprema of $(-a_n)$ are $-I_n$. Letting $n\to\infty$: $$\begin{aligned}\limsup(-a_n)&=\lim(-I_n)\\&=-\lim I_n\\&=-\liminf a_n\end{aligned}.$$ ∎

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

A 3,800-year-old recursive sequence. The Babylonian clay tablet YBC 7289 (around 1800 BC) shows a square with its diagonal labelled $1;24,51,10$ in base 60, which is $1.41421296\ldots$, correct to about six decimal places. The method was probably the recursion $a_{n+1}=\frac12\left(a_n+\frac2{a_n}\right)$, a close cousin of Worked example 2. It converges to $\sqrt2$ by exactly the argument you saw (monotone from the second term on, bounded below by $\sqrt2$), but astonishingly fast: the number of correct digits roughly doubles at every step. Starting from $a_1=1$: $1.5$, $1.41667$, $1.4142157$, $1.41421356237469$. Today this is Newton's method, and it's how computers still calculate square roots.

Where limsup shows up next. For a power series $\sum c_nz^n$, the radius of convergence is $\rho=\frac1{\limsup\sqrt[n]{|c_n|}}$ (the Cauchy–Hadamard formula, lesson 16). The $\limsup$ is needed because $\sqrt[n]{|c_n|}$ often doesn't converge, and it's the reason exam questions about power series quietly assume you're comfortable with today's lesson.

A sequence that visits every real number. List the rationals as $q_1,q_2,q_3,\dots$ (they're countable, lesson 3). Every real number is a limit of some subsequence, so this sequence has every real number as an accumulation point (Übung 6.16). And if $a_{n+1}-a_n\to0$, the accumulation points of a bounded sequence fill the whole interval $[\liminf a_n,\limsup a_n]$ (Übung 6.18): the tide sweeps every level in between, again and again.
