1. The story: the couriers between two cities

Across the river from the Free City lies a sister city with arrows of its own. The two councils agree to trade, and they hire couriers. A courier takes any journey of the Free City and delivers a journey in the sister city. The councils accept only fair couriers, and the rules are the same as for the surveyors of lesson 10: if two journeys are joined end to end, the delivery of the joint journey is the join of the two deliveries; if a journey is stretched, its delivery is stretched by the same factor. A surveyor was simply a courier whose sister city is a single line of numbers.

The first discovery is the same as for the surveyors. A fair courier only needs an instruction for each of the mapmaker's roads: “road one goes to this journey over there, road two to that one”. The fair rules then force every other delivery. And any instruction sheet works: for every choice of deliveries on the roads there is exactly one fair courier.

The second discovery: couriers can be combined. Two couriers walking side by side, whose deliveries are joined, form a fair courier; so does a courier whose deliveries are all doubled. The couriers form a city of their own.

The third discovery is the instruction book. Once both cities have chosen mapmaker's roads, every delivery can be written as a list of numbers over there. The instruction sheet of a courier becomes a table: one column per road at home, holding the address of its delivery across the river. With the table, a clerk who never leaves the office can compute any delivery: read the address of the journey at home, multiply by the table, and get the address of the delivery.

The last discovery is about lost parcels. Some journeys are delivered to the central square of the sister city: the courier carries them nowhere. These lost journeys form a district of the Free City. And all the journeys delivered to the same place over there form one parallel street of that district. So the parallel streets at home match the places reached over there, one to one, and the match is itself fair.

In mathematics: a fair courier is a linear map $T:V\to W$; the instruction sheet is the list $T(v_1),\dots,T(v_n)$ on a basis, and “any sheet works, and determines the courier” is the extension principle; the city of couriers is the vector space $\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$; the table is the matrix $[T]^B_C$, and the clerk's rule is $[T]^B_C[v]_B=[Tv]_C$; the lost journeys form the kernel $\mathrm{Ker}\,T$; the journeys delivered to the same place form a fiber $v+\mathrm{Ker}\,T$; and the one-to-one match is the isomorphism theorem $V/\mathrm{Ker}\,T\cong\mathrm{Im}\,T$.

2. Linear maps

Throughout, $K$ is a field and $V,W$ are vector spaces over the same $K$. In lesson 10 the target was $K$ itself; now it is any $W$. Every definition and proof of lesson 10, sections 2 and 3, carries over with $K$ replaced by $W$.

Definition (linear map; script Definition 3.1.1, p. 67). A map $T:V\to W$ is linear (a linear map, a linear transformation, a homomorphism) if

  1. $T(v_1+v_2)=T(v_1)+T(v_2)$ for all $v_1,v_2\in V$ (additivity), and
  2. $T(av)=aT(v)$ for all $a\in K$, $v\in V$ (homogeneity).

The set of all linear maps $V\to W$ is $\mathrm{Hom}_K(V,W)$, or $\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$ when $K$ is clear. One often writes $Tv$ for $T(v)$.

In the lecture: Halbeisen says “Vektorraumhomomorphismus” and writes $\varphi(x+_Vy)=\varphi(x)+_W\varphi(y)$ to stress that the two plus signs live in different spaces. He uses $\varphi,\psi$ for maps and $x_j$, $y_i$ for the basis vectors of $V$ and $W$; we follow the script: $T,S$ for maps, $B=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ and $C=(w_1,\dots,w_m)$ for bases. A functional (lesson 10) is exactly a linear map with $W=K$, so $V^*=\mathrm{Hom}(V,K)$.

Examples (script, pp. 68–70; check each against the two rules):

  • Trivial maps. The identity $\mathrm{Id}_V:V\to V$, $v\mapsto v$, and the zero map $0:V\to W$, $v\mapsto0_W$.
  • The main example. For a matrix $A=(a_{ij})\in M_{m\times n}(K)$, the map $m_A:K^n\to K^m$, $x\mapsto Ax$, where $Ax$ is the column with entries $(Ax)_i=\sum_{j=1}^na_{ij}x_j$ (script Definition 2.1.4). Read by columns: if $a_1,\dots,a_n$ are the columns of $A$, then $Ax=x_1a_1+\dots+x_na_n$. Some authors write $T_A$ for $m_A$.
  • Differentiation. $D:K[x]\to K[x]$, $a_0+a_1x+\dots+a_nx^n\mapsto a_1+2a_2x+\dots+na_nx^{n-1}$, where $2=1+1$, $3=1+1+1$, … in $K$. Linearity is the rule $(ap+bq)'=ap'+bq'$, which here follows from comparing coefficients.
  • Analysis. $f\mapsto\int_0^1f(x)\,dx$ on the Riemann-integrable functions (a functional).
  • Multiplication by a polynomial. $p\mapsto x^{73}p$ from $\mathbb F_{37}[x]_{\le12}$ to $\mathbb F_{37}[x]_{\le210}$ (Example 3.1.11; any fixed polynomial works).
  • The shift. $S:K^\infty\to K^\infty$, $(a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots)\mapsto(a_2,a_3,\dots)$. It maps $\mathrm{Fib}(K)$ into itself, because shifting a Fibonacci-type sequence gives one again.
  • Geometry. Rotations about $0$, reflections in lines through $0$, stretching along an axis, projections onto a line through $0$ (Example 3.1.9).
  • Coordinates. The coordinate map $\Phi_B:V\to K^n$, $v\mapsto[v]_B$ (section 6).

Non-examples on $\mathbb R^2$: a translation $x\mapsto x+(1,0)$ (moves $0$); $x\mapsto(x_1^2,x_2)$ (doubling $x$ quadruples the first entry); $x\mapsto(|x_1|,0)$. On $\mathbb C$ as a complex vector space: $z\mapsto\bar z$. Each fails one rule, and one counterexample is enough to show it.

Proof A: first properties of a linear map (script Proposition 3.1.14 and Exercise 3.1.2, pp. 67–70)

Claim. Let $T:V\to W$ be linear. Then (a) $T(0_V)=0_W$ and $T(-v)=-T(v)$; (b) $T\big(\sum_{i=1}^na_iv_i\big)=\sum_{i=1}^na_iT(v_i)$ for all $n\ge1$, $a_i\in K$, $v_i\in V$. (c) A map $T:V\to W$ is linear if and only if $T(av_1+bv_2)=aT(v_1)+bT(v_2)$ for all $a,b\in K$ and $v_1,v_2\in V$.

The idea. This is Proof A of lesson 10 with values in $W$, and we take the script's route for (a): the zero vector satisfies $0_V+0_V=0_V$, so its image satisfies $T(0_V)+T(0_V)=T(0_V)$, and in a group the only element with $u+u=u$ is the neutral element. For (b), a linear combination is built from one sum and one multiple at a time.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line (a): “Since $0_V+0_V=0_V$, we have $T(0_V)=T(0_V+0_V)$.” Last line: “so $0_W=T(0_V)$.”
  • First line (b): “Induction on $n$; for $n=1$ this is homogeneity.” Last line: “which is the claim for $n+1$.”
  • First line (c): “($\Rightarrow$) Let $a,b\in K$, $v_1,v_2\in V$.” “($\Leftarrow$) Take $a=b=1$; then take $b=0$.”
  • The gap: in (a), add $-T(0_V)$ on both sides; in (b), split off the last term with additivity.
The storyWhat you write

Standing still twice is standing still, so the courier's delivery of “standing still” doubled is itself; only standing still over there has that property.

(a) Since $0_V+0_V=0_V$, additivity gives $T(0_V)=T(0_V+0_V)=T(0_V)+T(0_V)$. Adding $-T(0_V)\in W$ on both sides gives $0_W=T(0_V)$. Then $T(v)+T(-v)=T(v+(-v))=T(0_V)=0_W$, so $T(-v)=-T(v)$ (the inverse in $W$ is unique, lesson 4).

A trip along several roads is the trip so far, joined with one more stretched road.

(b) Induction on $n$. For $n=1$: $T(a_1v_1)=a_1T(v_1)$ by homogeneity. If the claim holds for $n$, then $T\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_iv_i\big)$ $=T\big(\sum_{i=1}^na_iv_i\big)+T(a_{n+1}v_{n+1})$ (additivity) $=\sum_{i=1}^na_iT(v_i)+a_{n+1}T(v_{n+1})$ (hypothesis, homogeneity), the claim for $n+1$.

One rule for two-road mixtures contains both rules.

(c) If $T$ is linear, (b) with $n=2$ gives the formula. Conversely, $a=b=1$ gives additivity. With $a=b=0$ the formula gives $T(0_V)=0\cdot T(v_1)+0\cdot T(v_2)=0_W$; then $b=0$, $v_2=0_V$ gives $T(av_1)=aT(v_1)+0_W=aT(v_1)$. ∎

Moves used: write $0$ as $0+0$ and distribute induction: base, hypothesis, step unfold the definition

The proof in one piece

(a) Because $0_V+0_V=0_V$, linearity gives $T(0_V)=T(0_V)+T(0_V)$; adding $-T(0_V)$ gives $T(0_V)=0_W$. Then $T(v)+T(-v)=T(0_V)=0_W$, so $T(-v)=-T(v)$.

(b) By induction on $n$: the case $n=1$ is homogeneity, and $T\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_iv_i\big)=T\big(\sum_{i=1}^na_iv_i\big)+a_{n+1}T(v_{n+1})$ $=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_iT(v_i)$ by additivity, homogeneity and the hypothesis.

(c) “$\Rightarrow$” is (b) for $n=2$. “$\Leftarrow$”: $a=b=1$ gives additivity; $a=b=0$ gives $T(0_V)=0_W$; then $b=0$, $v_2=0_V$ gives homogeneity. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. “Show that $T$ is linear” in an exam: use (c). Write $T(av_1+bv_2)$, insert the formula of $T$, and rearrange into $aT(v_1)+bT(v_2)$, naming the rule used (linearity of the derivative, of evaluation, distributivity of matrices).
  2. “Show that $T$ is not linear”: one counterexample. First try $T(0)\ne0$; then $T(2v)\ne2T(v)$ for a simple $v$.
  3. If asked for $T(0)=0$ itself: $T(0)=T(0+0)=T(0)+T(0)$, subtract $T(0)$.

In short: linear $\iff$ $T(av_1+bv_2)=aTv_1+bTv_2$; and $T(0)=0$ comes from $0=0+0$.

Worked example 1: linear or not? (script Examples 3.1.5–3.1.13)

Decide for each map whether it is linear. (a) $T:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2$, $T(x)=(x_1-2x_3,\ 4x_2)$. (b) $S:K^\infty\to K^\infty$, the shift $(a_1,a_2,\dots)\mapsto(a_2,a_3,\dots)$. (c) $R:M_{2\times2}(\mathbb R)\to M_{2\times2}(\mathbb R)$, $R(X)=X^T$ (transpose: rows become columns). (d) $Q:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2$, $Q(x)=(x_1x_2,\ x_2)$. (e) $E:\mathbb R[x]\to\mathbb R[x]$, $E(p)(x)=p(x+1)$.

  1. (a): write $T$ as $m_A$. Which matrix?
    Answer

    $T(x)=Ax$ with $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-2\\0&4&0\end{pmatrix}$: row $i$ holds the coefficients of the $i$-th entry. $m_A$ is linear because $A(ax+by)=aAx+bAy$ entry by entry: $\sum_ja_{ij}(ax_j+by_j)$ $=a\sum_ja_{ij}x_j+b\sum_ja_{ij}y_j$. Linear.

  2. (b): compare $S(a\,u+b\,w)$ and $aS(u)+bS(w)$ entry by entry.
    Answer

    The $n$-th entry of $S(au+bw)$ is the $(n+1)$-th entry of $au+bw$, which is $au_{n+1}+bw_{n+1}$; that is also the $n$-th entry of $aS(u)+bS(w)$. Sequences are equal when all entries are. Linear.

  3. (c) and (e): same test.
    Answer

    (c) The $(i,j)$ entry of $(aX+bY)^T$ is the $(j,i)$ entry of $aX+bY$, namely $ax_{ji}+by_{ji}$, which is the $(i,j)$ entry of $aX^T+bY^T$. Linear. (e) $E(ap+bq)(x)=(ap+bq)(x+1)$ $=ap(x+1)+bq(x+1)$ $=\big(aE(p)+bE(q)\big)(x)$. Linear: substituting $x+1$ commutes with sums and multiples.

  4. (d): find a counterexample.
    Answer

    $Q(2(1,1))=Q(2,2)=(4,2)$, but $2Q(1,1)=2(1,1)=(2,2)$. Not linear (not homogeneous). Note that $Q(0)=0$: passing the zero test proves nothing.

3. A linear map is determined by its values on a basis

This is the most used fact of the chapter. In lesson 10 it was Proofs B and C for functionals; the script states it once for all linear maps (Satz 3.1.15, p. 70) and stresses the two halves: uniqueness and existence. The proofs below are lesson 10's, word for word, with $W$ in place of $K$.

Proof B: two linear maps that agree on a basis are equal (script Satz 3.1.15, uniqueness; lecture §7)

Claim. Let $v_1,\dots,v_n$ be a basis of $V$ and $T:V\to W$ linear. For every $v=\sum_{j=1}^na_jv_j$:

$$T(v)=\sum_{j=1}^na_j\,T(v_j).$$

In particular, if $S,T:V\to W$ are linear with $S(v_j)=T(v_j)$ for $j=1,\dots,n$, then $S=T$.

The idea. Every vector is a combination of the basis, with its coordinates as coefficients (lesson 6), and a linear map respects combinations (Proof A). So the $n$ vectors $T(v_1),\dots,T(v_n)$ and the coordinates of $v$ compute $T(v)$; two maps with the same $n$ vectors compute the same $T(v)$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $v\in V$. Since $v_1,\dots,v_n$ is a basis, $v=\sum_ja_jv_j$ with $a_j\in K$.”
  • Last line: “Hence $S(v)=T(v)$ for all $v\in V$, i.e. $S=T$.”
  • The gap: apply Proof A (b) to $S$ and to $T$.

Proof C: any values on a basis come from exactly one linear map (script Satz 3.1.15, existence; the extension principle)

Claim. Let $v_1,\dots,v_n$ be a basis of $V$ and let $w_1,\dots,w_n\in W$ be any vectors. Then there is exactly one linear map $T:V\to W$ with $T(v_j)=w_j$ for $j=1,\dots,n$, namely

$$T\Big(\sum_{j=1}^na_jv_j\Big):=\sum_{j=1}^na_jw_j.$$

The idea. Proof B tells us what $T$ must be, so define it that way (“linear expansion”, “extend linearly”). For the definition to make sense, the coefficients $a_j$ of $v$ must be unique: that is where independence enters. Then check the two rules, using that the coordinates of $v+v'$ and of $\lambda v$ are the sums and multiples of the coordinates.

  • The gap: three checks: $T(v_j)=w_j$, additivity, homogeneity.

The proof in one piece

4. Maps between $K^n$ and $K^m$ are matrices

For $V=K^n$ with the standard basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$, Proof C says: a linear map $T:K^n\to K^m$ is a free choice of $n$ columns $T(e_1),\dots,T(e_n)\in K^m$. Put them side by side as a matrix.

Consequence (script Lemma 3.1.18, p. 72). For every linear map $T:K^n\to K^m$ there is exactly one matrix $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$ with $T=m_A$, namely the matrix whose $j$-th column is $T(e_j)$:

$$A=\big(\,T(e_1)\ \big|\ T(e_2)\ \big|\ \cdots\ \big|\ T(e_n)\,\big).$$

Proof. $Ae_j$ is the $j$-th column of $A$ (the column rule $Ax=\sum_jx_ja_j$ with $x=e_j$). So $m_A$ and $T$ are linear maps that agree on the basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$, hence $m_A=T$ by Proof B. If also $T=m_{A'}$, then the $j$-th column of $A'$ is $A'e_j=T(e_j)$, the $j$-th column of $A$, so $A'=A$. ∎

Rule of thumb: to find the matrix of $T:K^n\to K^m$, apply $T$ to $e_1,\dots,e_n$ and write the results as columns. Example: the rotation $R_\theta$ of $\mathbb R^2$ by the angle $\theta$ sends $e_1$ to $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ and $e_2$ to $(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$, so $R_\theta=m_A$ with $A=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$. The projection onto the first axis is $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$; the reflection in the diagonal $x_1=x_2$ swaps $e_1$ and $e_2$, so it is $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.

5. The vector space $\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$, and composition

Linear maps can be added and multiplied by scalars value by value, exactly like functionals in lesson 10.

Definition (the vector space $\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$; script Section 3.5, p. 97; lecture §7). For $T,S\in\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$ and $\lambda\in K$ define $T+S:V\to W$ and $\lambda T:V\to W$ by

$$\begin{aligned}(T+S)(v)&:=T(v)+S(v),\\(\lambda T)(v)&:=\lambda\,T(v)\qquad(v\in V).\end{aligned}$$

The zero vector is the zero map $0:V\to W$, $v\mapsto0_W$.

In the lecture the operations are written $\varphi+_{\mathrm{Hom}}\psi$ and $\lambda\cdot_{\mathrm{Hom}}\varphi$, to keep them apart from the operations of $W$ on the right-hand side.

Proof D: $\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$ is a vector space (lecture, Satz 7.1; script Lemma 3.5.1 and Proposition 3.5.2)

Claim. For $T,S\in\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$ and $\lambda\in K$, the maps $T+S$ and $\lambda T$ are linear. With these operations, $\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$ is a vector space over $K$.

The idea. The set $\mathrm{Abb}(V,W)$ of all maps $V\to W$ is a vector space with the value-by-value operations: each axiom is the same axiom of $W$, applied at each point $v$ (this is how $\mathbb R^{\mathbb R}$ in lesson 5 and $V^*$ in lesson 10 were checked). So it is enough to show that $\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$ is a subspace of $\mathrm{Abb}(V,W)$: it contains the zero map and is closed under the two operations. That is the script's second proof; it saves checking eight axioms again.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “$\mathrm{Abb}(V,W)$ with value-by-value operations is a vector space; we show that $\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$ is a subspace.”
  • Last line: “So $\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$ is a subspace of $\mathrm{Abb}(V,W)$, hence a vector space.”
  • The gap: (1) the zero map is linear; (2) $T+S$ is linear; (3) $\lambda T$ is linear. Each by the one-line test of Proof A (c).

6. Coordinates and the matrix of a linear map

Section 4 turned every linear map $K^n\to K^m$ into a matrix. For general $V$ and $W$ we first translate vectors into columns of numbers by choosing bases, and then the same trick works. From now on in this section, $V$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional, and a basis is an ordered basis: a list $B=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$, where the order matters.

Definition (coordinate vector, coordinate map; script Definition 3.3.1, p. 77). Let $B=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ be an ordered basis of $V$ and $v\in V$. The coordinate vector of $v$ with respect to $B$ is the column

$$[v]_B=\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\\vdots\\a_n\end{pmatrix}\in K^n,$$

where $a_1,\dots,a_n\in K$ are the scalars with $v=a_1v_1+\dots+a_nv_n$.

The coordinate vector is well defined because the $a_j$ are unique (lesson 6). The map $\Phi_B:V\to K^n$, $v\mapsto[v]_B$, is the coordinate map.

Consequence (script Proposition 3.3.2, p. 77). $\Phi_B$ is linear and bijective.

Proof. Linear: if $v=\sum_ja_jv_j$ and $v'=\sum_jb_jv_j$, then $av+bv'=\sum_j(aa_j+bb_j)v_j$, and by uniqueness these are the coordinates of $av+bv'$; so $[av+bv']_B=a[v]_B+b[v']_B$. Injective: if $[v]_B=[v']_B$, then $v=\sum_ja_jv_j=v'$. Surjective: any column $(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ is $[v]_B$ for $v=\sum_ja_jv_j$. ∎ (So $\Phi_B^{-1}(a_1,\dots,a_n)=\sum_ja_jv_j$. Lesson 12 draws the conclusion: every $n$-dimensional space “is” $K^n$.)

Examples (script Examples 3.3.3 and 3.3.4, pp. 78–79). For the standard basis $E$ of $K^n$, $[x]_E=x$. For $B=\big((1,1),(1,-1)\big)$ in $\mathbb R^2$, $\big[(x,y)\big]_B=\big(\frac{x+y}2,\frac{x-y}2\big)$, because $\frac{x+y}2(1,1)+\frac{x-y}2(1,-1)=(x,y)$. In $K[x]_3$ with $C=(1,\ x+1,$ $x^2+x+1,$ $x^3+x^2+x+1)$, the polynomial $p=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3$ has $[p]_C=(a_0-a_1,\ a_1-a_2,\ a_2-a_3,\ a_3)$: compare coefficients from $x^3$ downwards. The order matters: in $B'=(x^3,x^2,x,1)$, $[p]_{B'}=(a_3,a_2,a_1,a_0)$.

Definition (the matrix of a linear map; script Definition 3.3.7, p. 81; lecture §7). Let $T:V\to W$ be linear, $B=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ an ordered basis of $V$ and $C=(w_1,\dots,w_m)$ an ordered basis of $W$. The representation matrix of $T$ with respect to $B$ and $C$ is the $m\times n$ matrix whose $j$-th column is the coordinate vector of $T(v_j)$:

$$[T]^B_C=\big([Tv_1]_C\,\big|\cdots\big|\,[Tv_n]_C\big).$$

It lies in $M_{m\times n}(K)$. Equivalently, $[T]^B_C=(a_{ij})$ with

$$\begin{aligned}T(v_j)&=a_{1j}w_1+\dots+a_{mj}w_m\\&=\sum_{i=1}^ma_{ij}w_i\qquad(j=1,\dots,n).\end{aligned}$$

If $V=W$ and $B=C$, one writes $[T]_B:=[T]^B_B$.

In the lecture: Halbeisen writes $A_\varphi$ for the matrix of $\varphi$ with respect to $B_V=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ and $B_W=\{y_1,\dots,y_m\}$, with entries $\mu^j_i$ (upper index $j$ = column, lower index $i$ = row): $\varphi(x_j)=\sum_i\mu^j_iy_i$. It is the same matrix as $[\varphi]^{B_V}_{B_W}$; the bases are not in his notation, so always say which bases you use. The script's $[T]^B_C$ has the basis of the starting space on top, the basis of the target space below. Watch the size: rows = $\dim W$, columns = $\dim V$.

The method (three steps).

  1. Apply $T$ to each basis vector $v_j$ of $B$.
  2. Write each $T(v_j)$ as a combination of $w_1,\dots,w_m$; the coefficients form the column $[Tv_j]_C$. (If $C$ is a standard basis, read them off; otherwise solve a small system, or use the dual basis of lesson 10: the $i$-th coefficient is $w_i^*(Tv_j)$.)
  3. Put the columns side by side, in the order of $B$.

Proof E: the matrix computes the map (script Proposition 3.3.8, p. 81; lecture §7, $A_\varphi\cdot(\lambda_j)=(\alpha_i)$)

Claim. With $T$, $B$, $C$ as in the definition, for every $v\in V$:

$$[T]^B_C\,[v]_B=[Tv]_C.$$

The idea. Write $v=\sum_jx_jv_j$. Linearity gives $Tv=\sum_jx_jT(v_j)$, and each $T(v_j)$ is $\sum_ia_{ij}w_i$. Collect the coefficient of each $w_i$: it is $\sum_ja_{ij}x_j$, the $i$-th entry of the matrix times the column. Uniqueness of coordinates in $W$ finishes it.

  • First line: “Let $v\in V$ and $[v]_B=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, i.e. $v=\sum_{j=1}^nx_jv_j$. Let $[T]^B_C=(a_{ij})$, i.e. $T(v_j)=\sum_{i=1}^ma_{ij}w_i$.”
  • Last line: “So the $i$-th coordinate of $Tv$ is $\sum_ja_{ij}x_j$, the $i$-th entry of $[T]^B_C[v]_B$.”

Coordinates with respect to $C$ are unique, so the $i$-th entry of $[Tv]_C$ is $\sum_ja_{ij}x_j$. That is the $i$-th entry of the product $[T]^B_C[v]_B$ (definition of $Ax$). Hence $[T]^B_C[v]_B=[Tv]_C$. ∎

The proof in one piece

Let $[T]^B_C=(a_{ij})$, so $T(v_j)=\sum_ia_{ij}w_i$, and let $v=\sum_jx_jv_j$, so $[v]_B=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$. Proof F: $T\mapsto[T]^B_C$ is linear and bijective (script Satz 3.5.3, p. 98; lecture p. 43; Serie 7, exercise 30)

Claim. Let $\dim V=n$, $\dim W=m$, with ordered bases $B=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ and $C=(w_1,\dots,w_m)$. The map

$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal M^B_C:\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)&\to M_{m\times n}(K),\\T&\mapsto[T]^B_C\end{aligned}$$

is linear and bijective: $[T+S]^B_C=[T]^B_C+[S]^B_C$, $[\lambda T]^B_C=\lambda[T]^B_C$, every matrix is the matrix of exactly one linear map.

The idea. Everything happens column by column. Column $j$ of $[T+S]^B_C$ is the coordinate vector of $(T+S)(v_j)=Tv_j+Sv_j$, and the coordinate map is linear, so it is the sum of the $j$-th columns. Injective: equal matrices mean equal values on the basis, so equal maps (Proof B). Surjective: a matrix prescribes values on the basis, and Proof C builds the map.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line (linear): “Let $T,S\in\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$, $\lambda\in K$. The $j$-th column of $[T+S]^B_C$ is $[(T+S)v_j]_C$.”
  • First line (injective): “Let $[T]^B_C=[S]^B_C$.” Last line: “so $T=S$.”
  • First line (surjective): “Let $A=(a_{ij})\in M_{m\times n}(K)$.” Last line: “so $[T]^B_C=A$.”
  • The gap: linearity of $\Phi_C$; Proof B; Proof C.

The proof in one piece

Linear: since the coordinate map $\Phi_C$ is linear, the $j$-th columns satisfy $[(T+S)v_j]_C=[Tv_j]_C+[Sv_j]_C$ and $[(\lambda T)v_j]_C=\lambda[Tv_j]_C$; so $[T+S]^B_C=[T]^B_C+[S]^B_C$ and $[\lambda T]^B_C=\lambda[T]^B_C$. How to reproduce this under exam pressure

7. Kernel, image and fibers

In Worked example 4 a nonzero vector was sent to $0$. The vectors sent to $0$, and more generally the vectors sent to one fixed $w$, describe exactly how far $T$ is from being injective.

Definition (kernel, image, fiber; script Proposition 3.2.1, p. 72, and Section 3.7.1, p. 112). Let $T:V\to W$ be linear.

  • The kernel of $T$ is $\mathrm{Ker}(T)=\{v\in V\mid Tv=0_W\}$.
  • The image of $T$ is $\mathrm{Im}(T)=\{Tv\mid v\in V\}$ (also written $\mathrm{Bild}(T)$).
  • For $w\in W$, the fiber (preimage) of $w$ is $T^{-1}(w)=\{v\in V\mid Tv=w\}$. So $\mathrm{Ker}(T)=T^{-1}(0_W)$.

For $A\in M_{m\times n}(K)$, $\mathrm{Ker}(m_A)=\{x\in K^n\mid Ax=0\}$ is the solution set of the homogeneous system $Ax=0$ (script Example 3.2.2); one writes $\mathrm{Ker}(A)$.

The fiber $T^{-1}(w)$ is defined for every map, also when $T$ has no inverse; it is a set, not the value of an inverse map. Remember from lesson 9: for $U\subseteq V$ a subspace and $v\in V$, $v+U=\{v+u\mid u\in U\}$ is a coset, and $v+U=v'+U\iff v-v'\in U$.

Proof G: kernel and image are subspaces, fibers are cosets of the kernel (script Propositions 3.2.1, 3.2.3 (1) and 3.7.1)

Claim. Let $T:V\to W$ be linear. (a) $\mathrm{Ker}(T)$ is a subspace of $V$ and $\mathrm{Im}(T)$ is a subspace of $W$. (b) If $w\in\mathrm{Im}(T)$ and $v_0\in V$ is any vector with $Tv_0=w$, then

$$T^{-1}(w)=v_0+\mathrm{Ker}(T);$$

if $w\notin\mathrm{Im}(T)$, then $T^{-1}(w)=\emptyset$. (c) $T$ is injective if and only if $\mathrm{Ker}(T)=\{0_V\}$.

The idea. (a) is the subspace test, with linearity doing the work. For (b), two vectors with the same image differ by a vector with image $0$: $T(v)=T(v_0)$ means $T(v-v_0)=0$. So the solutions of $Tv=w$ are one solution plus all solutions of $Tv=0$. (c) is (b) read for sizes: every nonempty fiber has as many elements as the kernel, so all fibers have at most one element exactly when the kernel does.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (a) Subspace test twice: “$0\in$; let $a,b\in K$ and $v_1,v_2\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$ …”, and the same in $\mathrm{Im}(T)$ with preimages.
  • (b) First line: “We show both inclusions.” “$\supseteq$: let $u\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$.” “$\subseteq$: let $v\in T^{-1}(w)$.” Last line: “so $v=v_0+(v-v_0)\in v_0+\mathrm{Ker}(T)$.”
  • (c) First line: “$\Rightarrow$: $0_V\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$, and …” “$\Leftarrow$: let $Tv_1=Tv_2$.” Last line: “so $v_1=v_2$.”
  • The gap: $T(v-v_0)=Tv-Tv_0$ (Proof A).
The storyWhat you write

Lost journeys joined and stretched stay lost; deliveries joined and stretched are again deliveries.

(a) $T(0_V)=0_W$, so $0_V\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$ and $0_W\in\mathrm{Im}(T)$. If $v_1,v_2\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$ and $a,b\in K$: $T(av_1+bv_2)=aTv_1+bTv_2=0_W$, so $av_1+bv_2\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$. If $w_1=Tv_1$, $w_2=Tv_2$: $aw_1+bw_2=T(av_1+bv_2)\in\mathrm{Im}(T)$.

Add a lost journey to a journey that reaches $w$: it still reaches $w$.

(b) $\supseteq$: for $u\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$, $T(v_0+u)=Tv_0+Tu=w+0_W=w$, so $v_0+u\in T^{-1}(w)$.

Two journeys that reach the same place differ by a lost journey.

$\subseteq$: let $Tv=w$. Then $T(v-v_0)=Tv-Tv_0=w-w=0_W$, so $u:=v-v_0\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$ and $v=v_0+u\in v_0+\mathrm{Ker}(T)$. If $w\notin\mathrm{Im}(T)$, no $v$ has $Tv=w$, so $T^{-1}(w)=\emptyset$.

Only the still journey is lost exactly when no two journeys meet.

(c) $\Rightarrow$: $T(0_V)=0_W$, and an injective $T$ has at most one preimage of $0_W$, so $\mathrm{Ker}(T)=\{0_V\}$. $\Leftarrow$: if $Tv_1=Tv_2$, then $T(v_1-v_2)=0_W$, so $v_1-v_2\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)=\{0_V\}$ and $v_1=v_2$. ∎

Moves used: the subspace test: zero, sums, multiples compare cosets by subtracting representatives decide membership element by element

The proof in one piece

(a) $T(0_V)=0_W$ gives $0_V\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$, $0_W\in\mathrm{Im}(T)$. For $v_1,v_2\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$: $T(av_1+bv_2)=aTv_1+bTv_2=0$. For $Tv_1,Tv_2\in\mathrm{Im}(T)$: $aTv_1+bTv_2=T(av_1+bv_2)\in\mathrm{Im}(T)$. So both are subspaces.

(b) Let $Tv_0=w$. If $u\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$, then $T(v_0+u)=w$. If $Tv=w$, then $T(v-v_0)=0$, so $v=v_0+(v-v_0)\in v_0+\mathrm{Ker}(T)$. Hence $T^{-1}(w)=v_0+\mathrm{Ker}(T)$. If $w\notin\mathrm{Im}(T)$, the fiber is empty by definition of the image.

(c) If $T$ is injective, $0_V$ is the only preimage of $0_W$. If $\mathrm{Ker}(T)=\{0\}$ and $Tv_1=Tv_2$, then $v_1-v_2\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$, so $v_1=v_2$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Kernel/image subspace: $T(0)=0$, then one line $T(av_1+bv_2)=aTv_1+bTv_2$.
  2. Fiber: “let $Tv_0=w$”; $\supseteq$ by $T(v_0+u)=w$; $\subseteq$ by $T(v-v_0)=0$.
  3. Injective $\iff$ kernel $0$: “$Tv_1=Tv_2\Rightarrow T(v_1-v_2)=0\Rightarrow v_1-v_2\in\mathrm{Ker}=\{0\}$”.

In short: all solutions of $Tv=w$ = one solution + all solutions of $Tv=0$. To show injectivity, check the kernel.

Definition (script Definition 3.2.5, p. 73; lecture p. 43). A linear map $T:V\to W$ is called a monomorphism if it is injective, an epimorphism if it is surjective, an isomorphism if it is bijective, an endomorphism if $W=V$, and an automorphism if $W=V$ and $T$ is bijective. $V$ and $W$ are isomorphic, written $V\cong W$, if there is an isomorphism $V\to W$. $\mathrm{End}(V):=\mathrm{Hom}(V,V)$.

So far: $\Phi_B:V\to K^n$ is an isomorphism (section 6), $T\mapsto[T]^B_C$ is an isomorphism $\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)\to M_{m\times n}(K)$ (Proof F), and lesson 10, Exercise 5 built an isomorphism $W^\perp\to(V/W)^*$. Proof H: the isomorphism theorem (script Proposition 3.7.16, p. 116)

Claim. Let $T:V\to W$ be linear and $N:=\mathrm{Ker}(T)$. Then

$$\bar T:V/N\to\mathrm{Im}(T),\qquad v+N\mapsto T(v)$$

is well defined, linear and bijective. So $V/\mathrm{Ker}(T)\cong\mathrm{Im}(T)$. Moreover $\bar T(\pi(v))=T(v)$ for the quotient map $\pi:V\to V/N$, $v\mapsto v+N$.

The idea. By Proof G, the fibers of $T$ are exactly the cosets $v+N$. So “the coset of $v$” and “the value $T(v)$” carry the same information: each coset is one fiber and corresponds to one point of the image. The map $\bar T$ just names this correspondence. Well defined: a different representative of the same coset differs by a kernel vector, which does not change $T$. Injective: different cosets are different fibers, so different values.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Well defined, first line: “Let $v+N=v'+N$, i.e. $v-v'\in N$.” Last line: “so $T(v)=T(v')$.”
  • Linear: use the operations of $V/N$: $(v+N)+(v'+N)=(v+v')+N$, $\lambda(v+N)=\lambda v+N$.
  • Injective, first line: “Let $\bar T(v+N)=0_W$.” Last line: “so $v+N=0+N$.” Surjective: “Let $w\in\mathrm{Im}(T)$, so $w=T(v)$ …”
  • The gap: each step is one use of $v+N=v'+N\iff v-v'\in N$.
The storyWhat you write

Well defined. If $v+N=v'+N$, then $v-v'\in N$ (lesson 9), so $T(v)-T(v')=T(v-v')=0$ and $T(v)=T(v')$. The value lies in $\mathrm{Im}(T)$.

Joining two streets and then reading the place is the same as joining the two places.

Linear. $\bar T\big((v+N)+(v'+N)\big)=\bar T\big((v+v')+N\big)$ $=T(v+v')=T(v)+T(v')$ $=\bar T(v+N)+\bar T(v'+N)$, and $\bar T\big(\lambda(v+N)\big)=\bar T(\lambda v+N)=\lambda T(v)=\lambda\bar T(v+N)$.

The only street that reaches the square is the district of lost journeys itself.

Injective. If $\bar T(v+N)=0_W$, then $T(v)=0_W$, so $v\in N$ and $v+N=0+N$, the zero of $V/N$. A linear map with kernel $\{0\}$ is injective (Proof G (c)).

The proof in one piece

Let $N=\mathrm{Ker}(T)$. If $v+N=v'+N$, then $v-v'\in N$, so $T(v)=T(v')$; hence $\bar T(v+N):=T(v)$ is well defined, with values in $\mathrm{Im}(T)$. By the definition of the operations on $V/N$ and the linearity of $T$, $\bar T((v+N)+(v'+N))=T(v+v')=T(v)+T(v')$ and $\bar T(\lambda(v+N))=T(\lambda v)=\lambda T(v)$, so $\bar T$ is linear. If $\bar T(v+N)=0$, then $v\in N$, so $v+N=N$ is the zero of $V/N$; thus $\mathrm{Ker}(\bar T)=\{0\}$ and $\bar T$ is injective. Every $w=T(v)\in\mathrm{Im}(T)$ equals $\bar T(v+N)$, so $\bar T$ is surjective. Hence $\bar T$ is an isomorphism and $V/\mathrm{Ker}(T)\cong\mathrm{Im}(T)$; by construction $\bar T\circ\pi=T$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Define $\bar T(v+N):=T(v)$ and prove well defined: $v-v'\in N\Rightarrow T(v)=T(v')$.
  2. Linear: one line each, through the operations of $V/N$.
  3. Injective: $\bar T(v+N)=0\Rightarrow v\in N\Rightarrow v+N=N$.
  4. Surjective: $w=T(v)$ is $\bar T(v+N)$.

In short: well defined, linear, kernel zero, onto the image. (FS26, question 1 asks exactly whether “every linear map $\varphi:V\to W$ induces an injective linear map $V/\mathrm{Ker}(\varphi)\to W$”: yes, this $\bar T$ followed by the inclusion of $\mathrm{Im}(T)$ in $W$.)

Why it's cool

Proof F says that, once bases are chosen, linear maps between finite-dimensional spaces are matrices: adding maps is adding matrices, and every matrix is a map. A computer cannot store “the derivative on polynomials of degree at most $3$”, but it can store a $4\times4$ table of numbers, and Proof E says that the table computes everything. Every piece of software that does linear algebra, from 3D graphics (a rotation is a $3\times3$ matrix) to search engines and neural networks (a layer is a matrix followed by a simple nonlinear step), lives on this translation. The extension principle is just as practical: to describe a linear map of a $1000$-dimensional space, give $1000$ vectors, not a value for each of infinitely many inputs.

    Answer

    (A) true: $v+\mathrm{Ker}(\varphi)\mapsto\varphi(v)$ (Proof H, followed by the inclusion $\mathrm{Im}(\varphi)\subseteq W$). (B) false: the natural map $w\mapsto\varphi(w)+\mathrm{Im}(\varphi)$ is the zero map, since $\varphi(w)\in\mathrm{Im}(\varphi)$. Exercise 1 (Serie 7, exercise 30)

$V,W$ are vector spaces over $K$ with bases $B_V=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ and $B_W=(y_1,\dots,y_m)$; $\varphi,\psi\in\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$ have the $m\times n$ matrices $A_\varphi=(\beta_{ij})$ and $A_\psi=(\gamma_{ij})$ with respect to these bases. (a) How must the sum $A_\varphi+A_\psi$ of two $m\times n$ matrices be defined so that $A_{\varphi+\psi}=A_\varphi+A_\psi$? (b) How must $\lambda\cdot A_\varphi$ be defined so that $A_{\lambda\varphi}=\lambda\cdot A_\varphi$? Prove both.

Hint

Compute the $j$-th column of $A_{\varphi+\psi}$: write $(\varphi+\psi)(x_j)=\varphi(x_j)+\psi(x_j)$ in the basis $y_1,\dots,y_m$ and use that coordinates are unique.

Solution

Frame: the $(i,j)$ entry of a matrix of a map is the coefficient of $y_i$ in the image of $x_j$; coordinates with respect to a basis are unique. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

(a) By definition of the matrices, $\varphi(x_j)=\sum_{i=1}^m\beta_{ij}y_i$ and $\psi(x_j)=\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{ij}y_i$. By the definition of $\varphi+\psi$ in $\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$ and the axioms of $W$, $(\varphi+\psi)(x_j)=\varphi(x_j)+\psi(x_j)=\sum_{i=1}^m(\beta_{ij}+\gamma_{ij})y_i$. Since the coefficients with respect to $y_1,\dots,y_m$ are unique, the $(i,j)$ entry of $A_{\varphi+\psi}$ is $\beta_{ij}+\gamma_{ij}$. So the definition must be entrywise: $(\beta_{ij})+(\gamma_{ij}):=(\beta_{ij}+\gamma_{ij})$. It is the only possible one, because every pair of $m\times n$ matrices is a pair $A_\varphi,A_\psi$ (Proof F: every matrix is the matrix of some map).

(b) Likewise $(\lambda\varphi)(x_j)=\lambda\varphi(x_j)=\sum_i(\lambda\beta_{ij})y_i$, so the $(i,j)$ entry of $A_{\lambda\varphi}$ is $\lambda\beta_{ij}$, and the definition must be $\lambda\cdot(\beta_{ij}):=(\lambda\beta_{ij})$, also entrywise. These are the usual operations on $M_{m\times n}(K)$, and (a), (b) together say that $\varphi\mapsto A_\varphi$ is linear (Proof F).

(a) Only $p_1$ has a constant term. (c) Compute $T(1)$. (d) Compute $T(p_2)$ and $T(p_3)$ first and compare with the list. For $T(p_1)$ and $T(p_4)$: no constant term, so no $p_1$ in them; solve for the coefficients of $p_2,p_3,p_4$. (e) Write $p=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3$; or use Proof G.

Solution

(a) Let $a_1p_1+a_2p_2+a_3p_3+a_4p_4=0$. Constant term: $7a_1=0$, so $a_1=0$. Coefficients of $x$, $x^2$, $x^3$: $a_2+a_3+a_4=0$, $a_2+2a_3+4a_4=0$, $a_2+3a_3+9a_4=0$. (b) For $p\in V$, $xp'$ has degree at most $3$, so $T$ maps $V$ to $V$. For $a,b\in\mathbb R$, $p,q\in V$: $T(ap+bq)=x(ap+bq)'=x(ap'+bq')=a\,xp'+b\,xq'=aT(p)+bT(q)$, by linearity of the derivative and distributivity. So $T$ is linear.

(c) $T(1)=x\cdot0=0$, so $1\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)$, $\mathrm{Ker}(T)\ne\{0\}$, and $T$ is not injective (Proof G (c)). Not invertible.

(d) $T(x^k)=kx^k$. So $T(p_2)=x+2x^2+3x^3=p_3$ and $T(p_3)=x+4x^2+9x^3=p_4$. $T(p_1)=2x+6x^2+12x^3$ has no constant term, so its $p_1$-coefficient is $0$; write it as $a\,p_2+b\,p_3+c\,p_4$: $a+b+c=2$, $a+2b+4c=6$, $a+3b+9c=12$, so $b+3c=4$, $b+5c=6$, giving $c=1$, $b=1$, $a=0$: $T(p_1)=p_3+p_4$. $T(p_4)=x+8x^2+27x^3$: $a+b+c=1$, $a+2b+4c=8$, $a+3b+9c=27$, so $b+3c=7$, $b+5c=19$, $c=6$, $b=-11$, $a=6$: $T(p_4)=6p_2-11p_3+6p_4$ (check: $x^3$: $6-33+54=27$ ✓; $x^2$: $6-22+24=8$ ✓; $x$: $6-11+6=1$ ✓). Hence

$$[T]_B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\0&0&0&6\\1&1&0&-11\\1&0&1&6\end{pmatrix}.$$

(e) For $p=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3$: $T(p)=a_1x+2a_2x^2+3a_3x^3$. This equals $x^3$ iff $a_1=0$, $a_2=0$, $a_3=\frac13$, with $a_0$ arbitrary. So $T^{-1}(\{x^3\})=\{\frac13x^3+c\mid c\in\mathbb R\}=\frac13x^3+\mathrm{Ker}(T)$, where $\mathrm{Ker}(T)$ is the constants, as Proof G (b) predicts.

Exercise 3 (HS21, problem 3 (c)–(e), 12 points)

$K$ is a field and $P_n\subseteq K[X]$ the polynomials of degree at most $n$, with the ordered basis $E=(1,X,\dots,X^n)$. (c) Show that $T:P_n\to P_n$, $f(X)\mapsto f(X+1)-\frac{df}{dX}$, is linear. (d) Find $[T]_E$; write it out for $n=3$. (e) Is $T$ an isomorphism?

Hint

(d) Expand $(X+1)^k$ with the binomial theorem: the term $kX^{k-1}$ cancels against the derivative. (e) Look at the highest power: $T(f)$ has the same leading term as $f$.

Solution

Frame: (c) the one-line test; (d) column $k$ is $[T(X^k)]_E$; (e) injective via the kernel, surjective by building preimages. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

(c) Substituting $X+1$ and differentiating are both linear (Worked example 1 (e); the derivative, section 2), and both keep the degree at most $n$. So $T(af+bg)=(af+bg)(X+1)-(af+bg)'$ $=af(X+1)+bg(X+1)-af'-bg'$ $=aT(f)+bT(g)$.

(d) By the binomial theorem, $(X+1)^k=\sum_{i=0}^k\binom kiX^i$, and $\frac{d}{dX}X^k=kX^{k-1}=\binom k{k-1}X^{k-1}$. So

$$T(X^k)=X^k+\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}\binom kiX^i\qquad(k\ge0),$$

and $[T]_E=(t_{ik})_{0\le i,k\le n}$ with $t_{kk}=1$, $t_{ik}=\binom ki$ for $i\le k-2$, and $t_{ik}=0$ for $i=k-1$ or $i>k$. For $n=3$: $T(1)=1$, $T(X)=X$, $T(X^2)=X^2+1$, $T(X^3)=X^3+3X+1$, so

$$[T]_E=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1&1\\0&1&0&3\\0&0&1&0\\0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

(e) Yes. Injective: let $f\ne0$ with leading term $cX^d$, $c\ne0$. By (d), $T(f)=cX^d+$ (terms of degree $<d$), so $T(f)\ne0$. Thus $\mathrm{Ker}(T)=\{0\}$ and $T$ is injective (Proof G (c)). Surjective: we show $X^k\in\mathrm{Im}(T)$ by induction on $k$. $X^0=T(1)$. If $1,X,\dots,X^{k-1}\in\mathrm{Im}(T)$, then $X^k=T(X^k)-\sum_{i\le k-2}\binom kiX^i$ is a combination of elements of the subspace $\mathrm{Im}(T)$ (Proof G (a)), so it lies in it. Hence $\mathrm{Im}(T)\supseteq\mathrm{Sp}(E)=P_n$. So $T$ is bijective and linear: an isomorphism. (Lesson 15 gives a shortcut: an injective linear map between spaces of the same finite dimension is bijective.)

$D(\sum_ka_kx^k)=\sum_{k\ge1}k\,a_kx^{k-1}$, and $k\cdot a_k$ means $a_k$ added $k$ times. In characteristic $0$, $k\cdot1\ne0$ for $k\ge1$; in $\mathbb F_p$, $k\cdot1=0$ exactly when $p\mid k$. For surjectivity over $\mathbb F_p$, try to hit $x^{p-1}$.

(f) $\dim\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$ and $\dim M_{m\times n}(K)$ agree when $\dim V=n$ and $\dim W=m$.

Hint

(a) Apply $T$ to a relation $\sum a_iv_i=0$. (b) Think of the zero map. (c) Subspace test, using $T(0)=0$. (f) Proof F.

Solution

Frame: each answer is a short proof or one counterexample.

(a) True. If $\sum a_iv_i=0$, then $\sum a_iT(v_i)=T(0)=0$ (Proof A), so all $a_i=0$ by independence of the $T(v_i)$.

(b) False. The zero map sends the independent vector $e_1$ to $0$, and the family $(0)$ is dependent. (It is true for injective $T$: if $\sum a_iT(v_i)=0$, then $T(\sum a_iv_i)=0$, so $\sum a_iv_i\in\mathrm{Ker}(T)=\{0\}$ and all $a_i=0$.)

(c) True (script Exercise 3.2.4). $T(0)=0\in U$, so $0\in T^{-1}(U)$. If $Tv_1,Tv_2\in U$, then $T(av_1+bv_2)=aTv_1+bTv_2\in U$ because $U$ is a subspace. For $U=\{0\}$ this is the kernel.

(d) False: $0\mapsto1\ne0$. (It is “affine”, a linear map plus a constant.)

(e) True. If $Tv_0=w$, then $T^{-1}(w)=v_0+\mathrm{Ker}(T)$ (Proof G), and $u\mapsto v_0+u$ is a bijection $\mathrm{Ker}(T)\to v_0+\mathrm{Ker}(T)$ with inverse $x\mapsto x-v_0$.

(f) True. Proof F gives a linear bijection between them, and lesson 12 shows that a linear bijection preserves dimension; both are $mn$.
