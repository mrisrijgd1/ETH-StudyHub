Why it matters for the exam

Work and energy are the most used tool of the whole exam. Problem 2 almost always needs a speed from an energy balance (the halfpipe in FS25, the fastest path in FS26, the ramp in FS24), and the multiple-choice part asks directly about work and power (FS24, FS25, HS25, FS26: all in the exercises below). This lesson builds the definitions; lesson 6 turns them into energy conservation, the fastest way to solve most problem-2 parts.

Recap of lesson 4 (3 min)

  1. A lantern hangs in a train that accelerates with $A$. At what angle does it hang, and which way?

    Answer

    $\tan\theta=\frac Ag$, tilted backwards, against the acceleration: in the train's frame the inertial force $-m\vec A$ pushes it back.

  2. Which fictitious force acts on a body at rest in a rotating frame?

    Answer

    Only the centrifugal force $m\omega^2r_\perp$, outwards from the axis. The Coriolis force needs a velocity $\vec v\,'$ relative to the frame.

  3. Why does the Coriolis force never do work?

    Answer

    $\vec F_C=-2m\,\vec\omega\times\vec v\,'$ is perpendicular to $\vec v\,'$. Today you'll see the general rule: a force perpendicular to the motion does no work.

1. The story: the castle well

Spring comes to the fortress, and the cisterns are empty. Two servants must bring water up from the deep well in the courtyard. Mira winds the windlass and lifts a full bucket twenty metres; Tomas carries buckets across the flat courtyard to the kitchen all morning and comes back exhausted. “I've done more work than you,” he says. The steward, who keeps the accounts, shakes his head: “By my book, you did none at all.”

In the afternoon the steward buys a horse to turn a bigger windlass. The horse lifts the same buckets as Mira, but four times as fast. “Same work,” says the steward, “but I pay for the horse by the hour.”

In physics: work is force times the distance moved along the force. Carrying a bucket horizontally at constant height costs muscle effort, but the holding force (up) is perpendicular to the motion (sideways): it does no work. Lifting does work, and the same amount whether you are fast or slow. How fast you do work is the power. And whatever work the forces do on a body shows up as a change of its kinetic energy. That's the plan for this lesson.

2. The toolbox: line element and gradient

Before work and energy, Ensslin's script takes a short excursion into vector analysis (section 4.1). You need two pieces of it now and two more in lesson 6; the rest is for Physics II.

The line element (section 4.1.1, page 93). Along a curve, a tiny step is the vector

$$d\vec r=\begin{pmatrix}dx\\dy\\dz\end{pmatrix}=dx\,\hat x+dy\,\hat y+dz\,\hat z,$$

with length $|d\vec r|=ds=\sqrt{dx^2+dy^2+dz^2}$.

If the curve is given as $\vec r(t)$ (for example the trajectory of a body), then $d\vec r=\dot{\vec r}\,dt=\vec v\,dt$: the step points along the velocity. That's how you will compute every line integral in practice.

The gradient (section 4.1.4, page 98). A scalar field $u(x,y,z)$ assigns a number to every point: a height on a map, a temperature, later a potential energy. Its gradient is the vector of its partial derivatives,

$$\nabla u=\operatorname{grad}u=\begin{pmatrix}\partial u/\partial x\\ \partial u/\partial y\\ \partial u/\partial z\end{pmatrix}.$$

Its meaning comes from the total differential: moving by $d\vec r$, the field changes by

$$du=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}dx+\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}dy+\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}dz=\nabla u\cdot d\vec r.$$

Three consequences (script, pages 102–103):

  • Direction of steepest ascent. For a step of fixed length $ds$, $du=|\nabla u|\,ds\cos\alpha$ is largest when $d\vec r$ points along $\nabla u$. So $\nabla u$ points where $u$ grows fastest, and $|\nabla u|$ is that rate.
  • Perpendicular to level surfaces. On a level surface (Niveaufläche) $u=\text{const}$, every step $d\vec r$ along it has $du=0$, so $\nabla u\cdot d\vec r=0$: the gradient is perpendicular to the level surface. On a map, it's perpendicular to the contour lines.
  • Line integral of a gradient. Summing $du$ along any curve from $\vec r_1$ to $\vec r_2$: $\int_{\vec r_1}^{\vec r_2}\nabla u\cdot d\vec r=u(\vec r_2)-u(\vec r_1)$, whatever the curve. This is the heart of lesson 6.

Try it: the gradient on a map

The map shows a landscape with two hills; the lines are level lines of the height $u(x,y)$. Drag the dot (or tap anywhere). The blue arrow is $\nabla u$; the dashed line is the direction along the level line.

Wherever you put the dot, the arrow crosses the level line at a right angle, and it is longest where the lines are closest together (the steepest slope).

Divergence and curl, in two minutes (open if you want them now; lesson 6 uses the curl)

For a vector field $\vec v(x,y,z)$, the script defines (sections 4.1.6–4.1.7, pages 104–107):

  • the divergence $\nabla\cdot\vec v=\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}$, a number: how much the field “springs out” of a point (source strength, Quellstärke);
  • the curl, a vector: how much the field circulates around a point, $$\nabla\times\vec v=\begin{pmatrix}\partial v_z/\partial y-\partial v_y/\partial z\\ \partial v_x/\partial z-\partial v_z/\partial x\\ \partial v_y/\partial x-\partial v_x/\partial y\end{pmatrix}.$$

Example: $\vec v=(-y,x,0)$ circles the origin. Its divergence is $0+0+0=0$, its curl is $(0,0,1-(-1))=(0,0,2)$. The field $\vec v=(x,y,0)$ points straight outwards: divergence $2$, curl $0$.

Gauss's theorem (the flux out of a closed surface equals the integral of the divergence inside) and Stokes's theorem (the circulation around a loop equals the flux of the curl through it) are stated in the script; Physics I exams don't ask for them.

3. Work

A constant force along a straight path (section 4.2.1, page 109). If the point where a constant force $\vec F$ acts moves by the straight displacement $\vec s$, the force does the work

$$W=\vec F\cdot\vec s=F_\parallel\,s=F\,s\cos\alpha,$$

where $\alpha$ is the angle between $\vec F$ and $\vec s$, and $F_\parallel=F\cos\alpha$ is the component of the force along the motion. The unit is the joule: $1\,\mathrm J=1\,\mathrm{N\,m}=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s^2}=1\,\mathrm{W\,s}$.

  • $\alpha<90^\circ$: $W>0$. Gravity on a falling stone.
  • $\alpha>90^\circ$: $W<0$. Gravity on a stone thrown upwards; friction, always.
  • $\alpha=90^\circ$: $W=0$. Gravity on a body sliding horizontally; the holding force on Tomas's bucket.

The script's footnote makes the steward's point: carrying a suitcase between two points at the same height is no work in the sense of physics, and movers carrying a piano down the stairs do negative work, even if they charge a positive price.

A general force along a curved path (pages 109–110). If the force changes from place to place, or the path $\Gamma$ from $\vec r_1$ to $\vec r_2$ is curved, cut the path into tiny steps $d\vec r$, on each of which the force is constant, and add up:

$$W=\int_{\vec r_1(\Gamma)}^{\vec r_2}\vec F\cdot d\vec r.$$

Here $\vec F$ is the sum of all forces acting at that point, unless you ask for the work of one particular force. Rupp writes the same thing as $W=\int_\Gamma\vec F\cdot d\vec r$, with the path under the integral sign.

Recipe: computing a work integral

  1. Write the force $\vec F(x,y,z)$ in components.
  2. Straight path along an axis (the usual exam case): if the motion is along $x$, then $d\vec r=dx\,\hat x$ and $W=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}F_x(x)\,dx$. Radial motion: $W=\int_{r_1}^{r_2}F_r(r)\,dr$.
  3. Curved path: parametrise it, $\vec r(t)$ for $t$ from $t_1$ to $t_2$. Then $d\vec r=\dot{\vec r}\,dt$ and $W=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\vec F(\vec r(t))\cdot\dot{\vec r}(t)\,dt$, an ordinary integral.
  4. Shortcut: if $\vec F\perp d\vec r$ all along the path, $W=0$ without any integral. If $\vec F$ is constant, $W=\vec F\cdot(\vec r_2-\vec r_1)$, whatever the path.
  5. Check the sign: does the force help the motion ($W>0$) or oppose it ($W<0$)?

Worked example 1: lifting the bucket by two paths

A bucket of mass $m$ is brought from the bottom of the well, $\vec r_1=(0,0)$, to the kitchen door, $\vec r_2=(\ell,h)$ (axis $x$ horizontal, $y$ up). Path A: straight up by $h$, then sideways by $\ell$. Path B: the straight diagonal. What work does gravity $\vec F_G=-mg\,\hat y$ do on each path?

  1. Path A, first piece (up): $d\vec r=dy\,\hat y$. The work?

    Answer

    $\int_0^h(-mg)\,dy=-mgh$. Gravity opposes the lift.

  2. Path A, second piece (sideways): the work?

    Answer

    $d\vec r=dx\,\hat x\perp\vec F_G$, so $\vec F_G\cdot d\vec r=0$: no work. In total, $W_A=-mgh$.

  3. Path B: parametrise $\vec r(t)=(\ell t,ht)$ for $t\in[0,1]$. The work?

    Answer

    $\dot{\vec r}=(\ell,h)$, so $\vec F_G\cdot\dot{\vec r}=(0,-mg)\cdot(\ell,h)=-mgh$, and $W_B=\int_0^1(-mgh)\,dt=-mgh$.

  4. What do you notice?

    Answer

    Both paths give $-mgh$: for gravity only the height difference counts. That's no accident (the force is constant, see the recipe's shortcut), and lesson 6 makes it a principle: gravity is a conservative force.

Worked example 2: stretching a spring

A spring with spring constant $D$ (Rupp's and the formula sheet's letter; some books write $k$) is stretched slowly from its rest length by $\ell$. The spring force is $F_x=-Dx$. What work does the spring force do, and what work do you do?

  1. Set up the integral for the spring force.

    Answer

    The motion is along $x$ from $0$ to $\ell$: $W_{\text{spring}}=\int_0^\ell(-Dx)\,dx=-\frac12D\ell^2$.

  2. You pull slowly, so your force just balances the spring: $F_x=+Dx$. Your work?

    Answer

    $W_{\text{you}}=\int_0^\ell Dx\,dx=\frac12D\ell^2$. This is the energy stored in the spring, the $U(x)=\frac12D(x-x_0)^2$ on the formula sheet (page 2, left).

  3. Why is the answer not simply “force times distance” $=D\ell\cdot\ell$?

    Answer

    The force grows from $0$ to $D\ell$ along the way. The integral averages it: the area of the triangle under $F(x)=Dx$, which is $\frac12\cdot\ell\cdot D\ell$.

Motions without work (script, page 111). (1) Uniform circular motion: the centripetal force $\vec F_{ZP}=-m\frac{v^2}r\hat r$ is perpendicular to $d\vec r=\vec v\,dt$, so $W=0$. (2) Frictionless horizontal sliding: gravity $(0,-mg)$ is perpendicular to $d\vec r=(dx,0)$. The same holds for the normal force on any frictionless surface, the tension of a pendulum string and the Coriolis force: all perpendicular to the motion, all workless.

Try it: does the path matter?

Two force fields, and a family of paths from A to B. Bend the path with the slider and watch the work. Left choice: gravity, a constant force down. Right choice: a “whirl” $\vec F=F_0\,(-y,\,x)$ that circles the origin.

For gravity the number never moves. For the whirl it changes with every path, and on a closed loop it isn't zero. Hold on to this picture: it is exactly the difference between conservative and non-conservative forces in lesson 6.

4. Power

Power is work per time (section 4.2.2, page 111):

$$P=\frac{dW}{dt},\qquad 1\,\mathrm W=1\,\mathrm{J/s}=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s^3}.$$

The old unit horsepower is $1\,\mathrm{PS}\approx735\,\mathrm W$. If the point where $\vec F$ acts moves with velocity $\vec v$, then $dW=\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\vec F\cdot\vec v\,dt$, so

$$P=\vec F\cdot\vec v.$$

Conversely, $W=\int P\,dt$; for constant power, $W=P\,\Delta t$. The horse and Mira do the same work $mgh$ per bucket, but the horse delivers four times the power.

Worked example 3: the car on the hill (script, page 111)

A car of mass $m=1000\,\mathrm{kg}$ drives at $v=72\,\mathrm{km/h}=20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ up a road with gradient $\tan\alpha=10\,\%$. Take $g\approx10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

  1. What power does the engine need just to climb?

    Answer

    Along the road the engine must balance the downhill component of the weight, $mg\sin\alpha$. With $\vec F\parallel\vec v$: $P_1=mg\sin\alpha\cdot v\approx mg\,\alpha\,v$ (small angle, $\sin\alpha\approx\tan\alpha=0.1$) $=1000\cdot10\cdot0.1\cdot20\,\mathrm W=20\,\mathrm{kW}$.

  2. At the same speed it also accelerates with $a=5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. How much extra power?

    Answer

    The extra force is $ma$, so $P_2=mav$ $=1000\cdot5\cdot20\,\mathrm W=100\,\mathrm{kW}$. Accelerating is expensive.

  3. Air drag is $F=\frac\rho2c_WAv^2$ (lesson 3). How does its power grow with speed?

    Answer

    $P_3=Fv=\frac\rho2c_WAv^3\propto v^3$. Driving twice as fast costs eight times the power against the air.

Try it: the power budget of a car

A car of 1000 kg with $c_WA=0.7\,\mathrm{m^2}$ in air of density $1.2\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, at constant speed. Change the slope and the speed.

On a steep slope at low speed, climbing dominates; on the flat motorway, the air takes almost everything, growing like $v^3$.

5. Kinetic energy and the work–energy theorem

Now the payoff (section 4.3, page 112). Let $\vec F$ be the total force on a point mass of constant mass $m$, so $\vec F=m\frac{d\vec v}{dt}$. Along its trajectory, $d\vec r=\vec v\,dt$:

$$\begin{aligned}W&=\int_{\vec r_1}^{\vec r_2}\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\int m\frac{d\vec v}{dt}\cdot\vec v\,dt\\&=\int_{\vec v_1}^{\vec v_2}m\,\vec v\cdot d\vec v=\frac m2\left(v_2^2-v_1^2\right).\end{aligned}$$

The middle step uses $\frac{d}{dt}\left(\vec v\cdot\vec v\right)=2\,\vec v\cdot\frac{d\vec v}{dt}$. So define

Kinetic energy and the work–energy theorem $$T=\frac12mv^2=\frac{p^2}{2m},\qquad W_{1\to2}=T_2-T_1.$$

The work done by the total force equals the change of kinetic energy. It holds for every force (conservative or not), for speeds much smaller than the speed of light. Rupp, the script and the formula sheet write $T$; some books write $E_{\text{kin}}$.

Check with uniform circular motion: $|\vec v|$ is constant, so $T$ doesn't change, and indeed the centripetal force does no work. A force perpendicular to the velocity can change the direction of motion, never the speed.

Worked example 4: the braking distance

A cart of mass $m$ slides on a horizontal road with speed $v_0$ and brakes with locked wheels (sliding friction coefficient $\mu_G$). How far does it slide?

  1. Which forces do work?

    Answer

    Weight and normal force are vertical, the motion horizontal: no work. Only sliding friction $F_R=\mu_GF_N=\mu_Gmg$, opposite to the motion, does work: $W=-\mu_Gmg\,d$ over the distance $d$.

  2. Apply the work–energy theorem.

    Answer

    $W=T_2-T_1$ with $T_2=0$: $-\mu_Gmg\,d=0-\frac12mv_0^2$, so

    $$d=\frac{v_0^2}{2\mu_Gg}.$$

    The mass cancels, and doubling the speed quadruples the distance. No time, no acceleration, no kinematics needed.

Why it's cool. In the 1780s James Watt needed to sell steam engines to brewers who used horses. He measured a horse turning a mill and defined the “horsepower” so that buyers could compare. Today the unit of power carries his name, and the work–energy theorem is how engineers still size every engine, lift and brake: you never need the details of the motion, only the forces along the path.

6. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed.

Exercise 1 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.3(c), 1 point, original)

Which statement about mechanical power is correct? (A) Power is independent of the force applied. (B) Work is defined as the power delivered per time. (C) Power is computed as the integral of the force over the path. (D) If a constant power is delivered for a longer time, the total work done is larger.

Solution

D. For constant power, $W=P\,\Delta t$ grows with $\Delta t$. (A) $P=\vec F\cdot\vec v$ depends on the force. (B) It's the other way round: power is work per time, $P=\frac{dW}{dt}$. (C) The integral of the force over the path is the work, not the power.

Exercise 2 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.3(e), 1 point, original)

A body of mass $m$ moves in the $x$-direction on a plane from $x=0$ to $x=L$. A force $F(x)=kx$ with a constant $k$ acts parallel to the plane; friction is negligible. Which statement about the work of this force is correct? (A) The work depends on the body's speed at $x=0$. (B) The work is $W=\frac12kL^2$. (C) The work depends linearly on $L$: $W=kL$. (D) The work is zero, because the force acts along the motion.

Solution

B. $W=\int_0^LF(x)\,dx=\int_0^Lkx\,dx=\frac12kL^2$. (A) Work depends only on the force along the path, not on how fast the body moves. (C) That would be a constant force $kL$; here the force grows along the way. (D) A force along the motion does maximal work, not zero.

Exercise 3 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.3(d), 1 point, original)

A particle moves in a two-dimensional conservative force field $\vec F(x,y)$, exactly along an equipotential line (the potential energy $U(x,y)$ never changes along the way). Which statement about the force $\vec F$ is correct? (A) $\vec F$ must be zero everywhere on this path. (B) $\vec F$ does work continuously, because the particle moves. (C) $\vec F$ does no work, because the force vector is always exactly perpendicular to the direction of motion. (D) $\vec F$ must always point parallel to the direction of motion.

Solution

C. In a conservative field $\vec F=-\nabla U$ (formula sheet, page 2, right; lesson 6). The gradient is perpendicular to the level lines of $U$, so $\vec F\perp d\vec r$ along an equipotential line, and $\vec F\cdot d\vec r=0$. (A) The force need not vanish; it just points across the line. (B) Moving is not enough: only the component along the motion does work. (D) Parallel to the motion would change $U$.

Exercise 4 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.1(a), 1 point, original)

A cyclist trains on a climb for $\Delta t=10\,\mathrm{min}$ with a constant cadence of $f=90$ pedal turns per minute. The power meter shows a constant power $P=300\,\mathrm W$. With what force $F$ does she push on the pedals? The crank is $l=\frac16\,\mathrm m\approx165\,\mathrm{mm}$ long. (A) $F=\frac{300}{90\cdot6}\,\mathrm N$ (B) $F=\frac{600}{\pi}\,\mathrm N$ (C) $F=300\,\mathrm N$ (D) $F=2\pi\cdot300\cdot6\cdot90\,\mathrm N$

Solution

B. Take the force tangential to the pedal's circle, so $\vec F\parallel\vec v$ and $P=Fv$. The pedal moves on a circle of radius $l$ with angular velocity $\omega=2\pi f=2\pi\cdot\frac{90}{60\,\mathrm s}=3\pi\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $v=l\omega=\frac16\cdot3\pi\,\mathrm{m/s}=\frac\pi2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Then

$$F=\frac Pv=\frac{300\,\mathrm W}{\frac\pi2\,\mathrm{m/s}}=\frac{600}\pi\,\mathrm N\approx190\,\mathrm N.$$

Check (C) and (D) by size: $300\,\mathrm N$ would need $v=1\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and (D) is absurdly large. The trap is forgetting the minutes-to-seconds conversion or the $2\pi$.

Exercise 5 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.1(b), 1 point, original)

Same cyclist. What height difference $\Delta h$ does she climb in the training session? The total mass (cyclist, bike and bottles) is $m=81\,\mathrm{kg}$; $10\,\%$ of the power is lost to friction and air drag; $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. (A) $\Delta h=\frac{81}{200}\,\mathrm m$ (B) $\Delta h=\frac{0.9\cdot300}{81}\,\mathrm m$ (C) $\Delta h=\frac{1980}{81}\,\mathrm m$ (D) $\Delta h=200\,\mathrm m$

Solution

D. The work delivered is $W=P\,\Delta t=300\,\mathrm W\cdot600\,\mathrm s=180\,000\,\mathrm J$. Of this, $90\,\%$ lifts the mass (she rides at constant cadence, so her kinetic energy doesn't change): $0.9\cdot180\,000\,\mathrm J=mg\,\Delta h$, so

$$\Delta h=\frac{162\,000\,\mathrm J}{81\,\mathrm{kg}\cdot10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}=200\,\mathrm m.$$

(B) forgets the time: it's a power divided by a weight, not a height.

Exercise 6 (FS26, problem 3 “Weltraumlift”, part (a), 1 point, original)

A planet has mass $M$ and radius $R$. Assume for this part that it doesn't rotate. What work must be done to carry a test mass $m$ slowly from the ground ($r=R$) up to the height $r=r_S$?

Solution

Gravity points inwards, $F_r=-G\frac{Mm}{r^2}$, and grows weaker with height, so you need the integral. Carrying slowly, the lifting force balances gravity: $F_{\text{lift},r}=+G\frac{Mm}{r^2}$. The motion is radial, $d\vec r=dr\,\hat r$:

$$W=\int_R^{r_S}G\frac{Mm}{r^2}\,dr=GMm\left[-\frac1r\right]_R^{r_S}=GMm\left(\frac1R-\frac1{r_S}\right).$$

Check: positive (you lift against gravity), and for $r_S=R+h$ with $h\ll R$ it becomes $GMm\frac{h}{R^2}=mgh$ with $g=\frac{GM}{R^2}$.

Exercise 7 (the crate on the ramp, exam style)

A crate of mass $m=20\,\mathrm{kg}$ is pulled at constant speed a distance $s=5\,\mathrm m$ up a ramp of angle $\alpha=30^\circ$ by a rope parallel to the ramp. The sliding friction coefficient is $\mu_G=0.2$. Take $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, $\cos30^\circ\approx0.87$. (a) Find the work done by each force: weight, normal force, friction and rope. (b) Check that the total work is zero, and say why it must be.

Solution

(a) The displacement $\vec s$ points up the ramp.

  • Weight: angle $90^\circ+\alpha$ to $\vec s$. $W_G=mg\,s\cos(90^\circ+\alpha)$ $=-mg\,s\sin\alpha$ $=-200\cdot5\cdot0.5\,\mathrm J$ $=-500\,\mathrm J$ (equivalently $-mgh$ with $h=s\sin\alpha=2.5\,\mathrm m$).
  • Normal force: perpendicular to the ramp, so $W_N=0$.
  • Friction: $F_R=\mu_GF_N=\mu_Gmg\cos\alpha$ $=0.2\cdot200\cdot0.87\,\mathrm N\approx35\,\mathrm N$, down the ramp, so $W_R=-F_Rs\approx-174\,\mathrm J$.
  • Rope: at constant speed the forces along the ramp balance, $F_S=mg\sin\alpha+F_R$ $\approx100+35=135\,\mathrm N$, so $W_S=F_Ss\approx+674\,\mathrm J$.

(b) $W_G+W_N+W_R+W_S$ $\approx-500+0-174+674=0$. It must be zero by the work–energy theorem: the speed is constant, so $T_2-T_1=0$.

Exercise 8 (the braking distance, a quick one)

A car brakes from $108\,\mathrm{km/h}$ with locked wheels on a wet road, $\mu_G=0.5$, $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. How long is the braking distance? And from $54\,\mathrm{km/h}$?

Solution

$108\,\mathrm{km/h}=30\,\mathrm{m/s}$: $d=\frac{v_0^2}{2\mu_Gg}=\frac{900}{2\cdot0.5\cdot10}\,\mathrm m=90\,\mathrm m$. At half the speed, $15\,\mathrm{m/s}$: $d=\frac{225}{10}\,\mathrm m=22.5\,\mathrm m$, a quarter.

Scoring yourself: Exercises 1–6 make 6 points. With 4 you're done. Exercise 7 is the kind of reasoning problem 2 and 3 need (each force, its work, the sum); Exercise 8 is a warm-up.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

A chocolate bar is a mountain. A 100 g bar of chocolate holds about $2.2\,\mathrm{MJ}$. A person of $70\,\mathrm{kg}$ climbing a height $h$ does the work $mgh$ against gravity, so the bar would lift you $h=\frac{2.2\cdot10^6}{70\cdot10}\,\mathrm m\approx3\,\mathrm{km}$. In reality your muscles convert only about a quarter of it into work (the rest is heat), so it's “only” about 800 m: still a good mountain.

Human power. A fit person can keep up about $100$–$200\,\mathrm W$ for an hour; a Tour de France rider climbs at $400\,\mathrm W$ or more, and a sprinter reaches $2000\,\mathrm W$ for a few seconds. One horsepower, $735\,\mathrm W$, really is more than a human can keep up.
