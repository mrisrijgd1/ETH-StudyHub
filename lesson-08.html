Recap of lesson 7 (5 min)

  • Continuity at $x_0$: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that $|x-x_0|<\delta$ implies $|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$. Here $\delta$ may depend on $\varepsilon$ and on $x_0$.
  • Rules: sums, products, quotients (no zero in the denominator) and compositions of continuous functions are continuous; polynomials are continuous.
  • Intermediate value theorem: on an interval, a continuous $f$ takes every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$. Proof: $x_0=\sup\{x: f(x)\le c\}$, and continuity with $\varepsilon$ = the gap rules out $f(x_0)<c$ and $f(x_0)>c$.

  1. Is $f(x)=\frac1{x^2+1}$ continuous on $\mathbb R$? Why?

    Answer

    Yes: a quotient of polynomials whose denominator is $\ge1$, never $0$.

  2. Does $x^3=x+1$ have a solution in $[1,2]$?

    Answer

    $g(x)=x^3-x-1$ is continuous with $g(1)=-1<0<5=g(2)$, so yes, by the IVT.

1. The story: the walled garden

Beyond the kingdom lie two kinds of land. There is the open meadow, which has no fence: you can walk towards its edge forever without ever arriving. There is the endless plain, which never ends at all. And there is the walled garden $[a,b]$: it has a wall on each side, and the walls belong to the garden. It is closed (the edges are included) and bounded (finite length). Mathematicians call such an interval compact.

The ground in each land is a continuous road from lesson 7: no cliffs. On the open meadow, the ground near the missing edge can rise forever, like $\tfrac1x$ on $(0,1]$, or creep towards a height it never reaches, like $x$ on $[0,1)$. On the endless plain it can simply keep climbing, like $x$ on $[0,\infty)$.

The walled garden makes three promises that the other lands cannot keep:

  1. No tower is infinitely high. The ground is bounded.
  2. There is a highest tower, and you can stand on top of it. The maximum is attained, and so is the minimum.
  3. One rule of caution fits the whole garden. If you want your height to change by less than $\varepsilon$, a single step size $\delta$ works everywhere, not a different one at every spot. This is uniform continuity.

All three promises are proved with the same weapon as the intermediate value theorem: the supremum. Before we get there, one more gift of lesson 7. A road that always climbs can be read backwards. Tell me a height, and I can tell you the one place where the road has that height. That is the inverse function theorem.

2. Inverse functions and roots

Recall (Definition 3.39, p. 150). $f:D\to\mathbb{R}$ is strictly increasing (streng monoton wachsend) if $x<y$ implies $f(x)<f(y)$ for all $x,y\in D$, and strictly decreasing if $x<y$ implies $f(x)>f(y)$. Strictly monotone means one of the two.

A strictly monotone function is automatically injective: if $x\neq y$, say $x<y$, then $f(x)<f(y)$ (or $>$), so $f(x)\neq f(y)$. So it has an inverse on its image. The theorem says this inverse is as nice as $f$.

Satz 3.62, Umkehrsatz (p. 161). Let $I$ be an interval and $f:I\to\mathbb{R}$ continuous and strictly monotone. Then $f(I)$ is again an interval, and $f:I\to f(I)$ has an inverse $f^{-1}:f(I)\to I$ that is continuous and strictly monotone, in the same direction as $f$. If $I=[a,b]$, then $f(I)$ is the closed interval with endpoints $f(a)$ and $f(b)$.

The payoff: $n$-th roots (Beispiel 3.63, p. 161)

Let $n\in\mathbb{N}$, $n\ge1$, and $f(x)=x^n$ on $[0,\infty)$.

  • Strictly increasing: if $0\le x<y$, multiplying the inequality $x<y$ with itself $n$ times gives $x^n<y^n$ (all factors are $\ge0$, and at least $y>0$).
  • Every $c\ge0$ is a value: by Bernoulli's inequality (Lemma 3.5, p. 131: $(1+a)^n\ge1+na$ for $a\ge-1$) we get $(1+c)^n\ge1+nc>c$. So $c$ lies between $0^n=0$ and $(c+1)^n$, and the IVT gives an $x\in[0,c+1]$ with $x^n=c$.
  • The theorem: so $f:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ is a continuous, strictly increasing bijection, and its inverse $\sqrt[n]{\ }:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ is continuous and strictly increasing.

This is the third time $\sqrt2$ appears in this course. In lesson 6 the supremum built it as a number. In lesson 7 the IVT found it as a crossing point. Now it's a value of a continuous function. With roots we can define rational powers, $x^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{x^m}$ for $x\ge0$, and $x^{-m/n}=1/x^{m/n}$ for $x>0$.

Proof A: the inverse function theorem

Not a classic exam question, but it uses only tools you have.

The idea. A road that always climbs meets every height once, so it can be read backwards, and reading it backwards can't create cliffs, because anything between two heights belongs to a place between the two corresponding positions.

What the definitions force (before any idea)

The theorem has three claims, so the proof has three parts. Parts (a) and (b) follow from what we know. Part (c) is “$g=f^{-1}$ is continuous at every $y_0\in f(I)$”, and the definition of continuity forces its frame:

  • First line: “Let $y_0\in f(I)$ and $\varepsilon>0$, and put $x_0=g(y_0)$.”
  • Last line: “…so $|y-y_0|<\delta$ implies $|g(y)-g(y_0)|<\varepsilon$ for all $y\in f(I)$.”
  • The gap to fill: find a $\delta>0$. That's the only real idea.
The storyWhat you write

We may assume the road climbs. A road that always descends is a climbing road walked from the other end.

Without loss of generality $f$ is strictly increasing; otherwise replace $f$ by $-f$, which is strictly increasing and continuous.

A climbing road meets each height it reaches exactly once. So the question “where is the road at height $y$?” always has exactly one answer. Reading the road backwards is the function $g$.

(a) $f:I\to f(I)$ is surjective by definition of $f(I)$, and injective because it is strictly increasing. Hence it is bijective, and $g=f^{-1}:f(I)\to I$ exists.

Further along means higher, and higher means further along. Read backwards: a higher height belongs to a later place.

For $x_1,x_2\in I$: $x_1<x_2\iff f(x_1)<f(x_2)$. (“$\Rightarrow$” is the definition. “$\Leftarrow$”: if $x_1\ge x_2$, then $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$ or $f(x_1)>f(x_2)$, contradicting $f(x_1)<f(x_2)$.) With $y_i=f(x_i)$ this reads $g(y_1)<g(y_2)\iff y_1<y_2$, so $g$ is strictly increasing.

Between the lowest and the highest height the road reaches, no height is skipped. That's lesson 7's unbroken road.

(b) If $I=[a,b]$: monotonicity gives $f(a)\le f(x)\le f(b)$, so $f([a,b])\subseteq[f(a),f(b)]$, and the intermediate value theorem gives equality. For a general interval, let $c=\inf f(I)$, $d=\sup f(I)$ (possibly $\mp\infty$). For $c<y<d$ there are $x_-,x_+\in I$ with $f(x_-)<y<f(x_+)$, since $y$ is neither a lower nor an upper bound of $f(I)$. The intermediate value theorem between $x_-$ and $x_+$ gives $y\in f(I)$. So $(c,d)\subseteq f(I)\subseteq[c,d]$, an interval.

Now the backwards reading must not jump. To keep the answer within $\varepsilon$ of $x_0$, post two guards on the road: one just left of $x_0$, one just right, both closer than $\varepsilon$.

(c) Let $y_0\in f(I)$, $\varepsilon>0$, $x_0=g(y_0)$, and suppose first that $x_0$ is not an endpoint of $I$. Choose $x_-,x_+\in I$ with $x_0-\varepsilon<x_-<x_0<x_+<x_0+\varepsilon$, and let $y_\pm=f(x_\pm)$. Then $y_-<y_0<y_+$.

The guards' heights $y_-$ and $y_+$ fence in $y_0$. Any height between the two fences belongs to a place between the two guards, so within $\varepsilon$ of $x_0$.

Let $\delta=\min(y_+-y_0,\ y_0-y_-)>0$. If $y\in f(I)$ and $|y-y_0|<\delta$, then $y_-<y<y_+$. Since $g$ is strictly increasing, $x_-<g(y)<x_+$, so $|g(y)-g(y_0)|<\varepsilon$.

At an end of the road there is only one side to guard.

If $x_0$ is the left endpoint, then $y_0=\min f(I)$ and there are no $y\in f(I)$ below $y_0$; take only $x_+$ and $\delta=y_+-y_0$. The right endpoint is symmetric. So $g$ is continuous. ∎

The proof in one piece (as you'd write it in the exam)

Without loss of generality $f$ is strictly increasing (else use $-f$). (a) $f:I\to f(I)$ is surjective by definition and injective by strict monotonicity, so $g=f^{-1}$ exists. Since $x_1<x_2\iff f(x_1)<f(x_2)$, we get $g(y_1)<g(y_2)\iff y_1<y_2$: $g$ is strictly increasing. (b) For $I=[a,b]$, monotonicity gives $f([a,b])\subseteq[f(a),f(b)]$ and the intermediate value theorem gives equality; for general $I$, every $y$ strictly between $\inf f(I)$ and $\sup f(I)$ lies between two values of $f$, hence in $f(I)$ by the intermediate value theorem, so $f(I)$ is an interval. (c) Let $y_0\in f(I)$, $\varepsilon>0$, $x_0=g(y_0)$. If $x_0$ is not an endpoint, choose $x_\pm\in I$ with $x_0-\varepsilon<x_-<x_0<x_+<x_0+\varepsilon$, set $y_\pm=f(x_\pm)$ and $\delta=\min(y_+-y_0,y_0-y_-)>0$. For $y\in f(I)$ with $|y-y_0|<\delta$ we have $y_-<y<y_+$, so $x_-<g(y)<x_+$ and $|g(y)-g(y_0)|<\varepsilon$. At an endpoint only one of $x_\pm$ is needed. ∎

3. Promise one: bounded

Recall (Definition 3.37, p. 149). $f:D\to\mathbb{R}$ is bounded if there is an $M$ with $|f(x)|\le M$ for all $x\in D$.

Satz 3.67, Beschränktheit (p. 165). Let $a<b$ and $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ continuous. Then $f$ is bounded. (The script allows $f$ to be complex-valued; the proof is the same with $|\cdot|$ on $\mathbb{C}$.)

Every hypothesis is needed (Wichtige Übung 3.68, p. 166). These three are the counterexamples the exam asks for:

Proof B: continuous on $[a,b]$ means bounded

The idea. A surveyor starts at the left wall and maps the land eastwards, keeping track of how far the map reaches with a finite height limit. Wherever the survey seems to stop, continuity lights up a small neighbourhood where the heights are under control, and the survey can be pushed past that point. So it can only stop at the east wall.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • The goal “$f$ is bounded on $[a,b]$” forces the last line: “…so $|f(x)|\le M$ for all $x\in[a,b]$.”
  • There's no formula for $M$, so we use the supremum move, which forces the first line: “Let $$X=\{\,t\in[a,b]: f\text{ is bounded on }[a,t]\,\}.$$” The goal becomes $b\in X$.
  • The gap to fill: show $X$ can't stop before $b$. That's where continuity comes in.
The storyWhat you write

The surveyor's map: every point $t$ such that the stretch from the wall $a$ to $t$ has some height limit.

Let $$X=\{\,t\in[a,b]: f\text{ is bounded on }[a,t]\,\}.$$

At the wall itself there is only one point, so the survey has begun. The map can't go past the east wall, so it has a furthest edge $s_0$ (lesson 6).

$a\in X$, since $|f|\le|f(a)|$ on $\{a\}$. So $X\neq\emptyset$, and $X$ is bounded above by $b$. By completeness, $s_0=\sup X$ exists, and $a\le s_0\le b$.

At $s_0$ the surveyor lights a lantern. Continuity says: within the lantern's radius $\delta$, the ground stays within $1$ of the height at $s_0$. So the lit stretch has a height limit.

By continuity of $f$ at $s_0$ with $\varepsilon=1$, there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)-f(s_0)|<1$ for $x\in[a,b]$, $|x-s_0|<\delta$. By the triangle inequality, $$|f(x)|\le|f(x)-f(s_0)|+|f(s_0)|<1+|f(s_0)|$$ for these $x$.

The light reaches back past $s_0-\delta$. And the map already reached past that point, because $s_0$ is its furthest edge. Say it reached $t$, with height limit $M_0$.

Since $s_0-\delta<s_0=\sup X$, the number $s_0-\delta$ is not an upper bound of $X$. So there is $t\in X$ with $t>s_0-\delta$, and some $M_0$ with $|f(x)|\le M_0$ on $[a,t]$.

Glue the old map (up to $t$) and the lit stretch together. They overlap, and together they reach $s_0+\frac\delta2$, or the east wall if that's closer. Both pieces have a height limit, so the whole has one: the bigger of the two.

Let $t_1=\min\left(b,\ s_0+\tfrac\delta2\right)$ and $x\in[a,t_1]$. If $x\le t$, then $|f(x)|\le M_0$. If $x>t$, then $s_0-\delta<x\le s_0+\frac\delta2$, so $|x-s_0|<\delta$ and $|f(x)|<1+|f(s_0)|$. Hence $|f(x)|\le\max\big(M_0,\,1+|f(s_0)|\big)$ on $[a,t_1]$, so $t_1\in X$.

But nothing in the map lies beyond its furthest edge $s_0$. Since $s_0+\frac\delta2$ is beyond it, the minimum must have been the east wall: the survey reached $b$.

As $s_0$ is an upper bound of $X$, $t_1\le s_0$. Since $s_0+\frac\delta2>s_0$, this forces $t_1=b$. So $b\in X$: $f$ is bounded on $[a,b]$. ∎

Moves used: the supremum trick continuity with a chosen ε

The proof in one piece (as you'd write it in the exam)

Let $X=\{t\in[a,b]: f\text{ is bounded on }[a,t]\}$. Then $a\in X$ and $X\subseteq[a,b]$, so $s_0=\sup X\in[a,b]$ exists by completeness. By continuity at $s_0$ (with $\varepsilon=1$) there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)|<1+|f(s_0)|$ for all $x\in[a,b]$ with $|x-s_0|<\delta$. As $s_0-\delta$ is not an upper bound of $X$, there is $t\in X$ with $t>s_0-\delta$ and $M_0$ with $|f|\le M_0$ on $[a,t]$. Let $t_1=\min(b,s_0+\frac\delta2)$. Every $x\in[a,t_1]$ satisfies $x\le t$ or $|x-s_0|<\delta$, so $|f(x)|\le\max(M_0,1+|f(s_0)|)$, and $t_1\in X$. Hence $t_1\le s_0<s_0+\frac\delta2$, so $t_1=b\in X$. ∎

    On $[a,a]=\{a\}$ the only value is $f(a)$, so $|f|\le|f(a)|$ there: $f$ is bounded on $[a,a]$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Why must $t_1$ be $b$? Give both reasons.

4. Promise two: maximum and minimum are attained (the highest tower)

Definition (p. 166). $f:D\to\mathbb{R}$ attains a maximum at $x_{\max}\in D$ if $f(x)\le f(x_{\max})$ for all $x\in D$, and a minimum at $x_{\min}\in D$ if $f(x)\ge f(x_{\min})$ for all $x\in D$. The value $f(x_{\max})$ is the maximum of $f$.

Korollar 3.69 (p. 166). Let $a<b$ and $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ continuous. Then $f$ attains both a maximum and a minimum.

Proof C: the maximum and minimum are attained

The idea. Being bounded already gives a lowest ceiling $S=\sup f([a,b])$. The question is whether some tower actually touches it. If none does, carry a bell that rings louder the closer you are to the ceiling. The bell is itself continuous on the walled garden, so promise one limits how loud it gets, and that means the towers stay a fixed distance below $S$. Then $S$ wasn't the lowest ceiling after all.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • The goal “a maximum is attained” means: there is $x_{\max}$ with $f(x)\le f(x_{\max})$ for all $x$. The only possible value is $S=\sup f([a,b])$, so the goal is “some $x$ has $f(x)=S$”.
  • An existence claim with no formula for $x$ suggests contradiction. First line: “Suppose $f(x)<S$ for all $x\in[a,b]$.” Last line: “…an upper bound smaller than $S$, contradicting that $S$ is the least upper bound. So some $x_{\max}$ has $f(x_{\max})=S$.”
  • The gap to fill: produce that smaller upper bound. That's the bell.
The storyWhat you write

Promise one: the towers have a height limit. So there is a lowest ceiling $S$ over the garden.

By the boundedness theorem $f$ is bounded, so $S=\sup f([a,b])$ exists by completeness.

Suppose no tower touches the ceiling.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that $f(x)<S$ for all $x\in[a,b]$.

Carry a bell whose loudness is $1$ divided by the gap between the tower under you and the ceiling. The closer to the ceiling, the louder. The gap is never $0$, so the bell always works.

Then $F(x)=\dfrac{1}{S-f(x)}$ is well-defined and positive on $[a,b]$, and continuous as a quotient of continuous functions whose denominator has no zero.

The bell's loudness is a continuous function on the walled garden too, so promise one applies to it: the loudness has a limit $M$.

By the boundedness theorem applied to $F$, there is $M>0$ with $\dfrac{1}{S-f(x)}\le M$ for all $x\in[a,b]$.

A loudness limit means no tower comes closer than $\frac1M$ to the ceiling. So the ceiling could be lowered by $\frac1M$, and $S$ wasn't the lowest ceiling.

Multiplying by $\frac{S-f(x)}{M}>0$ gives $\frac1M\le S-f(x)$, that is, $f(x)\le S-\frac1M$ for all $x$. So $S-\frac1M$ is an upper bound of $f([a,b])$ smaller than $S$, contradicting that $S$ is the least upper bound.

So some tower touches the ceiling. For the lowest point, turn the garden upside down.

Hence there is $x_{\max}\in[a,b]$ with $f(x_{\max})=S$, the maximum. Applying this to $-f$ gives the minimum. ∎

Moves used: a helper function contradict “least” reduce to one case (−f)

The proof in one piece (as you'd write it in the exam)

By the boundedness theorem, $S=\sup f([a,b])$ exists. Suppose $f(x)<S$ for all $x$. Then $F(x)=\frac1{S-f(x)}$ is well-defined and continuous on $[a,b]$, hence bounded by some $M>0$. So $f(x)\le S-\frac1M$ for all $x$, an upper bound smaller than $S$, contradicting $S=\sup f([a,b])$. Hence $f(x_{\max})=S$ for some $x_{\max}$. Applying this to $-f$ gives the minimum. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Boundedness ⇒ $S=\sup f([a,b])$ exists.
  2. Assume $f(x)<S$ for all $x\in[a,b]$.
  3. Helper: $F=\frac1{S-f}$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, so bounded by some $M>0$ (boundedness again).
  4. Contradiction: $f\le S-\frac1M$, an upper bound smaller than $S$.
  5. Minimum: apply the result to $-f$.

In short: “if the supremum is never attained, one over the gap is bounded, so the gap is not small”.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why is $F=\frac1{S-f}$ well-defined and continuous?

    Answer

    By the assumption, $S-f(x)>0$ for every $x$, so the denominator is never $0$; a quotient of continuous functions with non-zero denominator is continuous.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). From $\frac1{S-f(x)}\le M$, derive the smaller upper bound.

    Answer

    Multiply by the positive number $\frac{S-f(x)}M$: $\frac1M\le S-f(x)$, so $f(x)\le S-\frac1M$ for all $x$.

5. Promise three: uniform continuity (one rule for the whole garden)

Compare the two definitions with the quantifiers in front. Read them slowly; the only difference is where the point stands.

Definition
continuous on $D$
(Def. 3.45, p. 153)		$\forall\varepsilon>0\ \ \forall x_0\in D\ \ \exists\delta>0\ \ \forall x\in D:\ |x-x_0|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$
uniformly continuous on $D$
(Def. 3.71, p. 167)		$\forall\varepsilon>0\ \ \exists\delta>0\ \ \forall x,y\in D:\ |x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$

In the first line you choose $\delta$ after seeing the point $x_0$, so $\delta$ may depend on $\varepsilon$ and on $x_0$. In the second line you must choose $\delta$ before any point is named, so one $\delta$ has to work everywhere. In lesson 1 terms: “$\forall x_0\ \exists\delta$” became “$\exists\delta\ \forall x_0$”, and swapping $\forall$ and $\exists$ changes the meaning. Uniformly continuous implies continuous (take $y=x_0$), not the other way round.

Proof D: $x^2$ is not uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ (Übung 3.73, p. 167)

The idea. On the endless plain the parabola gets steeper and steeper. Whatever single step size you promise, far enough out half a step already climbs more than $1$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)

The goal is a negation, built with the lesson 1 rules:

$$\begin{aligned}&\exists\varepsilon>0\ \ \forall\delta>0\ \ \exists x,y\in D:\\ &|x-y|<\delta\ \text{ and }\ |f(x)-f(y)|\ge\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$
  • First lines: “Let $\varepsilon=1$. Let $\delta>0$ be arbitrary.” (We choose $\varepsilon$; $\delta$ is given to us.)
  • Last line: “Then $|x-y|<\delta$ but $|f(x)-f(y)|\ge1$.”
  • The gap to fill: choose $x$ and $y$, depending on $\delta$.
The storyWhat you write

The Doubter names a tolerance: the height may change by less than $1$.

Let $\varepsilon=1$.

You claim one step size works everywhere. The Doubter accepts whatever step size $\delta$ you offer.

Let $\delta>0$ be arbitrary.

The Doubter walks far east, to where the ground is very steep, and takes half of your step.

Let $x=\frac1\delta$ and $y=x+\frac\delta2$. Then $|x-y|=\frac\delta2<\delta$.

Half a step on such steep ground climbs more than the tolerance.

$$y^2-x^2=x\delta+\tfrac{\delta^2}{4}>x\delta=1.$$

So no $\delta$ works for $\varepsilon=1$. ∎

Moves used: the negation recipe

The proof in one piece

6. Heine's theorem and its proof (worth 7 exam points)

Satz 3.75, Heine (p. 167). Let $a<b$ and $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ continuous. Then $f$ is uniformly continuous.

Proof E: Heine's theorem

This is exactly Figalli FS26 B1. The idea is the surveyor from promise one again, now carrying a fixed stride. For a given tolerance $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$, the surveyor maps how far a single stride length $\class{k-del}{\delta}$ keeps any two nearby heights within $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$ of each other. Wherever that map seems to stop, a lantern (continuity) gives a stride that works near that point, and gluing the two strides together pushes the map further. So it reaches the east wall.

You'll need these
  • Supremum, two properties (lesson 6; Satz 2.59, p. 105–106, says a non-empty set that is bounded above has one). $s=\sup \class{k-X}{X}$ means (1) upper bound: $t\le s$ for all $t\in \class{k-X}{X}$, and (2) least: no number below $s$ is an upper bound, so for every $r<s$ some $t\in \class{k-X}{X}$ has $t>r$.
  • Continuity at $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$ (Definition 3.45, p. 153): for every $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}>0$ there is $\class{k-del}{\delta}>0$ with $$|x-\class{k-x0}{s_0}|<\class{k-del}{\delta}\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(\class{k-x0}{s_0})|<\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}.$$
  • Triangle inequality (lesson 5): $|u-w|\le|u-v|+|v-w|$. Two points that are each close to a third are close to each other.
  • Uniform continuity on $[a,t]$: one $\class{k-del}{\delta}>0$ such that all $x_1,x_2\in[a,t]$ with $|x_1-x_2|<\class{k-del}{\delta}$ have $|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$.

Colour code$\class{k-x0}{s_0}$ gold, $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$ blue, every $\class{k-del}{\delta}$ green, $\class{k-X}{X}$ teal.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • The goal “$f$ is uniformly continuous” forces the first line “Let $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}>0$.” and the last lines “…so there is $\class{k-del}{\delta}>0$ with $|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$ for all $x_1,x_2\in[a,b]$ with $|x_1-x_2|<\class{k-del}{\delta}$. As $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$ was arbitrary, $f$ is uniformly continuous.”
  • There's no formula for $\class{k-del}{\delta}$, so we use the supremum move: “Let $\class{k-X}{X}$ be the set of $t$ for which one $\class{k-del}{\delta}$ works on $[a,t]$.” The goal becomes $b\in \class{k-X}{X}$.
  • The gap to fill: show $\class{k-X}{X}$ can't stop before $b$, exactly as in the boundedness proof, with one extra step (gluing two strides).
The storyWhat you write

The Doubter fixes a tolerance $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$. The surveyor's map: every point $t$ such that one single stride length works on the whole stretch from $a$ to $t$.

Let $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}>0$ and let $\class{k-X}{X}$ be the set of $t\in[a,b]$ for which there is $\class{k-del}{\delta}>0$ with $|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$ for all $x_1,x_2\in[a,t]$ with $|x_1-x_2|<\class{k-del}{\delta}$.

At the wall any stride works. A stride that works on a long stretch also works on a shorter one, so the map has no holes: it is everything from $a$ up to its furthest edge $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$, maybe without $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$ itself.

$a\in \class{k-X}{X}$ (on $\{a\}$ every $\class{k-del}{\delta}$ works), and $\class{k-X}{X}\subseteq[a,b]$, so $\class{k-x0}{s_0}=\sup \class{k-X}{X}\in[a,b]$ exists by completeness. If $t\in \class{k-X}{X}$ and $a\le t'\le t$, then $t'\in \class{k-X}{X}$ with the same $\class{k-del}{\delta}$. Hence $[a,\class{k-x0}{s_0})\subseteq \class{k-X}{X}\subseteq[a,\class{k-x0}{s_0}]$.

At $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$ the surveyor lights a lantern, asking for half the tolerance. Inside its radius $\class{k-del}{\delta}_1$ every height is within $\frac{\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}}{2}$ of the height at $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$, so any two of them are within $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$ of each other.

By continuity at $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$ there is $\class{k-del}{\delta}_1>0$ with $|f(x)-f(\class{k-x0}{s_0})|<\frac{\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}}{2}$ for $x\in[a,b]$, $|x-\class{k-x0}{s_0}|<\class{k-del}{\delta}_1$. For two such points $x_1,x_2$, the triangle inequality gives $$|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\frac{\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}}{2}+\frac{\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}}{2}=\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}.$$

Step back half a lantern radius, to $t_0$. That point is inside the map, so the old map gives a stride $\class{k-del}{\delta}_0$ that works up to $t_0$.

Let $$t_0=\max\left(a,\ \class{k-x0}{s_0}-\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2\right).$$ Then $t_0\in \class{k-X}{X}$, so there is $\class{k-del}{\delta}_0>0$ that works on $[a,t_0]$.

Take the shorter of the two strides and walk up to $t_1$, half a radius past $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$. Two points one stride apart are either near the lantern, which handles them, or both left of $t_0$, where the old stride handles them. The stride is short enough that no pair can straddle both.

Let $$t_1=\min\left(b,\ \class{k-x0}{s_0}+\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2\right),$$ $$\class{k-del}{\delta}=\min\left(\class{k-del}{\delta}_0,\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2\right),$$ and $x_1,x_2\in[a,t_1]$ with $|x_1-x_2|<\class{k-del}{\delta}$. Case A, one of them (oBdA $x_1$) has $|x_1-\class{k-x0}{s_0}|\le\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2$: then $$|x_2-\class{k-x0}{s_0}|<\class{k-del}{\delta}+\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2\le\class{k-del}{\delta}_1,$$ so both are within $\class{k-del}{\delta}_1$ of $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$ and $|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$. Case B, both have $|x_j-\class{k-x0}{s_0}|>\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2$: as $x_j\le \class{k-x0}{s_0}+\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2$, both are $<\class{k-x0}{s_0}-\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2\le t_0$, so $\class{k-del}{\delta}\le\class{k-del}{\delta}_0$ gives $|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$. Hence $t_1\in \class{k-X}{X}$.

But nothing in the map lies beyond its furthest edge. So $t_1$ must be the east wall, and the wall is in the map: one stride works for the whole garden.

So $t_1\le \class{k-x0}{s_0}<\class{k-x0}{s_0}+\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2$, which forces $t_1=b$. Then $b\in \class{k-X}{X}$: some $\class{k-del}{\delta}>0$ works on all of $[a,b]$. As $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$ was arbitrary, $f$ is uniformly continuous. ∎

Moves used: the supremum trick continuity with a chosen ε ε/2 twice minimum of two δs

The proof in one piece (as you'd write it in the exam)

Proof. Let $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}>0$ and

$$\begin{aligned}&\class{k-X}{X}=\big\{\,t\in[a,b]\ :\ \exists\class{k-del}{\delta}>0\\ &\qquad\forall x_1,x_2\in[a,t]:\\ &\qquad|x_1-x_2|<\class{k-del}{\delta}\Rightarrow|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}\,\big\}.\end{aligned}$$

$a\in \class{k-X}{X}$, since for $x_1,x_2\in[a,a]$ we have $|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|=0$. So $\class{k-X}{X}\subseteq[a,b]$ is non-empty and bounded, and $\class{k-x0}{s_0}=\sup \class{k-X}{X}\in[a,b]$ exists by completeness. If $t\in \class{k-X}{X}$ and $a\le t'\le t$, the $\class{k-del}{\delta}$ for $t$ also works for $t'$, so $t'\in \class{k-X}{X}$. Since every $t'<\class{k-x0}{s_0}$ lies below some element of $\class{k-X}{X}$, we get

$$[a,\class{k-x0}{s_0})\subseteq \class{k-X}{X}\subseteq[a,\class{k-x0}{s_0}].\qquad(1)$$

By continuity of $f$ at $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$ there is $\class{k-del}{\delta}_1>0$ with $|f(x)-f(\class{k-x0}{s_0})|<\frac{\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}}{2}$ for all $x\in[a,b]$ with $|x-\class{k-x0}{s_0}|<\class{k-del}{\delta}_1$. For $$x_1,x_2\in[a,b]\cap(\class{k-x0}{s_0}-\class{k-del}{\delta}_1,\class{k-x0}{s_0}+\class{k-del}{\delta}_1)$$ the triangle inequality gives

$$\begin{aligned}|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|&\le|f(x_1)-f(\class{k-x0}{s_0})|\\ &\quad+|f(\class{k-x0}{s_0})-f(x_2)|\\ &<\tfrac{\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}}{2}+\tfrac{\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}}{2}=\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}.\qquad(2)\end{aligned}$$

By (1), $$t_0=\max\left(a,\ \class{k-x0}{s_0}-\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2\right)\in \class{k-X}{X},$$ so there is $\class{k-del}{\delta}_0>0$ with

$$\begin{aligned}&\forall x_1,x_2\in[a,t_0]:\\ &|x_1-x_2|<\class{k-del}{\delta}_0\Rightarrow|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}.\qquad(3)\end{aligned}$$

Let $$t_1=\min\left(b,\ \class{k-x0}{s_0}+\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2\right)$$ and $$\class{k-del}{\delta}=\min\left(\class{k-del}{\delta}_0,\ \frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2\right).$$ We claim that $|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$ for all $x_1,x_2\in[a,t_1]$ with $|x_1-x_2|<\class{k-del}{\delta}$.

  • Case A: $|x_1-\class{k-x0}{s_0}|\le\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2$ or $|x_2-\class{k-x0}{s_0}|\le\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2$; oBdA the first. Then $|x_2-\class{k-x0}{s_0}|\le|x_2-x_1|+|x_1-\class{k-x0}{s_0}|$, which is $<\class{k-del}{\delta}+\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2\le\class{k-del}{\delta}_1$. Both points lie within $\class{k-del}{\delta}_1$ of $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$, and (2) gives the claim.
  • Case B: $|x_1-\class{k-x0}{s_0}|>\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2$ and $|x_2-\class{k-x0}{s_0}|>\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2$. Since $x_j\le t_1\le \class{k-x0}{s_0}+\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2$, we get $x_j<\class{k-x0}{s_0}-\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2\le t_0$ for $j=1,2$. So $x_1,x_2\in[a,t_0]$ and $|x_1-x_2|<\class{k-del}{\delta}\le\class{k-del}{\delta}_0$, and (3) gives the claim.

So $t_1\in \class{k-X}{X}$, hence $t_1\le \class{k-x0}{s_0}$. Since $\class{k-x0}{s_0}+\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2>\class{k-x0}{s_0}$, this forces $t_1=b$, and then $b\le \class{k-x0}{s_0}\le b$. So $b=t_1\in \class{k-X}{X}$, which says exactly: there is $\class{k-del}{\delta}>0$ with $|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|<\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$ for all $x_1,x_2\in[a,b]$ with $|x_1-x_2|<\class{k-del}{\delta}$. As $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}>0$ was arbitrary, $f$ is uniformly continuous. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Fix $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$, define $\class{k-X}{X}$ = endpoints $t$ with a uniform $\class{k-del}{\delta}$ on $[a,t]$.
  2. $a\in \class{k-X}{X}$, bounded ⇒ $\class{k-x0}{s_0}=\sup \class{k-X}{X}$; and $[a,\class{k-x0}{s_0})\subseteq \class{k-X}{X}$ because shrinking keeps a $\class{k-del}{\delta}$ valid.
  3. Window: continuity at $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$ with $\frac{\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}}{2}$ gives $\class{k-del}{\delta}_1$; any two points in the window differ by less than $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$.
  4. Left piece: $t_0=\class{k-x0}{s_0}-\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2\in \class{k-X}{X}$ gives $\class{k-del}{\delta}_0$.
  5. Glue: $t_1=\min(b,\class{k-x0}{s_0}+\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2)$, $\class{k-del}{\delta}=\min(\class{k-del}{\delta}_0,\frac{\class{k-del}{\delta}_1}2)$, cases “near $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$” and “far from $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$”. So $t_1\in \class{k-X}{X}$.
  6. Hence $t_1\le \class{k-x0}{s_0}$ forces $\class{k-x0}{s_0}=b\in \class{k-X}{X}$.

Compared with the IVT proof: same skeleton (set, supremum, continuity at the supremum, contradiction), but here the contradiction is “we can reach past $\class{k-x0}{s_0}$” in every case, so there's only one case to run, plus the gluing.

Worked example 1: compute a maximum (Felder HS22, 1.A3)

Compute $\max\{3x^3-x\ :\ x\in[-1,1]\}$.

  1. Before computing anything: why must a maximum exist?

    Answer

    $f(x)=3x^3-x$ is a polynomial, so continuous, and $[-1,1]$ is a compact interval. By the extreme value theorem, $f$ attains a maximum. (Write this sentence in the exam; it's often a point.)

  2. Evaluate $f$ at $-1$, $0$ and $1$. Which value is the best candidate for the maximum?

    Answer

    $f(-1)=-3+1=-2$, $f(0)=0$, $f(1)=3-1=2$. Candidate: $2$, at $x=1$.

  3. What inequality do you have to prove to confirm the candidate?

    Answer

    $3x^3-x\le2$ for all $x\in[-1,1]$, that is, $3x^3-x-2\le0$.

  4. $x=1$ makes $3x^3-x-2$ equal to $0$. What does that let you do?

    Answer

    Factor out $(x-1)$:

    $$3x^3-x-2=(x-1)(3x^2+3x+2).$$

    Check by expanding: $(x-1)(3x^2+3x+2)$ is $3x^3+3x^2+2x-3x^2-3x-2$, which is $3x^3-x-2$.

  5. What sign does each factor have for $x\in[-1,1]$?

    Answer

    $x-1\le0$ because $x\le1$. And $3x^2+3x+2=3\left(x+\tfrac12\right)^2+\tfrac54$, which is $>0$ always (completing the square). So the product is $\le0$.

  6. Write the conclusion in one line.

    Answer

    $3x^3-x\le2$ on $[-1,1]$, with equality at $x=1$, so $\max\{3x^3-x : x\in[-1,1]\}=2$, attained at $x=1$.

Worked example 2: multiple choice (Einsiedler HS21, #10 (a) and (b))

Let $I\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be a bounded interval and $f:I\to\mathbb{R}$. True or false? (a) If $f$ is continuous, then $f$ is bounded. (b) If $f$ is continuous and injective, then $f$ is monotone.

  1. For (a): which theorem looks relevant, and what does it assume that this statement doesn't?

    Answer

    The boundedness theorem: continuous on $[a,b]$ implies bounded. It needs the interval to be closed and bounded. Here $I$ is only bounded, so it could be open at an end.

  2. So look for a counterexample: a continuous, unbounded function on a bounded interval that isn't closed.

    Answer

    $f(x)=\frac1x$ on $I=(0,1]$: continuous (quotient, denominator never $0$ there), and $f\!\left(\frac1n\right)=n$ for every $n$, so unbounded. (a) is false.

  3. For (b): what would a continuous function on an interval have to do in order to be not monotone?

    Answer

    Turn around: go up and then down (or down and then up).

  4. Why does turning around contradict injectivity?

    Answer

    If it goes up and then down, a value slightly below the turning point is taken once on the way up and once on the way down, by the intermediate value theorem on each side. Two different points with the same value contradict injectivity. (b) is true. This is Übung 3.61 (p. 160), proved in full in lesson 7's Bonus (b).

Parts (c) and (d) ask about antiderivatives (Stammfunktionen), which come in lesson 9 and later.

Why it's cool. On a closed, bounded interval, continuity alone gives three promises for free: bounded, maximum and minimum attained, uniformly continuous. Drop any one hypothesis (an open interval, an unbounded interval, a jump) and a counterexample breaks the promise, which is exactly what the exam's true/false questions test.

Solution

Answer B.

  • (A) False. $f(x)=x$ fits the setting ($f(0)=0$, values in $[0,\infty)$) and is uniformly continuous: for $\varepsilon>0$ take $\delta=\varepsilon$, then $|x-y|<\delta$ gives $|f(x)-f(y)|=|x-y|<\varepsilon$. But $f$ is unbounded. Uniform continuity limits how fast $f$ changes, not how far it gets.
  • (B) True. Let $c\in[0,\infty)$. Since $f\ge0$ and $f$ is unbounded, $f$ is not bounded by $c$, so there is $x_1$ with $f(x_1)>c$. Then $f(0)=0\le c<f(x_1)$, so $c$ lies between $f(0)$ and $f(x_1)$. The IVT on $[0,x_1]$ gives $x$ with $f(x)=c$. So every $c\ge0$ is a value: $f$ is surjective onto $[0,\infty)$.
  • (C) False. $f=0$ (constant) fits the setting and is monotone (increasing in the non-strict sense: $x\le y\Rightarrow f(x)\le f(y)$), but $f(1)=f(2)$. Only strict monotonicity gives injectivity.
  • (D) False (for later). $f(x)=\min(x,1)$ is Lipschitz with $L=1$ but has a kink at $x=1$, where it isn't differentiable.

Exercise 3 (Felder HS22, MC5 (B) and (D))

Open the original: HS22 exam

Let $a<b$ and $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$. True or false? (B) If $f$ is monotonically increasing, then $f$ is bounded. (D) If $f$ attains a maximum and a minimum, then $f$ is continuous.

Solution
  • (B) True. For $x\in[a,b]$ we have $a\le x\le b$, so monotonicity gives $f(a)\le f(x)\le f(b)$. Hence $|f(x)|\le\max\big(|f(a)|,|f(b)|\big)$ for all $x$. Notice we needed no continuity at all, only the two endpoints, which $[a,b]$ contains.
  • (D) False. Let $m=\frac{a+b}2$ and $f(x)=0$ for $x<m$, $f(x)=1$ for $x\ge m$. It attains its maximum $1$ (at $b$) and its minimum $0$ (at $a$), but it jumps at $m$. The extreme value theorem says “continuous on $[a,b]$ ⇒ max and min attained”; the converse is false.

For the record, the exam's (A) “continuous ⇒ differentiable” and (C) “Riemann integrable ⇒ continuous” are also false, so the answer is (B).

Exercise 4 (the twin of Worked example 1)

Compute $\min\{3x^3-x\ :\ x\in[-1,1]\}$, with the same three parts: existence, candidate, proof.

Solution

Frame: existence (extreme value theorem), a candidate (the values at a few points), then the inequality $f(x)\ge$ candidate for all $x$, proved by factoring.

  • Existence: $f(x)=3x^3-x$ is continuous on the compact interval $[-1,1]$, so the minimum exists by the extreme value theorem.
  • Candidate: $f(-1)=-3+1=-2$, which is the smallest of the values $-2,0,2$ at $-1,0,1$.
  • Proof: we need $3x^3-x\ge-2$, that is, $3x^3-x+2\ge0$. Since $x=-1$ is a root, $$3x^3-x+2=(x+1)(3x^2-3x+2).$$(Check: $$\begin{aligned}3x^3-3x^2+2x+3x^2-3x+2&=3x^3-x+2\end{aligned}.$$) For $x\ge-1$ the first factor is $\ge0$, and $$3x^2-3x+2=3\left(x-\tfrac12\right)^2+\tfrac54>0.$$So the product is $\ge0$.
  • Answer: $\min=-2$, attained at $x=-1$.

Exercise 5 (proof, FS26 MC3 (D) made precise)

Open the original: FS26 exam

Show that $f(x)=\frac1x$ is not uniformly continuous on $(0,1)$. Write down the negation first, then do $\delta=0.1$ with numbers, then the general proof.

Solution

Negation: $\exists\varepsilon>0\ \forall\delta>0\ \exists x,y\in(0,1)$ with $|x-y|<\delta$ and $\left|\frac1x-\frac1y\right|\ge\varepsilon$.

Numbers, $\varepsilon=1$, $\delta=0.1$: go close to $0$, where $\frac1x$ is steep: $x=0.1$, $y=0.05$. Then $|x-y|=0.05<0.1$, but $\frac1y-\frac1x=20-10=10\ge1$.

Frame: first lines “Let $\varepsilon=1$. Let $\delta>0$ be arbitrary.”, last line “Then $|x-y|<\delta$ but $\left|\frac1x-\frac1y\right|\ge1$.” The gap: choose $x,y$ depending on $\delta$.

Proof. Let $\varepsilon=1$ and $\delta>0$. Put $h=\min\left(\delta,\tfrac12\right)$, $x=h$ and $y=\frac h2$. Both lie in $(0,1)$, and $|x-y|=\frac h2<\delta$. But

$$\frac1y-\frac1x=\frac2h-\frac1h=\frac1h\ge2\ge1=\varepsilon.$$

So no $\delta$ works for $\varepsilon=1$. ∎ (We took $h\le\frac12$ only to make sure $x$ stays inside $(0,1)$ when someone offers a big $\delta$.)

Exercise 6 (inverse function theorem)

Let $f(x)=x^3+x$ on $[0,2]$. (a) Show $f$ is strictly increasing. (b) What is $f([0,2])$, and what does the inverse function theorem say about $f^{-1}$? (c) Compute $f^{-1}(2)$ and $f^{-1}(10)$.

Solution

(a) Frame: first line “Let $x<y$ in $[0,2]$”, last line “so $f(x)<f(y)$”. The gap: show $y^3-x^3>0$ by factoring.

Let $x<y$. Then $y^3-x^3=(y-x)(y^2+xy+x^2)$, and $$y^2+xy+x^2=\left(y+\tfrac x2\right)^2+\tfrac34x^2>0$$(it could only be $0$ if $x=0$ and $y=0$, but $x\neq y$). So $x^3<y^3$. Adding $x<y$ gives $x^3+x<y^3+y$: $f$ is strictly increasing.

(b) $f$ is a polynomial, so continuous, and strictly increasing on $I=[0,2]$. By the inverse function theorem $f([0,2])=[f(0),f(2)]=[0,10]$, and $f^{-1}:[0,10]\to[0,2]$ exists and is continuous and strictly increasing.

(c) $f(1)=1+1=2$, so $f^{-1}(2)=1$. $f(2)=8+2=10$, so $f^{-1}(10)=2$. (We can't write $f^{-1}$ as a closed formula, but the theorem tells us it exists and is continuous anyway.)

Bonus

(a) (Übung 3.78 a, p. 169) Show that a Lipschitz continuous function is uniformly continuous. (b) Let $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous. Show that $f([a,b])$ is a closed bounded interval, namely $[\min f,\ \max f]$. (Combine lessons 7 and 8.)

Solution

Frame for (a): first line “Let $\varepsilon>0$.”, then one $\delta$ chosen before any points, last line “so $|x-y|<\delta$ implies $|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x,y$”. Frame for (b): a set equality $A=B$ is always proved as two inclusions, $A\subseteq B$ and $B\subseteq A$. Moves: set equality by two inclusions.

(a) Let $|f(x)-f(y)|\le L|x-y|$ for all $x,y\in D$, and $\varepsilon>0$. If $L=0$, then $f$ is constant and any $\delta$ works. If $L>0$, let $\delta=\frac\varepsilon L$. Then $|x-y|<\delta$ gives $$|f(x)-f(y)|\le L|x-y|<L\cdot\frac\varepsilon L=\varepsilon.$$The $\delta$ does not depend on the points, so $f$ is uniformly continuous. ∎

(b) By the extreme value theorem there are $p,q\in[a,b]$ with $f(p)=m=\min f$ and $f(q)=M=\max f$. By definition $m\le f(x)\le M$ for all $x$, so $f([a,b])\subseteq[m,M]$. Conversely, let $c\in[m,M]$. Then $c$ lies between $f(p)$ and $f(q)$, and the IVT on the interval between $p$ and $q$ (which lies inside $[a,b]$) gives $x$ with $f(x)=c$. So $f([a,b])=[m,M]$. ∎ In words: a continuous image of a compact interval is a compact interval. This one sentence holds lessons 7 and 8 together.

9. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Why your screen can draw a graph. A plotting program evaluates $f$ at finitely many points, one per pixel, and joins them up. That's only honest if values at nearby points are close, with one tolerance for the whole picture. That is uniform continuity, and Heine's theorem guarantees it for every continuous function on a closed interval. Engineers rely on the same thing whenever a machine with precision $\delta$ is promised to deliver an output within $\varepsilon$, at every setting.

A whole family of irrational numbers (Lemma 3.65, p. 164). For $m,k\in\mathbb{N}$, $\sqrt[m]{k}$ is rational only if it is an integer. Proof: suppose $\sqrt[m]{k}=\frac pq$ in lowest terms. Then $k=\frac{p^m}{q^m}$, and $p^m,q^m$ still share no prime factor (any prime dividing $p^m$ divides $p$, and likewise for $q$). A fraction in lowest terms is an integer only if its denominator is $1$, so $q^m=1$, $q=1$, and $\sqrt[m]{k}=p\in\mathbb{Z}$. ∎ So $\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt5,\sqrt[3]2,\sqrt[3]{9},\dots$ are all irrational in one stroke, because no integer squared is $2$, no integer cubed is $9$, and so on.

The word “compact”. We used it for $[a,b]$, but it's much bigger. In chapter 5 (lesson 10 onwards) compact sets are defined for any metric space, and all three promises of this lesson hold for continuous functions on any compact set. Heine published the uniform continuity theorem in 1872. Dirichlet had already used the idea in his lectures in the 1850s, and Borel's covering theorem of 1895 later turned “compact” into one of the central ideas of modern mathematics.
