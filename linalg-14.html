1. The story: surveyors of surveyors

In lesson 10 the Free City hired surveyors: each one walks to a house and reads off a number, fairly (doubling the house doubles the reading, two houses together give the sum of readings). Now the council wants to check the surveyors. It hires inspectors, and an inspector is again fair: she takes a surveyor and returns a number.

One kind of inspector needs no instructions at all. Pick a house; the inspector “of that house” asks every surveyor what he reads at that house. No grid, no choice of roads: the house alone defines her. Every house has its inspector, and different houses have different inspectors. In a city with finitely many roads, these are all the inspectors there are. This is why the council calls the house-inspectors canonical. By contrast, pairing each road of a grid with “its” surveyor (lesson 10's dual basis) depends on the whole grid: change one road and all the pairings move.

Couriers (lesson 11) cooperate with surveyors too. A courier carries houses from the Free City to the sister city. A surveyor working in the sister city becomes a surveyor for the Free City: “send the house by courier, then read”. So couriers going forward turn surveyors backward. And some surveyors are useless for a district: they read zero on every house of it. The council keeps a register of them, the district's annihilator, and finds that the larger the district, the shorter the register.

In mathematics: the bidual $V^{**}$ (inspectors), the evaluation map $\tau:V\to V^{**}$ (house-inspectors), the dual map $T^*:W^*\to V^*$, $\psi\mapsto\psi\circ T$ (surveyors pulled back along a courier), and the annihilator $U^0$ of a subspace $U$ (surveyors that vanish on $U$), with $\dim U+\dim U^0=\dim V$.

2. Canonical and non-canonical

For a finite-dimensional $V$ we know $V\cong V^*$, since both have dimension $n$ (lesson 12, Proof D). The script makes this explicit (Proposition 10.3.1): for a basis $B=(v_i)$ the linear map $\Phi_B:V\to V^*$ with $\Phi_B(v_i)=v_i^*$ is injective, and an isomorphism if $\dim V<\infty$ (it maps a basis to a basis). But $\Phi_B$ depends on $B$, and not only through the labels. A construction is called canonical (or natural) if it needs no choice, or if the result does not depend on the choices made (script Section 10.3, p. 321).

Worked example 1: $\Phi_B$ depends on the basis

(a) Let $V=\mathbb R$. Compute $\Phi_B(1)$ for $B=(1)$ and for $B=(2)$. (b) Let $V=\mathbb R^2$, $B=E$ and $B'=(e_1,\,e_1+e_2)$. Compute $\Phi_E(e_1)$ and $\Phi_{B'}(e_1)$.

  1. (a) What is the dual basis of $(1)$, and of $(2)$?
    Answer

    For $B=(1)$: $v_1^*(x)=x$, since $v_1^*(1)=1$. For $B=(2)$: $v_1^*(x)=\frac x2$, since $v_1^*(2)=1$.

  2. (a) Compute $\Phi_B(1)$ in both cases.
    Answer

    $B=(1)$: $\Phi_B(1)=v_1^*$, the functional $x\mapsto x$. $B=(2)$: $1=\frac12\cdot2$, so $\Phi_B(1)=\frac12v_1^*$, the functional $x\mapsto\frac x4$. Two different functionals: $\Phi_B$ depends on $B$, even in dimension one. Scaling the basis by $c$ scales $\Phi_B$ by $1/c^2$.

  3. (b) Compute both.
    Answer

    $\Phi_E(e_1)=e_1^*$, i.e. $(x,y)\mapsto x$. For $B'=(b_1,b_2)=(e_1,e_1+e_2)$: the dual basis is $b_1^*(x,y)=x-y$, $b_2^*(x,y)=y$ (check: $b_1^*(1,0)=1$, $b_1^*(1,1)=0$, $b_2^*(1,0)=0$, $b_2^*(1,1)=1$). Since $e_1=b_1$, $\Phi_{B'}(e_1)=b_1^*$, i.e. $(x,y)\mapsto x-y$. The vector $e_1$ is in both bases, but its partner changed because the other basis vector changed.

So there are many isomorphisms $V\to V^*$ and none is better than the others. For $V^{**}$ the situation is different.

3. The bidual and the evaluation map

Definition (bidual; script Definition 10.3.2, p. 321). $V^{**}:=(V^*)^*$ is the bidual space of $V$: the linear maps $V^*\to K$. The evaluation map is

$$\begin{aligned}&\tau:V\to V^{**},\\ &\tau(v)(\ell):=\ell(v)\quad(\ell\in V^*).\end{aligned}$$

In words: $\tau(v)$ takes a functional and evaluates it at $v$. It uses no basis.

Before proving anything, check that $\tau(v)$ really lies in $V^{**}$, i.e. that it is linear in $\ell$: $\tau(v)(\ell_1+\alpha\ell_2)$ $=(\ell_1+\alpha\ell_2)(v)$ $=\ell_1(v)+\alpha\ell_2(v)$ by the definition of the vector space operations on $V^*$ (lesson 10). Note the two different kinds of linearity: $\tau(v)$ is linear in $\ell$, and the claim below is that $\tau$ is linear in $v$.

Proof A: the evaluation map is a canonical isomorphism (script Proposition 10.3.3, pp. 321–322)

Claim. $\tau:V\to V^{**}$ is linear and injective. If $\dim V<\infty$, it is an isomorphism.

The idea. Linearity: two elements of $V^{**}$ are equal if they agree on every $\ell$, and on each $\ell$ the claim is the linearity of $\ell$. Injectivity: check the kernel (lesson 11). If $\tau(v)=0$, then every functional vanishes at $v$; but if $v\ne0$, some functional does not (extend $v$ to a basis and take the dual basis vector). The script does this for finite dimension with the dual basis. Then a dimension count finishes: $\dim V^{**}=\dim V^*=\dim V$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Linear, first line: “Let $v,v'\in V$, $\alpha\in K$, and let $\ell\in V^*$ be arbitrary.” Last line: “So $\tau(\alpha v+v')=\alpha\tau(v)+\tau(v')$.”
  • Injective, first line: “Let $v\in\mathrm{Ker}(\tau)$, i.e. $\ell(v)=0$ for all $\ell\in V^*$.” Last line: “Hence $v=0$.”
  • Iso: “$\dim V^{**}=\dim V$, and an injective linear map between spaces of equal finite dimension is bijective.”
  • The gap: a functional that sees $v\ne0$.
The storyWhat you write

The inspector of a combined house hears the combined readings, because every surveyor is fair.

Linear. Let $v,v'\in V$, $\alpha\in K$. For every $\ell\in V^*$: $\tau(\alpha v+v')(\ell)=\ell(\alpha v+v')=\alpha\ell(v)+\ell(v')$ $=\alpha\tau(v)(\ell)+\tau(v')(\ell)=\big(\alpha\tau(v)+\tau(v')\big)(\ell)$. As $\ell$ was arbitrary, $\tau(\alpha v+v')=\alpha\tau(v)+\tau(v')$.

If every surveyor reads zero at a house, the house is the fountain: a surveyor hired along a grid through that house would read one.

Injective. Let $v\in\mathrm{Ker}(\tau)$: $\ell(v)=0$ for all $\ell\in V^*$. Suppose $v\ne0$. Extend $(v)$ to a basis $B=(v,v_2,\dots)$ of $V$ (lesson 7; in infinite dimension this needs the basis theorem of lesson 7's “What we skipped”). Its dual vector $v^*$ (the functional with $v^*(v)=1$ and $v^*(v_i)=0$, which exists by lesson 10) satisfies $v^*(v)=1\ne0$, a contradiction. So $v=0$, and $\tau$ is injective (lesson 11, Proof G).

In a city with finitely many roads, counting shows there are no other inspectors.

Iso if $\dim V=n<\infty$. By lesson 10, $\dim V^*=n$, hence $\dim V^{**}=\dim(V^*)^*=n$. $\tau$ maps a basis of $V$ to $n$ linearly independent vectors of $V^{**}$ (if $\sum_i a_i\tau(v_i)=0$, then $\tau(\sum_ia_iv_i)=0$, so $\sum_ia_iv_i=0$ by injectivity and all $a_i=0$), which form a basis of the $n$-dimensional $V^{**}$ (lesson 7). A linear map sending a basis to a basis is an isomorphism (lesson 12, Proof D). ∎

Moves used: two maps are equal if they agree at every point injective: check the kernel choose a basis adapted to a subspace count first: $n$ vectors in an $n$-dimensional space

The proof in one piece

For $v,v'\in V$, $\alpha\in K$ and all $\ell\in V^*$, $\tau(\alpha v+v')(\ell)=\ell(\alpha v+v')=\alpha\ell(v)+\ell(v')=(\alpha\tau(v)+\tau(v'))(\ell)$, so $\tau$ is linear. If $\tau(v)=0$ and $v\ne0$, extend $v$ to a basis and let $v^*$ be the dual vector of $v$; then $0=\tau(v)(v^*)=v^*(v)=1$, a contradiction. So $\mathrm{Ker}(\tau)=\{0\}$ and $\tau$ is injective. If $\dim V=n$, then $\dim V^{**}=\dim V^*=n$, so the injective $\tau$ maps a basis to $n$ independent vectors, a basis of $V^{**}$, and is an isomorphism. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Linearity: evaluate both sides at an arbitrary $\ell$.
  2. Injectivity: kernel; a nonzero $v$ is seen by its own dual basis vector after extending $v$ to a basis.
  3. Surjectivity only in finite dimension, by counting: $\dim V^{**}=\dim V$.

In short: $\tau(v)=$ “evaluate at $v$”; linear by linearity of each $\ell$, injective because some functional sees every $v\ne0$, onto by counting.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. $V=\mathbb R^2$, $v=(1,2)$. Write $\tau(v)$ as a map on $V^*$, using $\ell=ae_1^*+be_2^*$.
    Answer

    $\tau(v)(\ell)=\ell(1,2)=a+2b$. So $\tau(v)$ is the functional on $V^*$ that sends the row $(a,b)$ to $a+2b$: “multiply the row by the column $(1,2)$”. With $V^*$ written as rows (lesson 10), $V^{**}$ is columns again, and $\tau$ is the identity on columns.

  2. Rung 2. Why does the injectivity part not need $\dim V<\infty$, while the last part does?
    Answer

    Extending to a basis and the dual vector $v^*$ exist in every vector space (with the basis theorem for infinite dimension). Counting dimensions needs finite numbers. In fact for infinite-dimensional $V$, $\tau$ is never surjective (script Note “About $\tau$”; lesson 10, section 7 showed that the dual set is not a basis there).

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof A from the frame alone.

Why $\tau$ is called canonical, precisely. (1) It is defined without any basis. (2) It is natural: for every linear $T:V\to W$ the square “$\tau_W\circ T=T^{**}\circ\tau_V$” commutes (script Note “Naturality”, p. 322; Exercise 2 below, once $T^*$ is defined). (3) It turns dual bases into dual bases: $\tau(v_i)=(v_i^*)^*$ for every basis (script Exercise, p. 323; Exercise 1). Because of this, one identifies $V^{**}$ with $V$ in finite dimension and writes $v(\ell)=\ell(v)$. The symmetric notation $\langle\ell,v\rangle:=\ell(v)$, the dual pairing (Definition 10.3.5), is linear in each slot (Exercise 10.3.6: it is “bilinear”).

Worked example 2: house-inspectors and dual bases

Let $V=\mathbb R[x]_{\le2}$ and $p=1+x^2$. (a) Compute $\tau(p)$ on $\ell_1(q)=q(2)$ and on $\ell_2(q)=\int_0^1q(t)\,dt$. (b) Let $B=(1,x,x^2)$ with dual basis $(1^*,x^*,(x^2)^*)$. Show $\tau(x)=(x^*)^*$, i.e. $\tau(x)$ takes the values $0,1,0$ on $1^*,x^*,(x^2)^*$.

    Answer

    $\tau(p)(\ell_1)=\ell_1(p)=p(2)=5$ and $\tau(p)(\ell_2)=\int_0^1(1+t^2)\,dt=1+\frac13=\frac43$. The inspector of $p$ just asks each surveyor about $p$.

4. The dual map

Definition (dual map; script Definition 10.4.5, p. 325). For $T\in\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$ the dual map is

$$\begin{aligned}&T^*:W^*\to V^*,\\ &T^*(\psi):=\psi\circ T\quad(\psi\in W^*),\end{aligned}$$

that is, $\big(T^*\psi\big)(v)=\psi(Tv)$ for $v\in V$. Note the direction: $T$ goes from $V$ to $W$, but $T^*$ goes from $W^*$ to $V^*$. (The script warns in Note 10.4.6 that $T^*$ also denotes the adjoint map for scalar products, Linear Algebra II; under the identification of Note 10.5.10 the two agree.)

Proof B: the dual map is well defined and linear (script Proposition 10.4.7, p. 325; HS24, 3.A4 and FS25, 4.A4, first parts)

Claim. For every $\psi\in W^*$, $T^*(\psi)\in V^*$ (well defined), and $T^*:W^*\to V^*$ is linear.

The idea. “Well defined” here means: the formula lands where it should. $\psi\circ T$ is a composition of linear maps, hence linear $V\to K$ (lesson 11). Linearity of $T^*$ is a statement about functionals on $V$, so test it on an arbitrary $v$: there it is just the definition of $+$ and scalar multiplication in $W^*$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Well defined: “$\psi\circ T:V\to K$ is a composition of linear maps.”
  • Linear, first line: “Let $\psi_1,\psi_2\in W^*$, $\alpha\in K$, and $v\in V$.” Last line: “So $T^*(\alpha\psi_1+\psi_2)=\alpha T^*\psi_1+T^*\psi_2$.”
  • The gap: how $+$ and scalars are defined in $W^*$ and $V^*$ (pointwise).

“Send by courier, then read” is a fair surveyor, because both steps are fair.

Let $\psi\in W^*$. Then $\psi\circ T:V\to K$ is a composition of linear maps, hence linear (lesson 11): $T^*(\psi)\in V^*$.

Mixing two sister-city surveyors and then pulling back is the same as pulling back each and mixing.

Let $\psi_1,\psi_2\in W^*$, $\alpha\in K$. For every $v\in V$: $T^*(\alpha\psi_1+\psi_2)(v)=(\alpha\psi_1+\psi_2)(Tv)$ $=\alpha\psi_1(Tv)+\psi_2(Tv)$ $=\alpha(T^*\psi_1)(v)+(T^*\psi_2)(v)$ $=(\alpha T^*\psi_1+T^*\psi_2)(v)$. As $v$ was arbitrary, $T^*(\alpha\psi_1+\psi_2)=\alpha T^*\psi_1+T^*\psi_2$. ∎

The proof in one piece

For $\psi\in W^*$, $\psi\circ T$ is linear as a composition of linear maps, so $T^*\psi\in V^*$. For $\psi_1,\psi_2\in W^*$, $\alpha\in K$ and all $v\in V$, $T^*(\alpha\psi_1+\psi_2)(v)=(\alpha\psi_1+\psi_2)(Tv)=\alpha\psi_1(Tv)+\psi_2(Tv)=(\alpha T^*\psi_1+T^*\psi_2)(v)$. Hence $T^*$ is linear. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Write the definition with the direction: $T^*:W^*\to V^*$, $\psi\mapsto\psi\circ T$.
  2. Well defined: a composition of linear maps is linear.
  3. Linear: evaluate both sides at an arbitrary $v\in V$.

In short: everything about $T^*$ is checked by evaluating at a vector: $(T^*\psi)(v)=\psi(Tv)$.

Proof C: the rules of the dual map (script Proposition 10.4.8, p. 325; HS24, 3.A4)

Claim. Let $T:V\to W$ and $S:W\to U$ be linear. Then (1) $(S\circ T)^*=T^*\circ S^*$; (2) $(\mathrm{id}_V)^*=\mathrm{id}_{V^*}$; (3) if $T$ is an isomorphism, then so is $T^*$, and $(T^*)^{-1}=(T^{-1})^*$.

The idea. (1) and (2) are one line each: composition of maps is associative. The order reverses because pulling back goes backwards: to pull a surveyor from the third city to the first, first pull him back to the second, then to the first. (3) follows from (1) and (2) without any computation: apply ${}^*$ to $T\circ T^{-1}=\mathrm{id}$ and $T^{-1}\circ T=\mathrm{id}$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (1) “For $\varphi\in U^*$: $(S\circ T)^*(\varphi)=\varphi\circ(S\circ T)=\dots=T^*(S^*\varphi)$.”
  • (2) “For $\varphi\in V^*$: $(\mathrm{id}_V)^*\varphi=\varphi\circ\mathrm{id}_V=\varphi$.”
  • (3) “$\mathrm{id}_{V^*}=(\mathrm{id}_V)^*=(T^{-1}\circ T)^*=T^*\circ(T^{-1})^*$, and the other order.”
The storyWhat you write

A surveyor of the third city, pulled back along a relay, is pulled back twice: first to the middle city, then to the first.

(1) For every $\varphi\in U^*$: $(S\circ T)^*(\varphi)=\varphi\circ(S\circ T)=(\varphi\circ S)\circ T$ $=T^*(\varphi\circ S)=T^*(S^*\varphi)=(T^*\circ S^*)(\varphi)$.

Pulling back along “stay home” changes nothing.

(2) For every $\varphi\in V^*$: $(\mathrm{id}_V)^*(\varphi)=\varphi\circ\mathrm{id}_V=\varphi=\mathrm{id}_{V^*}(\varphi)$.

A courier who can be undone gives a pull-back that can be undone, by the pull-back of the return courier.

(3) Let $T$ be an isomorphism with inverse $T^{-1}:W\to V$ (linear, lesson 12, Proof C). By (2) and (1): $\mathrm{id}_{V^*}=(\mathrm{id}_V)^*=(T^{-1}\circ T)^*=T^*\circ(T^{-1})^*$ and $\mathrm{id}_{W^*}=(T\circ T^{-1})^*=(T^{-1})^*\circ T^*$. So $T^*$ is bijective with inverse $(T^{-1})^*$; it is linear by Proof B. ∎

5. The annihilator

Definition (annihilator; script Definition 10.5.1, p. 327). For a vector space $V$ and a subset $\emptyset\ne U\subseteq V$,

$$\begin{aligned}U^0&:=\{\varphi\in V^*\mid\varphi(u)=0\ \text{for all }u\in U\}\\ &=\{\varphi\in V^*\mid U\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}\,\varphi\}.\end{aligned}$$

In the lecture it is written $U^\perp$; some authors call it the orthogonal space of $U$ (Note 10.5.2). In $K^n$ with functionals as rows: $U^0$ is the set of rows $\varphi$ with $\varphi u=0$ for all $u\in U$.

Proof E: the annihilator is a subspace, and $\dim U^0=\dim V-\dim U$ (script Propositions 10.5.3 (1) and 10.5.4, pp. 328–329; FS25, 4.A1–4.A2)

Claim. (a) $U^0$ is a subspace of $V^*$. (b) Let $\dim V=n$ and $U\subseteq V$ a subspace with basis $(u_1,\dots,u_k)$, extended to a basis $B=(u_1,\dots,u_k,v_1,\dots,v_{n-k})$ of $V$, with dual basis $B^*=(u_1^*,\dots,u_k^*,v_1^*,\dots,v_{n-k}^*)$. Then $U^0=\mathrm{Sp}(v_1^*,\dots,v_{n-k}^*)$, and $\dim U^0=\dim V-\dim U$.

The idea. (a) is the subspace test. (b) A basis adapted to $U$ makes everything visible. The dual vectors $v_j^*$ vanish on all $u_i$, hence on $U$: they lie in $U^0$. Conversely, write $\varphi\in U^0$ in the dual basis; its coefficients are its values on $B$ (lesson 10: coordinates in a dual basis are values), and the values on the $u_i$ are $0$. So $\varphi$ is a combination of the $v_j^*$ alone. These are part of a basis, hence independent: they are a basis of $U^0$, with $n-k$ elements.

  • (b) ⊆: “Let $\varphi\in U^0$. Write $\varphi=\sum_i\alpha_iu_i^*+\sum_j\beta_jv_j^*$. Evaluate at $u_i$: $\alpha_i=0$.”
  • The gap: a subset of a basis is independent, and a functional vanishing on a spanning set vanishes on the span.

The direction “$T$ surjective $\Rightarrow T^*$ injective” needs nothing: if $\psi\circ T=0$ and $T$ is onto, then $\psi=0$. That is FS18, 4 (a) in one line.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof F from the frame alone.

One more consequence is worth a preview. Combining (1) with Proof E gives $\dim\mathrm{Ker}\,T^*=\dim W-\dim\mathrm{Im}\,T$. With the rank theorem of lesson 15 this turns into $\dim\mathrm{Im}\,T=\dim\mathrm{Im}\,T^*$, and by Proof D that says: the column rank of a matrix equals its row rank (script Corollaries 10.5.8–10.5.9). That is lesson 16.

Why it's cool

Annihilators turn subspaces into equations. A plane through $0$ in $\mathbb R^3$ is $\{v:\varphi(v)=0\}$ for one functional $\varphi$, a line through $0$ needs two, and in general a $k$-dimensional subspace of $K^n$ is cut out by exactly $n-k$ independent linear equations, namely a basis of $U^0$ (Proof E and Exercise 6). Every system of homogeneous linear equations is a question about an annihilator, which is why Gaussian elimination (lessons 16–17) and duality belong together. And Proof D explains a mystery of matrix arithmetic: the transpose, which looks like a mere rearrangement of entries, is the matrix of a genuine map, the dual map, and the rule $(AB)^T=B^TA^T$ is the order reversal of pulling back.

For wonder: points and lines trade places (optional, 5 min)

In $V=\mathbb R^3$, the annihilator turns lines through $0$ (dimension $1$) into planes through $0$ in $V^*$ (dimension $2$), and planes into lines. It reverses inclusions: if a line lies in a plane, the plane's annihilator lies in the line's (Exercise 5). Now think of lines through $0$ in $\mathbb R^3$ as the “points” and planes through $0$ as the “lines” of the projective plane, the geometry of perspective drawing. Then the annihilator is a dictionary that swaps points and lines and keeps incidence: “two points lie on one line” becomes “two lines meet in one point”. Every theorem of projective geometry has a dual theorem for free; for example, Pascal's theorem about six points on a conic dualises to Brianchon's theorem about six tangent lines. Proof A is why the dictionary works both ways: applying it twice gives back what you started with ($V^{**}=V$).

Worked example 6: quick exam items (HS18, (a); true/false)

True or false, with a reason. $V,W$ are finite-dimensional unless said otherwise.

  1. HS18, (a): “the term dual basis denotes a basis of $V^*$ that is uniquely determined by $V$.”
    Answer

    False. The dual basis is determined by a basis of $V$, not by $V$: Worked example 1 (b) shows two bases containing $e_1$ with different partners for it.

  2. For every vector space $V$, the map $\tau:V\to V^{**}$ is an isomorphism.
    Answer

    False in general: it is always injective, but surjective only if $\dim V<\infty$ (Proof A and the script's Note “About $\tau$”).

  3. If $T:V\to W$ is injective, then $T^*$ is injective.
    Answer

    False. $T:K\to K^2$, $T(x)=(x,0)$ is injective, but $T^*(e_2^*)=e_2^*\circ T=0$ with $e_2^*\ne0$. By Proof F (3), $T^*$ is surjective instead.

  4. If $U_1\subseteq U_2\subseteq V$, then $U_2^0\subseteq U_1^0$.
    Answer

    True. A functional vanishing on $U_2$ vanishes on the smaller $U_1$. The bigger the district, the shorter the register.

  5. $[T^*]^{C^*}_{B^*}=[T]^B_C$ for every $T\in\mathrm{End}(K^n)$ and $B=C=E$.
    Answer

    False (true only for symmetric matrices): it is the transpose. $T(x,y)=(y,0)$ has $[T]_E=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $[T^*]_{E^*}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.

Exercise 1 (script Exercise on p. 323: $\tau$ maps a basis to the dual of its dual)

Let $\dim V=n$, $B=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ a basis, $B^*=(v_1^*,\dots,v_n^*)$ its dual basis and $(B^*)^*=((v_1^*)^*,\dots,(v_n^*)^*)$ the basis of $V^{**}$ dual to $B^*$. Show that $\tau(v_i)=(v_i^*)^*$ for all $i$.

Hint

Two elements of $V^{**}$ are equal if they agree on a basis of $V^*$. Evaluate both sides at $v_j^*$.

Solution

Frame: compare two linear maps $V^*\to K$ on the basis $B^*$.

For every $j$: $\tau(v_i)(v_j^*)=v_j^*(v_i)=\delta_{ji}$, and $(v_i^*)^*(v_j^*)=\delta_{ij}$ by the definition of the dual basis of $B^*$. Both are linear maps $V^*\to K$ that agree on the basis $B^*$, so they are equal (lesson 11): $\tau(v_i)=(v_i^*)^*$. In words: $\tau$ sends every basis to the dual basis of its dual basis, for every basis at once; that is the precise sense in which $\tau$ needs no choice.

Exercise 2 (script Note “Naturality”, p. 322)

Let $T:V\to W$ be linear and $T^{**}:=(T^*)^*:V^{**}\to W^{**}$. Show that $\tau_W\circ T=T^{**}\circ\tau_V$ as maps $V\to W^{**}$.

Hint

Both sides send $v$ to an element of $W^{**}$; evaluate it at an arbitrary $\psi\in W^*$ and unfold the definitions one at a time.

Solution

Frame: equality of maps into $W^{**}$: test at $v\in V$, then at $\psi\in W^*$. (Checked twice; the script leaves it to the reader.)

Let $v\in V$ and $\psi\in W^*$. Left: $(\tau_W(Tv))(\psi)=\psi(Tv)$. Right: $\big(T^{**}(\tau_Vv)\big)(\psi)=\big(\tau_V(v)\circ T^*\big)(\psi)=\tau_V(v)(T^*\psi)=(T^*\psi)(v)=\psi(Tv)$. Equal for all $\psi$ and all $v$. So the square commutes: under the identification $V=V^{**}$, $W=W^{**}$, the map $T^{**}$ is $T$. (In matrices: $(A^T)^T=A$.)

Exercise 3 (FS18, problem 4, 15 points)

Let $V,W$ be finite-dimensional over $K$, $T\in\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$, and $T^*\in\mathrm{Hom}(W^*,V^*)$ given by $(T^*f)(v)=f(Tv)$. (a) (4 points) Show: if $T$ is surjective, then $T^*$ is injective. (b) (6 points) Let $T$ be injective and $U\subseteq W$ a subspace with $W=U\oplus\mathrm{Im}(T)$. Show that $\Phi:W\to V$, $w=u+Tv\mapsto v$, is a well-defined homomorphism. (c) (5 points) Show: if $T$ is injective, then $T^*$ is surjective.

Hint

(a) Kernel: if $f\circ T=0$, where can $f$ be nonzero? (b) Well defined: is the decomposition $w=u+Tv$ unique, and is $v$ determined by $Tv$? (c) Given $\varphi\in V^*$, try $f=\varphi\circ\Phi$ and compute $T^*f$.

Solution

Frame: (a) kernel; (b) uniqueness of the decomposition, then linearity; (c) exhibit a preimage. (Checked twice, no official solution. Proof F (3) gives (a) and (c) at once; the exam wants this hands-on route.)

(a) Let $f\in\mathrm{Ker}\,T^*$: $f(Tv)=0$ for all $v$. Since $T$ is surjective, every $w\in W$ is some $Tv$, so $f(w)=0$ for all $w$: $f=0$. So $T^*$ is injective (lesson 11, Proof G).

(b) Well defined: since $W=U\oplus\mathrm{Im}(T)$, every $w$ is $w=u+w'$ with unique $u\in U$, $w'\in\mathrm{Im}(T)$ (lesson 8). And $w'=Tv$ for exactly one $v$, because $T$ is injective. So $\Phi(w)=v$ is a well-defined value. Linear: if $w_1=u_1+Tv_1$, $w_2=u_2+Tv_2$ and $\alpha\in K$, then $w_1+\alpha w_2=(u_1+\alpha u_2)+T(v_1+\alpha v_2)$ with $u_1+\alpha u_2\in U$; by uniqueness, $\Phi(w_1+\alpha w_2)=v_1+\alpha v_2=\Phi(w_1)+\alpha\Phi(w_2)$.

(c) Let $T$ be injective. A complement $U$ of $\mathrm{Im}(T)$ in $W$ exists (lesson 8: extend a basis of $\mathrm{Im}(T)$ to a basis of $W$). Take $\Phi$ from (b); note $\Phi(Tv)=v$ (decomposition $0+Tv$), i.e. $\Phi\circ T=\mathrm{id}_V$. For $\varphi\in V^*$ put $f:=\varphi\circ\Phi\in W^*$. Then $T^*f=\varphi\circ\Phi\circ T=\varphi\circ\mathrm{id}_V=\varphi$. So $T^*$ is surjective.

Exercise 4 (script Proposition 10.5.3: the dual of a quotient, p. 328)

Let $U\subseteq V$ be a subspace and $\pi:V\to V/U$ the quotient map (lesson 9). Show that $\mathcal L:(V/U)^*\to U^0$, $S\mapsto S\circ\pi$, is a well-defined linear isomorphism. In words: functionals on $V/U$ are the same as functionals on $V$ that vanish on $U$.

Hint

$\mathcal L=\pi^*$ (Proof B), so linearity is free; $\pi$ is surjective, so $\pi^*$ is injective (Exercise 3 (a)). For surjectivity, given $T\in U^0$, define $T'(v+U):=T(v)$ and check that this is well defined (lesson 9).

Solution

Frame: lands in $U^0$; linear; injective; surjective via a well-defined map on cosets. (The script leaves parts as an exercise; checked twice.)

Lands in $U^0$: for $u\in U$, $(S\circ\pi)(u)=S(u+U)=S(0_{V/U})=0$. Linear: $\mathcal L=\pi^*$, linear by Proof B. Injective: if $S\circ\pi=0$, then $S(v+U)=0$ for all $v$, and every element of $V/U$ is some $v+U$, so $S=0$. Surjective: let $T\in U^0$. Define $T':V/U\to K$, $T'(v+U):=T(v)$. Well defined: if $v+U=v'+U$, then $v-v'\in U$, so $T(v)-T(v')=T(v-v')=0$. Linear: $T'((v+U)+\alpha(v'+U))=T'((v+\alpha v')+U)=T(v)+\alpha T(v')$. And $\mathcal L(T')=T'\circ\pi=T$. So $\mathcal L$ is an isomorphism. In finite dimension this gives $\dim U^0=\dim(V/U)=\dim V-\dim U$ again (lesson 9), a second proof of Proof E's formula.

Exercise 5 (rules for annihilators)

Let $U_1,U_2\subseteq V$ be subspaces. (a) Show: $U_1\subseteq U_2\Rightarrow U_2^0\subseteq U_1^0$. (b) Show $(U_1+U_2)^0=U_1^0\cap U_2^0$. (c) In $\mathbb R^3$ find a basis of $U^0$ for $U=\mathrm{Sp}((1,1,1))$, and describe $U$ as the solution set of linear equations.

Hint

(b) Two inclusions; for “⊇”, a functional vanishing on $U_1$ and on $U_2$ vanishes on every $u_1+u_2$. (c) One equation $a+b+c=0$ for $\varphi=(a,b,c)$.

Solution

Frame: unfold the definition; two inclusions; solve a linear equation.

(a) If $\varphi$ vanishes on $U_2$, it vanishes on the subset $U_1$.

(b) “⊆”: $U_i\subseteq U_1+U_2$, so by (a) $(U_1+U_2)^0\subseteq U_i^0$ for $i=1,2$. “⊇”: if $\varphi\in U_1^0\cap U_2^0$, then $\varphi(u_1+u_2)=\varphi(u_1)+\varphi(u_2)=0$ for all $u_i\in U_i$.

(c) $\varphi=ae_1^*+be_2^*+ce_3^*\in U^0\iff a+b+c=0$. Basis: $e_1^*-e_2^*$, $e_2^*-e_3^*$ (dimension $2=3-1$ ✓). And $U=\{v:(e_1^*-e_2^*)(v)=0,\ (e_2^*-e_3^*)(v)=0\}=\{(x,y,z):x=y=z\}$: two equations for a line in $\mathbb R^3$ (Exercise 6 shows that this always works).

Exercise 6 (the double annihilator: every subspace is a solution set)

Let $\dim V<\infty$ and $U\subseteq V$ a subspace. Show that $U=\{v\in V\mid\varphi(v)=0\text{ for all }\varphi\in U^0\}$. Equivalently, $\tau(U)=(U^0)^0$.

Hint

“⊆” is the definition. For “⊇”, take $v\notin U$ and find $\varphi\in U^0$ with $\varphi(v)\ne0$: extend a basis of $U$ by $v$, then to a basis of $V$, and use a dual basis vector.

Solution

Frame: two inclusions; the hard one by contraposition with a dual basis vector; then count for the second form. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

“⊆”: every $\varphi\in U^0$ vanishes on $U$ by definition. “⊇” by contraposition: let $v\notin U$ and $(u_1,\dots,u_k)$ a basis of $U$. Then $(u_1,\dots,u_k,v)$ is independent (lesson 6: $v\notin\mathrm{Sp}(u_i)$); extend it to a basis of $V$ and let $v^*$ be the dual vector of $v$ in its dual basis. By Proof E, $v^*\in U^0$, and $v^*(v)=1\ne0$. So $v$ is not in the right-hand set.

Second form: $\tau(v)\in(U^0)^0$ means $\tau(v)(\varphi)=\varphi(v)=0$ for all $\varphi\in U^0$, so the first form says $\tau(U)\subseteq(U^0)^0$, with equality because both have dimension $\dim U$ (Proof E twice: $\dim(U^0)^0=n-(n-\dim U)$; and $\tau$ is injective). So a $k$-dimensional subspace of $K^n$ is the solution set of the $n-k$ equations given by a basis of $U^0$.

Exercise 7 (the dual map in coordinates, from lesson 13)

Let $T:\mathbb R[x]_{\le2}\to\mathbb R^2$, $T(p)=(p(0),p(1))$, with $E=(1,x,x^2)$ and the standard basis $E_2=(e_1,e_2)$. (a) Describe $T^*(e_1^*)$ and $T^*(e_2^*)$ as functionals on $\mathbb R[x]_{\le2}$. (b) Compute $[T^*]^{E_2^*}_{E^*}$ with Proof D and check its columns against (a). (c) Is $T^*$ injective? Is it surjective? Use Proof F.

Hint

(a) $(T^*e_1^*)(p)=e_1^*(T(p))$. (b) Lesson 13, Exercise 5 (a) has $[T]^E_{E_2}$. (c) Is $T$ surjective? Injective?

Solution

Frame: unfold; transpose; translate injective/surjective through Proof F (3).

(a) $(T^*e_1^*)(p)=e_1^*(p(0),p(1))=p(0)$, so $T^*e_1^*=\mathrm{ev}_0$; likewise $T^*e_2^*=\mathrm{ev}_1$.

(b) $[T]^E_{E_2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\1&1&1\end{pmatrix}$, so $[T^*]^{E_2^*}_{E^*}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Column 1 should be $[\mathrm{ev}_0]_{E^*}=(\mathrm{ev}_0(1),\mathrm{ev}_0(x),\mathrm{ev}_0(x^2))=(1,0,0)$ ✓; column 2 $[\mathrm{ev}_1]_{E^*}=(1,1,1)$ ✓.

(c) $T$ is surjective: $T(1-x)=(1,0)$ and $T(x)=(0,1)$. So $T^*$ is injective (Proof F (3); directly: $\mathrm{ev}_0,\mathrm{ev}_1$ are independent). $T$ is not injective: $T(x^2-x)=(0,0)$. So $T^*$ is not surjective: its image is $(\mathrm{Ker}\,T)^0$, the functionals vanishing on $x^2-x$, a $2$-dimensional subspace of the $3$-dimensional $V^*$.
