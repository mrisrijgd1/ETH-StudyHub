Serie 4, explained: Subspaces, affine spaces, Latin squares

When a subset is a subspace, why subspaces of finite-dimensional spaces are finite-dimensional, bases of planes and their intersections in $\mathbb R^3$, all the lines of the tiny plane $\mathbb F_4^2$, and a card puzzle that those lines solve.

You're done when
you can (1) prove that $W$ is a subspace iff $\langle W\rangle=W$; (2) find a basis of a plane or a line given by equations, by solving for some coordinates; (3) list all lines through $0$ in $\mathbb F_4^2$ and count all lines; (4) explain how parallel classes of lines give orthogonal Latin squares.

Aufgabe 20 · Subspaces and spans; finite dimension is inherited

(a) Show: if $V$ is a vector space over $K$ and $W\subseteq V$, then $W$ is a subspace of $V$ iff $\langle W\rangle=W$.

(b) Show: if $V$ is finite-dimensional and $W\subseteq V$ is a subspace, then $W$ is finite-dimensional.

In short: (a) A subspace contains all combinations of its vectors, and a set that equals its span passes the subspace test. (b) Keep adding vectors of $W$ not yet spanned; independence in $V$ stops you after at most $\dim V$ steps.

The idea

(a) “Closed under $+$ and scaling” is the same as “closed under all finite linear combinations”, by induction on the number of terms. One direction uses that, the other just checks (U0) to (U2) using that $0_V$, $x+y$ and $\lambda x$ are combinations. (b) Build a basis of $W$ greedily: pick $y_1\ne0$ in $W$, then $y_2$ in $W$ outside $\langle y_1\rangle$, and so on. Each new vector keeps the list independent, and an independent list in $V$ has at most $\dim V$ vectors, so the process must stop with a list that spans $W$.

You need

Lesson 5, section 6: $W\subseteq V$ is a subspace iff (U0) $W\ne\emptyset$, (U1) $x+y\in W$ for $x,y\in W$, (U2) $\lambda x\in W$ for $\lambda\in K$, $x\in W$. Lesson 6, section 2: $\langle W\rangle$ is the set of all finite linear combinations of vectors of $W$; the empty combination is $0_V$. For (b): an independent list in $V$ has at most $\dim V$ elements (lesson 7, section 3), and adding a vector outside the span keeps a list independent (lesson 7, section 2).

Hint 1

(a) “$\Rightarrow$”: $W\subseteq\langle W\rangle$ always ($x=1\cdot x$). For $\langle W\rangle\subseteq W$ use induction on the number $n$ of terms in $\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_ix_i$.

Hint 2

(b) If $W\ne\{0_V\}$, choose $y_1\in W\setminus\{0_V\}$. If $\langle y_1,\dots,y_k\rangle\ne W$, choose $y_{k+1}\in W\setminus\langle y_1,\dots,y_k\rangle$. Why does this stop?

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

(a) “$\Rightarrow$”, first inclusion: every vector is a one-term combination of itself.

(a) ($\Rightarrow$) Let $W$ be a subspace. $W\subseteq\langle W\rangle$, since $x=1\cdot x$ for $x\in W$.

Second inclusion: combinations of vectors of $W$ stay in $W$, by induction on the number of terms.

$\langle W\rangle\subseteq W$: by induction on $n$, $\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_ix_i\in W$ for all $\lambda_i\in K$, $x_i\in W$. For $n=0$ the empty sum is $0_V\in W$. If true for $n$, then $\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\lambda_ix_i=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_ix_i+\lambda_{n+1}x_{n+1}\in W$ by (U2) and (U1). So $\langle W\rangle=W$.

“$\Leftarrow$”: check (U0) to (U2); each required vector is a linear combination.

($\Leftarrow$) Let $\langle W\rangle=W$. (U0): the empty combination $0_V\in\langle W\rangle=W$. (U1): for $x,y\in W$, $x+y=1\cdot x+1\cdot y\in\langle W\rangle=W$. (U2): $\lambda x\in\langle W\rangle=W$. So $W$ is a subspace.

(b) Build an independent list in $W$ greedily.

(b) If $W=\{0_V\}$, $\emptyset$ is a basis. Otherwise choose $y_1\in W\setminus\{0_V\}$. As long as $\langle y_1,\dots,y_k\rangle\ne W$, choose $y_{k+1}\in W\setminus\langle y_1,\dots,y_k\rangle$.

Each step keeps independence; independent lists in $V$ have at most $\dim V$ vectors. So it stops, and when it stops the list spans $W$.

By induction, every list $y_1,\dots,y_k$ is linearly independent (a vector outside the span of independent vectors keeps them independent). Independent vectors in $V$ number at most $d:=\dim V$, so the process stops after $k\le d$ steps, and then $\langle y_1,\dots,y_k\rangle=W$. So $\{y_1,\dots,y_k\}$ is a finite basis of $W$. ∎

Remark (official): if $\dim W=\dim V$, then $W=V$: a basis of $W$ is independent in $V$ with $\dim V$ elements, hence already a basis of $V$. So a subspace $W\ne V$ has smaller dimension. This is used in Aufgabe 22.

Trap

In (b), writing “take a basis of $W$; it has at most $\dim V$ elements”. That assumes $W$ already has a (finite) basis, which is what you are proving. Build the basis inside $W$ and use the bound from $V$ to stop.

Aufgabe 21 · Bases of planes and their intersections

For $\lambda\in\mathbb R$ and $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\ne(0,0,0)$ let $U_{\lambda;\alpha,\beta,\gamma}:=\{(x_1,x_2,x_3)\in\mathbb R^3:\alpha x_1+\beta x_2+\gamma x_3=\lambda\}$; it is a subspace iff $\lambda=0$.

(a) Find a basis of each of $U_{0;1,2,3}$, $U_{0;2,0,1}$, $U_{0;2,0,0}$.

(b) Find a basis of each of $U_{0;1,2,3}\cap U_{0;2,0,1}$, $U_{0;2,0,1}\cap U_{0;2,0,0}$, $U_{0;2,0,0}\cap U_{0;1,2,3}$.

In short: Solve each equation for one coordinate; the free coordinates give the basis vectors. Two different planes through $0$ meet in a line.

The idea

One equation in three unknowns leaves two free choices, so each $E_i$ is a plane and gets two basis vectors: solve for one variable, set one free variable to $1$ and the other to $0$, and vice versa. An intersection means both equations at once; two independent equations leave one free choice, a line, with one basis vector.

You need

Bases from free variables (lesson 6, section 5), intersections of subspaces (lesson 8, section 2).

Solution

Write $E_1:=U_{0;1,2,3}$, $E_2:=U_{0;2,0,1}$, $E_3:=U_{0;2,0,0}$.

Why this stepWhat you write

$E_1$: solve for $x_1$; $x_2,x_3$ are free.

$x\in E_1\iff x_1=-2x_2-3x_3$, so $x=x_2(-2,1,0)+x_3(-3,0,1)$. Both vectors lie in $E_1$ ($-2+2=0$, $-3+3=0$), so they span $E_1$; and $\lambda(-2,1,0)+\mu(-3,0,1)=(-2\lambda-3\mu,\lambda,\mu)=0$ forces $\lambda=\mu=0$. Basis: $\{(-2,1,0),(-3,0,1)\}$.

$E_2$: solve for $x_3$.

$x\in E_2\iff x_3=-2x_1$, so $x=x_1(1,0,-2)+x_2(0,1,0)$; independent by the first two coordinates. Basis: $\{(1,0,-2),(0,1,0)\}$.

$E_3$: the equation says $x_1=0$.

$x\in E_3\iff x_1=0$, so $x=x_2(0,1,0)+x_3(0,0,1)$. Basis: $\{(0,1,0),(0,0,1)\}$.

(b) $E_1\cap E_2$: substitute $x_3=-2x_1$ into the first equation.

(b) $x_3=-2x_1$ and $x_1+2x_2-6x_1=0$, so $x_2=\frac52x_1$ and $x=\frac{x_1}2(2,5,-4)$. Conversely $(2,5,-4)$ satisfies both ($2+10-12=0$, $4-4=0$). One non-zero vector is independent, so $\{(2,5,-4)\}$ is a basis of $E_1\cap E_2$.

$E_2\cap E_3$: $x_1=0$, then $x_3=0$.

$x_1=0$ and then $x_3=0$; $x_2$ is free. Basis: $\{(0,1,0)\}$.

$E_3\cap E_1$: $x_1=0$, then $2x_2+3x_3=0$.

$x_1=0$ and $x_2=-\frac32x_3$, so $x=-\frac{x_3}2(0,3,-2)$, and $(0,3,-2)$ satisfies both. Basis: $\{(0,3,-2)\}$.

Trap

Giving vectors that span but forgetting to check that each lies in the subspace, or giving a basis with the wrong number of vectors. A plane in $\mathbb R^3$ needs exactly two, a line exactly one. Quick check: plug each basis vector into the equation(s).

Aufgabe 22 · All lines of the plane $\mathbb F_4^2$

Let $\mathbb F_4=\{0,1,a,b\}$ be the field from Aufgabe 9. (a) Find a basis of every non-trivial subspace of $\mathbb F_4^2$. (b) List all elements of the non-trivial subspace containing $(1,b)$. (c) How many non-trivial affine spaces are there in $\mathbb F_4^2$?

In short: $\mathbb F_4^2$ has 16 points. The non-trivial subspaces are the 5 lines through $0$, each with 4 points. Each line has 4 parallel copies: 20 lines in total.

The idea

$\mathbb F_4^2$ is a plane with $4^2=16$ points. A non-trivial subspace has dimension 1 (not 0, not 2), so it is a line $\langle v\rangle$ through $0$ with 4 points. The 15 non-zero points split into these lines, 3 non-zero points each, so there are 5 lines through $0$: one for each “slope” $0,1,a,b$, plus the vertical line. Each line has 4 parallel translates that tile the plane, so there are $5\cdot4=20$ lines (non-trivial affine spaces) in total.

The 16 points of $\mathbb F_4^2$ (horizontal: $x$, vertical: $y$). Highlighted: the line $g_3=\langle(1,b)\rangle$, with points $(0,0)$, $(1,b)$, $(a,1)$, $(b,a)$. Lines over $\mathbb F_4$ do not look straight in a picture.

You need

Serie 1, Aufgabe 9 (the tables of $\mathbb F_4$: $x+x=0$, $1+a=b$, $a\cdot a=b$, $a\cdot b=1$, $b\cdot b=a$). Aufgabe 20 (dimension of a subspace). Lesson 9, section 2 (affine subspaces $v+U$) and section 3 (translates are equal or disjoint).

Hint 1

A non-trivial subspace $U$ of the 2-dimensional $\mathbb F_4^2$ has dimension 1 (Aufgabe 20), so $U=\langle v\rangle=\{0,v,av,bv\}$ for any $v\ne0$ in $U$. Normalise $v$: if its first entry $s\ne0$, use $s^{-1}v=(1,\ast)$.

Hint 2

(c) For a line $U$: each translate $v+U$ has 4 points, two translates are equal or disjoint, so the 16 points split into exactly 4 translates. Different lines give different translates, since $U=\{p-p':p,p'\in v+U\}$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

A subspace that is neither $\{0\}$ nor everything has dimension 1, so it is spanned by any of its non-zero vectors.

(a) $V:=\mathbb F_4^2$ has 16 elements and dimension 2. A non-trivial subspace $U$ has a basis with fewer than 2 and more than 0 vectors (Aufgabe 20), so $U=\langle u\rangle$ with $u\ne0$; and for any $v\in U\setminus\{0\}$, $U=\langle v\rangle$.

Normalise the spanning vector: first entry $1$, or the vector $(0,1)$.

For $v=(s,t)\ne0$: if $s\ne0$, then $s^{-1}v=(1,s^{-1}t)\in\langle v\rangle$; if $s=0$, then $t^{-1}v=(0,1)$. So $\langle v\rangle$ is one of

List the five lines, writing $0v,1v,av,bv$.

$$\begin{aligned}g_0&=\{(0,0),(1,0),(a,0),(b,0)\},\\g_1&=\{(0,0),(1,1),(a,a),(b,b)\},\\g_2&=\{(0,0),(1,a),(a,b),(b,1)\},\\g_3&=\{(0,0),(1,b),(a,1),(b,a)\},\\g_\infty&=\{(0,0),(0,1),(0,a),(0,b)\}.\end{aligned}$$ (E.g. $a(1,a)=(a,b)$, $b(1,a)=(b,1)$.) No non-zero vector occurs twice ($5\cdot3=15$ non-zero vectors in all), so these are 5 different subspaces, with bases $\{(1,0)\},\{(1,1)\},\{(1,a)\},\{(1,b)\},\{(0,1)\}$.

(b) Only one line contains $(1,b)$: $g_3$.

(b) $(1,b)$ lies in exactly one non-trivial subspace, $g_3=\langle(1,b)\rangle$: $0(1,b)=(0,0)$, $1(1,b)=(1,b)$, $a(1,b)=(a,ab)=(a,1)$, $b(1,b)=(b,bb)=(b,a)$. So $g_3=\{(0,0),(1,b),(a,1),(b,a)\}$.

(c) Each line $U$ has exactly 4 translates: each has 4 points, translates are equal or disjoint, 16 points in all.

(c) Fix a line $U$. Each $v+U$ has 4 elements ($v+u=v+u'\Rightarrow u=u'$) and contains $v$. If $v+U$ and $w+U$ share a point $v+u_1=w+u_2$, then $v+U=w+U$. So the 16 points split into disjoint translates of size 4: exactly $16:4=4$ translates.

Different lines give different translates, so count $5\cdot4$.

A translate $A=v+U$ determines $U=\{p-p':p,p'\in A\}$, so translates of different lines differ. Hence there are $5\cdot4=20$ non-trivial affine spaces in $\mathbb F_4^2$: the 5 lines through $0$ and 15 lines not through $0$. ∎

Explicitly: for $m\in\mathbb F_4$ and $q\in\mathbb F_4$, the lines $\{(x,y):y=m\cdot x+q\}$ (16 of them), and the vertical lines $\{(x,y):x=q\}$ (4 of them).

Trap

Counting lines by choosing two points: $\binom{16}2=120$ pairs, but each line contains $\binom42=6$ pairs, so $120:6=20$. If you count pairs, divide; if you count spanning vectors, remember that $v$, $av$, $bv$ span the same line.

Aufgabe 23 · Orthogonal Latin squares and a card puzzle

A Latin square of order 4 is a $4\times4$ array in which each of $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta$ occurs exactly once in every row and every column. Two Latin squares $Q,R$ are orthogonal if, writing their entries side by side, all 16 pairs are different.

(a) Find three pairwise orthogonal Latin squares of order 4. Official hint: take three suitable lines through $0$ in $\mathbb F_4^2$; label the points of the line with $\alpha$ and the points of its three translates with $\beta,\gamma,\delta$.

(b) Arrange the 4 jacks (Bauern), 4 queens (Damen), 4 kings (Könige) and 4 aces (Asse) of a Jass deck in a $4\times4$ square so that every row and every column contains all four values and all four suits $\clubsuit,\diamondsuit,\heartsuit,\spadesuit$.

In short: Each slanted line class of $\mathbb F_4^2$ gives a Latin square. Two different line classes give orthogonal squares, because two non-parallel lines meet in exactly one point.

The idea

Put the 16 cells of the square on the 16 points of $\mathbb F_4^2$: column $x$, row $y$. Take a slope $m\in\{1,a,b\}$ and colour the 4 parallel lines $y=mx+q$ with four letters. Every row ($y$ fixed) meets each slanted line exactly once, and so does every column, so the colouring is a Latin square. For two different slopes, a line of one class and a line of the other meet in exactly one point: that is orthogonality. (b) is (a) with values and suits as the letters.

You need

Aufgabe 22 (the lines of $\mathbb F_4^2$ and their translates). Two different lines through $0$ meet only in $0$ (lesson 8, section 2).

Hint 1

For $m\in\{1,a,b\}$ write in cell $(x,y)$ the letter $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta$ according to $q=y-mx\in\{0,1,a,b\}$. Why is every row and every column a permutation of the letters?

Hint 2

Orthogonality: let $A$ be a translate of $g_i$ and $A'$ a translate of $g_j$, $i\ne j$. Show $|A\cap A'|=1$: at most one (the difference of two common points lies in $g_i\cap g_j=\{0\}$), and at least one (the 4 points of $A$ lie in 4 different translates of $g_j$).

Solution

Cells: column $x$, row $y$ (rows listed from $y=b$ at the top to $y=0$ at the bottom). In $Q_i$, cell $(x,y)$ gets $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta$ according to $q=y-m_ix=0,1,a,b$, with slopes $m_1=1$, $m_2=a$, $m_3=b$.

Why this stepWhat you write

Each square is well defined: every point lies in exactly one translate of the line.

(a) For a line $g_i=\{(x,y):y=m_ix\}$ with $m_i\in\{1,a,b\}$, its translates are $h_{i,q}=\{(x,y):y=m_ix+q\}$, $q\in\mathbb F_4$ (Aufgabe 22). Every point lies in exactly one of them, so $Q_i$ has exactly one letter in each cell.

Latin: a row ($y$ fixed) or a column ($x$ fixed) meets each slanted line exactly once.

In row $y$, the cell $x$ has letter $q=y-m_ix$, and $x\mapsto y-m_ix$ is a bijection of $\mathbb F_4$ since $m_i\ne0$. In column $x$, $y\mapsto y-m_ix$ is a bijection. So each letter occurs once per row and once per column.

Orthogonal: two non-parallel lines meet in exactly one point. At most one:

Let $i\ne j$, $A$ a translate of $g_i$, $A'$ a translate of $g_j$. First $g_i\cap g_j=\{0\}$: a common $z\ne0$ would give $g_i=\langle z\rangle=g_j$. If $p,p'\in A\cap A'$, then $p-p'\in g_i\cap g_j=\{0\}$, so $p=p'$.

At least one: the 4 points of $A$ fall into the 4 translates of $g_j$, one each.

By the previous step, no two points of $A$ lie in the same translate of $g_j$. So the 4 points of $A$ lie in 4 different translates of $g_j$, i.e. in all of them, in particular in $A'$. So $|A\cap A'|=1$: each pair of letters (one from $Q_i$, one from $Q_j$) occurs in exactly one cell. $Q_1,Q_2,Q_3$ are pairwise orthogonal.

(b) Use $Q_1$ for the values and $Q_3$ for the suits.

(b) Values from $Q_1$ ($\alpha$ = jack J, $\beta$ = queen Q, $\gamma$ = king K, $\delta$ = ace A) and suits from $Q_3$ ($\alpha=\clubsuit$, $\beta=\diamondsuit$, $\gamma=\heartsuit$, $\delta=\spadesuit$). Since $Q_1,Q_3$ are Latin, every row and column has all values and all suits; since they are orthogonal, all 16 cards are different. ∎

One solution of (b), written out by StudyHub (the official solution gives the method; the table is not in our copy). Check any row or column: four values, four suits.

For wonder (official remark): Euler asked whether 36 officers from six regiments and six ranks can stand in a $6\times6$ square with every regiment and every rank once per row and column, i.e. two orthogonal Latin squares of order 6. He found none and guessed none exist. That is true for 6, but it was proved only much later that orthogonal Latin squares exist for every order except 2 and 6.

Trap

Using the horizontal lines ($m=0$) or the vertical lines: their colourings are constant along rows or columns, so they are not Latin squares. Only the three slanted classes $m=1,a,b$ work. And in (b), checking rows and columns but not that the 16 cards are all different (orthogonality).
