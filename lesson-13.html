Recap of lesson 12 (5 min)

  • Bolzano–Weierstrass: every bounded real sequence has a convergent subsequence; $\limsup$ and $\liminf$ are its largest and smallest accumulation points.
  • $a_n\to\infty$: for every $K>0$ there is $N$ with $a_n\ge K$ for $n\ge N$. Increasing and unbounded ⇒ $a_n\to\infty$.
  • Cauchy sequence: $$\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall m,n\ge N:\ |a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon.$$
  • Cauchy criterion: a real sequence converges ⇔ it is Cauchy. “⇐”: bounded, Bolzano–Weierstrass, the whole sequence follows its subsequence (two ε/2's and $k=\max\{N,K\}$).

  1. Is every bounded sequence convergent? Does it have a convergent subsequence?

    Answer

    Not convergent: $(-1)^n$. But it always has a convergent subsequence (Bolzano–Weierstrass).

  2. For $a_n=\sqrt n$, $|a_{n+1}-a_n|\to0$. Why isn't it Cauchy?

    Answer

    The Cauchy condition needs all pairs $m,n\ge N$ close, not just neighbours. $\sqrt{4N}-\sqrt N=\sqrt N\ge1$, so with $\varepsilon=1$ no $N$ works.

  3. In the Cauchy criterion, why is $n_k\ge N$ for $k=\max\{N,K\}$?

    Answer

    Subsequence indices satisfy $n_k\ge k$, and $k\ge N$.

1. The story: approaching the gate

The castle gate is enchanted: whatever stands in the gateway may be an illusion, or there may be nothing there at all. The king's surveyors don't care. They ask a different question: as a traveller walks closer and closer to the gate, never stepping into it, what height does the road settle at? That's the limit at the gate. If the road's height at the gate itself agrees with the height it settles at, the road is continuous there. If the road settles at one height but the gate shows another, the illusion can be removed: just repaint the gate. And sometimes the road from the west settles at one height and the road from the east at another: a jump, and no repainting can fix it.

The king's tax collectors measure a field differently from lesson 9's tilers. They cut the field into strips, walk into each strip, pick any spot they like, measure the height there, and multiply by the strip's width. Their total is a Riemann sum. Cheating is pointless: once the strips are thin enough, every possible choice of spots gives nearly the true area.

And the royal astronomer, who deals with huge and tiny numbers, never says exactly how big something is, only “of the order of”. That's Landau's notation.

In maths: the gate is the point $x_0$, the road's height is $f(x)$, “never stepping into it” is the punctured neighbourhood $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$, the strips are a partition, the spots are tags $z_k$, and “of the order of” is $O(\cdot)$.

2. Limits of functions

Definition (p. 274). Let $D\subseteq\mathbb R$, $f:D\to\mathbb R$, and $x_0\in\mathbb R$ an accumulation point of $D$ (every punctured interval $(x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta)\setminus\{x_0\}$ contains points of $D$). Then $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=A$ means

$$\begin{aligned}&\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x\in D:\\&\quad 0<|x-x_0|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-A|<\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$
  • $x_0$ itself is excluded ($0<|x-x_0|$). The value $f(x_0)$ plays no role, and $f$ need not even be defined at $x_0$. The set $(x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta)\setminus\{x_0\}$ is the punctured δ-neighbourhood.
  • Why an accumulation point: so that there are points to approach with. Then the limit is unique if it exists (Übung 6.38 (i), the Bonus).
  • Rules (as for sequences): limits of sums, multiples and products are the sums, multiples and products of the limits; $f\le g$ gives $\lim f\le\lim g$; and there's a sandwich lemma (Exercise 5).

Lemma 6.39 (p. 275). For $x_0\in D$ an accumulation point of $D$: $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ ⇔ $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=f(x_0)$.

If $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)$ exists but differs from $f(x_0)$, then $x_0$ is a removable discontinuity: redefining $f(x_0)$ as the limit makes $f$ continuous there. If $x_0\notin D$ and the limit exists, setting $f(x_0)=\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)$ extends $f$ continuously.

Example. $f(x)=\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}$ on $D=\mathbb R\setminus\{2\}$. For $x\neq2$, $f(x)=x+2$, so $\lim_{x\to2}f(x)=4$: with $\delta=\varepsilon$, $0<|x-2|<\delta$ gives $|f(x)-4|=|x-2|<\varepsilon$. Setting $f(2)=4$ extends $f$ continuously.

Proof A: limits through sequences (Lemma 6.40, p. 275)

Lemma 6.40. Let $x_0$ be an accumulation point of $D$. Then $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=A$ ⇔ for every sequence $(a_n)_n$ in $D\setminus\{x_0\}$ with $a_n\to x_0$ we have $f(a_n)\to A$.

You'll need these (lesson 10)
  • Proof E of lesson 10 (ε-δ continuity ⇔ sequential continuity). This proof is the same with $A$ in place of $f(x_0)$ and with $x_0$ excluded.
  • The negation of the limit statement: $$\begin{aligned}&\exists\varepsilon>0\ \forall\delta>0\ \exists x\in D:\\&\quad 0<|x-x_0|<\delta\ \text{and}\ |f(x)-A|\ge \varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

The idea. (⇒) If $f$ is within $\varepsilon$ of $A$ on a whole punctured neighbourhood, any sequence that eventually enters the neighbourhood (and never hits $x_0$) has images eventually within $\varepsilon$ of $A$. (⇐) Contrapositive: if the limit fails, for one bad $\varepsilon$ every punctured neighbourhood contains a bad point; choose one in radius $\frac1n$ for each $n$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • “⇒” starts “Let $(a_n)_n$ be in $D\setminus\{x_0\}$ with $a_n\to x_0$, and let $\varepsilon>0$”, and ends “$|f(a_n)-A|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$”.
  • “⇐” by contrapositive: assume the limit statement fails, and build a sequence for which $f(a_n)\not\to A$.
The storyWhat you write

“⇒” Near the gate, the road is within $\varepsilon$ of its settling height.

Let $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=A$, let $(a_n)_n$ be in $D\setminus\{x_0\}$ with $a_n\to x_0$, and let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)-A|<\varepsilon$ for all $x\in D$ with $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$.

The traveller eventually comes that near, and never stands in the gate.

Since $a_n\to x_0$, there is $N$ with $|a_n-x_0|<\delta$ for $n\ge N$; and $a_n\neq x_0$. So for $n\ge N$: $0<|a_n-x_0|<\delta$, hence $|f(a_n)-A|<\varepsilon$. So $f(a_n)\to A$.

“⇐” Suppose the road doesn't settle at $A$: for some tolerance, bad spots exist arbitrarily close to the gate.

Suppose $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=A$ fails. Then there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that for every $\delta>0$ there is $x\in D$ with $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$ and $|f(x)-A|\ge\varepsilon$.

Pick one bad spot within $1$, one within $\frac12$, one within $\frac13$…

For each $n$, with $\delta=\frac1n$, choose $a_n\in D$ with $0<|a_n-x_0|<\frac1n$ and $|f(a_n)-A|\ge\varepsilon$.

That traveller approaches the gate, but the road under him never comes near $A$.

Then $(a_n)_n$ lies in $D\setminus\{x_0\}$, $a_n\to x_0$, but $f(a_n)\not\to A$. This contradicts the sequence condition, which proves “⇐”. ∎

Moves used: use the limit with a chosen ε the negation recipe δ = 1/n builds a sequence

The proof in one piece

“⇒”: Let $(a_n)_n$ be in $D\setminus\{x_0\}$ with $a_n\to x_0$, and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)-A|<\varepsilon$ whenever $x\in D$, $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$, and $N$ with $|a_n-x_0|<\delta$ for $n\ge N$. As $a_n\neq x_0$, $|f(a_n)-A|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$.

“⇐”: If $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=A$ fails, there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that for each $n$ some $a_n\in D$ satisfies $0<|a_n-x_0|<\frac1n$ and $|f(a_n)-A|\ge\varepsilon$. Then $a_n\to x_0$ in $D\setminus\{x_0\}$ but $f(a_n)\not\to A$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Where in “⇒” do we use that $a_n\neq x_0$?

    Answer

    The δ-statement only covers $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$. Without $a_n\neq x_0$ we couldn't apply it to $a_n=x_0$, where $f$ may have any value (or none).

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). What is different from lesson 10's Proof E? Name two changes.

    Answer

    $A$ replaces $f(x_0)$, and $x_0$ is excluded: the sequences live in $D\setminus\{x_0\}$, and the bad points satisfy $0<|a_n-x_0|$.

  3. Rung 3 (4 min). Write both directions from the frame alone.

Use it to show a limit does not exist: find two sequences $a_n,b_n\to x_0$ (avoiding $x_0$) with different limits of $f(a_n)$ and $f(b_n)$. For the sign function at $0$: $f\left(\frac1n\right)=1$ but $f\left(-\frac1n\right)=-1$, so $\lim_{x\to0}\operatorname{sgn}(x)$ does not exist.

Proposition 6.41 (p. 276), composition. If $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=y_0$ and $g$ is continuous at $y_0$, then $\lim_{x\to x_0}g(f(x))=g(y_0)$. Why: for $a_n\to x_0$ in $D\setminus\{x_0\}$, Lemma 6.40 gives $f(a_n)\to y_0$, continuity gives $g(f(a_n))\to g(y_0)$, and Lemma 6.40 again gives the claim.

Improper limits. $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=+\infty$ means: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ with $f(x)>\frac1\varepsilon$ for all $x\in D$ with $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$. Similarly for $-\infty$.

3. One-sided limits, one-sided continuity, jumps

Definition (p. 276). $x_0$ is a right-sided accumulation point of $D$ if $D\cap(x_0,x_0+\delta)\neq\emptyset$ for every $\delta>0$. Then the right-sided limit $\lim_{x\searrow x_0}f(x)=A$ (also written $\lim_{x\to x_0^+}f(x)$) means

$$\begin{aligned}&\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x\in D\cap(x_0,x_0+\delta):\\&\quad |f(x)-A|<\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

The left-sided limit $\lim_{x\nearrow x_0}f(x)$ uses $D\cap(x_0-\delta,x_0)$ instead.

For $x_0\in D$: $f$ is right-continuous at $x_0$ if $\lim_{x\searrow x_0}f(x)=f(x_0)$, and left-continuous if $\lim_{x\nearrow x_0}f(x)=f(x_0)$. If both one-sided limits exist but are different, $x_0$ is a jump.

  • Both sides agree ⇔ the limit exists. If $x_0$ is a left- and right-sided accumulation point, then $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)$ exists ⇔ both one-sided limits exist and are equal. So for $x_0\in D$: continuous at $x_0$ ⇔ left- and right-continuous there.
  • Only one side available. If $D$ has no points just right of $x_0$ (for example $x_0=b$ for $D=[a,b]$), the right-sided limit isn't defined, and the left-sided limit alone decides: $\lim_{x\to b}f(x)=\lim_{x\nearrow b}f(x)$.
  • Examples. $\lim_{x\searrow0}\frac1x=+\infty$ and $\lim_{x\nearrow0}\frac1x=-\infty$. $\lim_{x\searrow0}\sqrt x=\lim_{x\to0}\sqrt x=0$ (on $D=[0,\infty)$, only the right side exists). The sign function has a jump at $0$: left limit $-1$, right limit $1$.

4. Limits at infinity

The idea. $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=A$ says: far enough to the right, the graph stays as close to the height $A$ as you like. Draw a horizontal band of half-width $\varepsilon$ around the line $y=A$. However thin the band, there is a point $R$ from which on the graph never leaves it again. This is the definition of $a_n\to A$ for sequences with one swap: the whole numbers $n\ge N$ become the real numbers $x>R$.

Definition (p. 278). Let $D$ be not bounded above.

  • $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=A$ means: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $R$ with $|f(x)-A|<\varepsilon$ for all $x\in D$ with $x>R$.
  • $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=\infty$ means: for every $K$ there is $R$ with $f(x)>K$ for all $x\in D$ with $x>R$.

(The script writes $R=\frac1\delta$, which turns this into a “$\delta$” definition. It says the same thing.) $x\to-\infty$ works the same way with “$x<-R$”.

What the definition forces
  • First line: “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” Next: “Let $R=\dots$” (to be found). Last line: “so $|f(x)-A|<\varepsilon$ for all $x>R$.”
  • The gap: bound $|f(x)-A|$ by something that shrinks as $x$ grows, then choose $R$ large enough.

Worked example: $\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1x=0$

  1. What has to become small?

    Answer

    $\left|\frac1x-0\right|=\frac1x$ (for $x>0$).

  2. For which $x$ is $\frac1x<\varepsilon$? Which $R$ does that suggest?

    Answer

    Exactly for $x>\frac1\varepsilon$. So $R=\frac1\varepsilon$ works.

  3. Write the proof down.

    Answer

    Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $R=\frac1\varepsilon$. For all $x>R$: $\left|\frac1x-0\right|=\frac1x<\frac1R=\varepsilon$. Hence $\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1x=0$. In the same way, $\frac c{x^m}\to0$ for every constant $c$ and every $m\ge1$.

Rational functions at $\infty$ (Übung 6.45). The trick: divide the numerator and the denominator by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator. Then every term of the form $\frac c{x^m}$ tends to $0$ (worked example above), and the limit can be read off with the limit rules.

The three cases, one example each.

  • Same degree: $\frac{3x^2+x}{x^2-5}=\frac{3+\frac1x}{1-\frac5{x^2}}\to\frac{3+0}{1-0}=3$. The limit is the ratio of the leading coefficients.
  • Denominator has the higher degree: $\frac{x}{x^2+1}=\frac{\frac1x}{1+\frac1{x^2}}\to\frac01=0$.
  • Numerator has the higher degree: $\frac{x^2}{x+1}=\frac{x}{1+\frac1x}$. The denominator tends to $1$ and the numerator to $\infty$, so the quotient tends to $\infty$.

In general, for polynomials $p,q$ with leading terms $a_kx^k$ and $b_\ell x^\ell$: $\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ is $\frac{a_k}{b_\ell}$ if $k=\ell$, it is $0$ if $k<\ell$, and it is $\pm\infty$ (the sign of $\frac{a_k}{b_\ell}$) if $k>\ell$. Also $\lim_{x\to\infty}p(x)=\pm\infty$ (the sign of $a_k$) for $k\ge1$.

5. Riemann sums

Definition 6.46 (p. 280). For a partition $Z=\{a=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_n=b\}$ of $[a,b]$, the mesh is $|Z|=\max_k(x_k-x_{k-1})$, the width of the widest piece. Tags are points $z_k\in[x_{k-1},x_k]$, one per piece. The Riemann sum is

$$R(f,Z,z)=\sum_{k=1}^nf(z_k)\,(x_k-x_{k-1}).$$

Satz 6.47 (p. 280). If $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$, then the Riemann sums converge to the integral as the mesh tends to $0$:

$$\begin{aligned}&\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall Z,z:\\&\quad |Z|<\delta\Rightarrow\left|R(f,Z,z)-\int_a^bf\,dx\right|<\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

The everyday form. Take $n$ equal pieces of width $\frac{b-a}n$ and right endpoints as tags: $z_k=a+k\frac{b-a}n$. The mesh is $\frac{b-a}n\to0$, so for integrable $f$:

$$\begin{aligned}&\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{b-a}n\sum_{k=1}^nf\left(a+k\,\frac{b-a}n\right)=\int_a^bf(x)\,dx.\\&\text{On }[0,1]:\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^nf\left(\frac kn\right)=\int_0^1f(x)\,dx\end{aligned}.$$

That's how exam questions use it: you recognise a sum as a Riemann sum and replace its limit by an integral.

Proof B: Riemann sums converge, for continuous $f$ (Satz 6.47)

You'll need these (lessons 8 and 9)
  • Heine: a continuous function on $[a,b]$ is uniformly continuous: for every $\eta>0$ there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)-f(y)|<\eta$ whenever $|x-y|<\delta$.
  • Additivity over intervals: $\int_a^bf=\sum_k\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}f$. Constants: $\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}c\,dx=c\,(x_k-x_{k-1})$.
  • Triangle inequality and monotonicity: $\left|\int g\right|\le\int|g|$, and $|g|\le c$ gives $\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}|g|\le c\,(x_k-x_{k-1})$.

The idea. On a thin piece, a continuous function barely changes (Heine), so $f(x)$ is close to the tag value $f(z_k)$ everywhere on the piece. Then the true area of the strip and the rectangle differ by at most “small height × width”. Add up the strips: the total error is at most “small height × $(b-a)$”.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” Then we must produce $\delta$, and only then take an arbitrary $Z$ with $|Z|<\delta$ and arbitrary tags $z$.
  • Last line: “so $\left|\int_a^bf-R(f,Z,z)\right|<\varepsilon$.”
  • The gap: compare piece by piece: $\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}f$ versus $f(z_k)(x_k-x_{k-1})$. Aim for an error of $\eta\cdot$width per piece with $\eta(b-a)<\varepsilon$.
The storyWhat you write

Fix a tolerance, and make the strips so thin that the height barely changes within one.

Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $\eta=\frac\varepsilon{2(b-a)}$. By Heine, $f$ is uniformly continuous, so there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)-f(y)|<\eta$ for all $x,y\in[a,b]$ with $|x-y|<\delta$.

Now any cut into thin strips, and any choice of spots.

Let $Z$ be a partition with $|Z|<\delta$ and $z$ tags. For $x\in[x_{k-1},x_k]$ we have $|x-z_k|\le x_k-x_{k-1}<\delta$, so $|f(x)-f(z_k)|<\eta$.

On one strip, the true area and the rectangle differ by at most “small height × width”.

$$\begin{aligned}\left|\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}f\,dx-f(z_k)(x_k-x_{k-1})\right|&=\left|\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}\bigl(f(x)-f(z_k)\bigr)dx\right|\\&\le\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}|f(x)-f(z_k)|\,dx\le\eta\,(x_k-x_{k-1}).\end{aligned}$$

Add up the strips; the widths add up to the whole field.

By additivity and the triangle inequality, $$\begin{aligned}\left|\int_a^bf\,dx-R(f,Z,z)\right|&\le \sum_{k=1}^n\eta\,(x_k-x_{k-1})\\&=\eta\,(b-a)\\&=\tfrac\varepsilon2\\&<\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$ ∎

Moves used: uniform continuity with a chosen tolerance estimate piece by piece the widths add up

The proof in one piece

Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and $\varepsilon>0$. By Heine there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)-f(y)|<\eta=\frac\varepsilon{2(b-a)}$ whenever $|x-y|<\delta$. Let $|Z|<\delta$ and let $z$ be tags. For $x\in[x_{k-1},x_k]$, $|x-z_k|<\delta$, so $|f(x)-f(z_k)|<\eta$, and therefore $$\begin{aligned}&\left|\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}f\,dx-f(z_k)(x_k-x_{k-1})\right|\\&\quad\le\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}|f(x)-f(z_k)|\,dx\le\eta(x_k-x_{k-1}).\end{aligned}$$ Summing over $k$ and using additivity: $$\left|\int_a^bf\,dx-R(f,Z,z)\right|\le\eta(b-a)=\frac\varepsilon2<\varepsilon.$$∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Heine with $\eta=\frac\varepsilon{2(b-a)}$ gives $\delta$.
  2. $|Z|<\delta$ ⇒ $|f(x)-f(z_k)|<\eta$ on each piece.
  3. Per piece: $\left|\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}(f(x)-f(z_k))\,dx\right|\le\eta\,\Delta x_k$.
  4. Sum: total error $\le\eta(b-a)<\varepsilon$.

In short: “Heine, per piece error η·width, the widths sum to b − a”. It's lesson 9's “continuous ⇒ integrable” estimate in new clothes.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why do we need uniform continuity, not just continuity at each point?

    Answer

    One $\delta$ must work for every piece of every partition with $|Z|<\delta$, wherever it lies in $[a,b]$. Pointwise continuity gives a $\delta$ depending on the point.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the per-piece estimate, naming each fact you use.

    Answer

    $$\begin{aligned}\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}f\,dx-f(z_k)\Delta x_k&=\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}(f(x)-f(z_k))\,dx\end{aligned}$$ (linearity and the integral of a constant); its absolute value is $\le\int|f(x)-f(z_k)|\,dx$ (triangle inequality) $\le\eta\,\Delta x_k$ (monotonicity, since the integrand is $<\eta$).

  3. Rung 3 (4 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

For a general integrable $f$, the script squeezes $f$ between continuous functions $f_-\le f\le f_+$ with $\int(f_+-f_-)<\frac\varepsilon2$ (Proposition 4.44) and applies the continuous case to both. That part is on the skip list.

6. Landau notation: $O$ and $o$

The idea. Often we don't care what a function is exactly, only how big it is compared to a simpler function $g$, near one point $x_0$ (often $0$ or $\infty$). Landau notation (like the royal astronomer's “of the order of”) has two words for this:

  • $f=O(g)$, “big O”: $f$ is at most about as big as $g$. Near $x_0$, $|f|$ is at most a fixed multiple $M$ of $|g|$.
  • $f=o(g)$, “little o”: $f$ is negligible compared to $g$. Near $x_0$, $|f|$ is smaller than any fraction $\varepsilon$ of $|g|$, however tiny.

The practical test: look at the ratio $\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ as $x\to x_0$ (when $g\neq0$ near $x_0$).

  • The ratio stays bounded ⇔ $f=O(g)$.
  • The ratio tends to $0$ ⇔ $f=o(g)$.
  • The ratio tends to a number $c\neq0$: then $f=O(g)$ but not $o(g)$ (same order).
  • The ratio is unbounded: $f$ is neither $O(g)$ nor $o(g)$.

Since “tends to $0$” implies “bounded near $x_0$”, every $o(g)$ is also $O(g)$.

Definition (p. 284). Let $x_0$ be an accumulation point of $D$ (or $x_0=\pm\infty$), and $f,g:D\to\mathbb R$.

  • $f(x)=O(g(x))$ for $x\to x_0$ if there are $\delta>0$ and $M>0$ with $|f(x)|\le M|g(x)|$ for all $x\in D$ with $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$.
  • $f(x)=o(g(x))$ for $x\to x_0$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)|\le\varepsilon|g(x)|$ for all $x\in D$ with $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$.

For $x_0=\infty$, replace “$0<|x-x_0|<\delta$” by “$x>R$”. The difference between the two: in $O$, one constant $M$ is enough; in $o$, it must work for every $\varepsilon>0$.

Careful with “=”. “$f=O(g)$” is not an equation; read it as “$f$ is in the class $O(g)$”. It only goes one way: $x=O(x^2)$ for $x\to\infty$ is true, but “$O(x^2)=x$” means nothing.

Worked example: compare $x^2$ and $x$

  1. Compute the ratio $\frac{x^2}{x}$. Is $x^2=o(x)$ for $x\to0$?

    Answer

    $\frac{x^2}x=x\to0$ for $x\to0$. So yes: $x^2=o(x)$, and hence also $x^2=O(x)$ for $x\to0$. Near $0$, $x^2$ is much smaller than $x$ (for $x=0.01$: $0.0001$ versus $0.01$).

  2. And for $x\to\infty$?

    Answer

    $\frac{x^2}x=x\to\infty$ is unbounded. So $x^2\neq O(x)$ for $x\to\infty$. The other way round, $\frac{x}{x^2}=\frac1x\to0$, so $x=o(x^2)$ for $x\to\infty$.

  3. What does this example teach?

    Answer

    The point $x_0$ matters. Near $0$, higher powers are smaller; near $\infty$, higher powers are bigger. Always say “for $x\to x_0$”.

Worked example: $O(1)$ and $o(1)$

  1. Take $g=1$. What do $f=O(1)$ and $f=o(1)$ mean?

    Answer

    The ratio is $\frac f1=f$ itself. So $f=O(1)$ means $f$ is bounded near $x_0$, and $f=o(1)$ means $f(x)\to0$.

  2. Is $\frac x{x+1}=O(1)$ for $x\to\infty$? Is it $o(1)$?

    Answer

    $\frac x{x+1}=\frac1{1+\frac1x}\to1$. It is bounded (between $0$ and $1$ for $x>0$), so $O(1)$. It does not tend to $0$, so it is not $o(1)$.

As a placeholder. $o(g)$ and $O(g)$ are also used inside formulas, for “some function of that size, which we don't write out”. So $f(x)=h(x)+o(g(x))$ means $f(x)-h(x)=o(g(x))$: $h$ is the main part, and the error is negligible compared to $g$.

Worked example: a main part plus an error

  1. Polynomial division gives $\frac{x^2+1}{x-1}=x+1+\frac2{x-1}$. What size is the remainder $\frac2{x-1}$ for $x\to\infty$?

    Answer

    $\frac2{x-1}\to0$, so it is $o(1)$.

  2. Write the result in Landau notation and say what it means.

    Answer

    $\frac{x^2+1}{x-1}=x+1+o(1)$ for $x\to\infty$: for large $x$, the function is $x+1$ up to an error that tends to $0$. (Its graph approaches the line $y=x+1$.)

Rules (Übung 6.53). $o(g)+o(g)=o(g)$ and $\alpha\,o(g)=o(g)$ for a constant $\alpha$, and the same for $O$. (Reason: if $\frac{f_1}g\to0$ and $\frac{f_2}g\to0$, then $\frac{f_1+f_2}g\to0$ and $\frac{\alpha f_1}g\to0$.) Why you'll need this: Taylor expansions later are written this way, for example $\sqrt{1+s}=1+\frac s2+O(s^2)$ for $s\to0$. In this lesson it appears in the worked examples (section 7) and in an exam multiple-choice question (Exercise 2).

7. Worked examples

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

Worked example 1: $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt{1+x}-1}x$

  1. What happens if you plug in $x=0$? Is that allowed?

    Answer

    You get $\frac00$, which is meaningless, and $x=0$ isn't in the domain anyway. The limit only looks at $x\neq0$ near $0$ (here $x\ge-1$), so we may transform the expression for $x\neq0$.

  2. Multiply numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{1+x}+1$. What do you get for $x\neq0$?

    Answer

    $\frac{(1+x)-1}{x\left(\sqrt{1+x}+1\right)}=\frac1{\sqrt{1+x}+1}$.

  3. Finish the computation, and name the facts you use.

    Answer

    $x\mapsto\frac1{\sqrt{1+x}+1}$ is continuous at $0$ (the root is continuous by lesson 8, then sums and quotients with nonzero denominator), so by Lemma 6.39 its limit at $0$ is its value $\frac12$. Since the two functions agree for $x\neq0$, $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt{1+x}-1}x=\frac12$.

  4. Rewrite the result in Landau notation.

    Answer

    $\frac{\sqrt{1+x}-1}x-\frac12\to0$, i.e. $\sqrt{1+x}-1-\frac x2=o(x)$, i.e. $\sqrt{1+x}=1+\frac x2+o(x)$ for $x\to0$.

Worked example 2: a limit of sums as an integral

Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{k^2}{n^3}$.

  1. Rewrite the sum in the form $\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^nf\left(\frac kn\right)$.

    Answer

    $\frac{k^2}{n^3}=\frac1n\left(\frac kn\right)^2$, so $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,1]$.

  2. Why does Satz 6.47 apply, and what is the limit?

    Answer

    $f(x)=x^2$ is continuous, hence integrable, and the sum is the Riemann sum for $n$ equal pieces with right endpoints as tags; the mesh $\frac1n\to0$. So the limit is $\int_0^1x^2\,dx=\frac13$ (lesson 9, Exercise 3).

Check without integrals: $\sum_{k=1}^nk^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6$, so the sum is $\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}\to\frac26=\frac13$. The Riemann-sum view is faster and works when no formula exists.

Worked example 3: big O and little o

Let $f(x)=3x+x^2$.

  • For $x\to0$: $f(x)=O(x)$, since $|3x+x^2|\le|x|(3+|x|)\le4|x|$ for $0<|x|<1$ ($M=4$, $\delta=1$). More precisely, $f(x)=3x+o(x)$, since $\frac{x^2}x=x\to0$.
  • For $x\to\infty$: $f(x)=O(x^2)$, since $\frac{3x+x^2}{x^2}=\frac3x+1\le4$ for $x>1$. But $f(x)\neq o(x^2)$, since $\frac{f(x)}{x^2}\to1\neq0$; and $f(x)\neq O(x)$, since $\frac{f(x)}x=3+x$ is unbounded.

Why it's cool. Riemann sums turn hard limits of sums into easy integrals (Worked example 2), and $O$ and $o$ let you compute limits by keeping the main term and throwing away the rest. That bookkeeping is the whole idea behind Taylor expansions (lesson 21).

Exercise 1 (Einsiedler HS21 mock exam, question 10, true or false)

Open the original: mock exam HS21

Let $D\subseteq\mathbb R$, $x_0\in D$ an accumulation point of $D$, and $f:D\to\mathbb R$. In which cases does it follow that $f$ is continuous at $x_0$?

  • (a) If $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)$ exists, then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.
  • (b) If $x_0$ is a left- and right-sided accumulation point of $D$ and $f$ is left- and right-continuous at $x_0$, then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.
  • (c) If $x_0$ is not a right-sided accumulation point of $D$ and $\lim_{x\nearrow x_0}f(x)=f(x_0)$, then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.
  • (d) If there is a sequence $x_n\in D$ with $x_n\to x_0$ and $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$, then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.
Solution
  • (a) False. $f(x)=0$ for $x\neq0$ and $f(0)=1$ on $D=\mathbb R$: $\lim_{x\to0}f(x)=0$ exists but differs from $f(0)$ (a removable discontinuity). Continuity at an accumulation point means exactly that the limit exists and equals $f(x_0)$.
  • (b) True. Both one-sided limits exist and equal $f(x_0)$, so $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=f(x_0)$, and $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ (continuity at an accumulation point means $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=f(x_0)$).
  • (c) True. Since $x_0$ is not a right-sided accumulation point, there is $\delta_0>0$ with $D\cap(x_0,x_0+\delta_0)=\emptyset$. For $\delta\le\delta_0$, the points $x\in D$ with $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$ all lie left of $x_0$. So the left-sided limit is the limit: $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=f(x_0)$, and $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.
  • (d) False. One sequence is not enough; continuity needs every sequence. Example: $f(x)=0$ for $x\le0$, $f(x)=1$ for $x>0$, $x_0=0$, $x_n=-\frac1n$: $f(x_n)=0=f(0)$, but $f\left(\frac1n\right)=1\not\to0$, so $f$ is not continuous at $0$.

Exercise 2 (Figalli FS26, MC11)

Open the original: FS26 exam

Which asymptotic statement is true for $x\to\infty$? (A) $x^2\cos x=O(x)$. (B) $\frac{\sin x}x=o\left(\frac1{x^2}\right)$. (C) $\sin x=o\left(\frac1{\log x}\right)$. (D) $\cos x=O\left((\log x)^2\right)$.

Solution

Answer (D). $|\cos x|\le1\le(\log x)^2$ for $x\ge e$, so $M=1$ and $R=e$ work.

  • (A) False: $\frac{x^2\cos x}x=x\cos x$ is unbounded (at $x=2\pi m$ it equals $2\pi m$).
  • (B) False: $\frac{\sin x/x}{1/x^2}=x\sin x$ does not tend to $0$ (at $x=\frac\pi2+2\pi m$ it equals $x$).
  • (C) False: $\frac{\sin x}{1/\log x}=\sin x\log x$ does not tend to $0$ (at $x=\frac\pi2+2\pi m$ it equals $\log x\to\infty$).

Exercise 3 (Felder FS23, 1.A5, 2 points)

Open the original: FS23 exam with solutions

Compute the limit of Riemann sums $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n\sin\left(\frac kn\right)$. (Write it as an integral. To evaluate it, use that $-\cos$ is an antiderivative of $\sin$, which you know from school; lesson 19 justifies it.)

Solution

The sum is the Riemann sum of $f(x)=\sin x$ on $[0,1]$ with $n$ equal pieces and right endpoints $z_k=\frac kn$ as tags. The mesh is $\frac1n\to0$ and $\sin$ is continuous, so the Riemann sums converge to the integral:

$$\begin{aligned}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n\sin\left(\frac kn\right)&=\int_0^1\sin x\,dx\\&=\bigl[-\cos x\bigr]_0^1\\&=1-\cos1\end{aligned}.$$

Exercise 4 (computing limits)

(a) $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^3-x^2-x-2}{x-2}$ (Übung 6.45). (b) $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x^2-x}{5x^2+3}$. (c) The one-sided limits of $\frac{|x|}x$ at $0$; does $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{|x|}x$ exist? (d) $\displaystyle\lim_{x\searrow0}\left(\frac1x-\frac1{x^2}\right)$.

Solution

(a) $7$. $x=2$ is a root of the numerator ($8-4-2-2=0$), and polynomial division gives $$x^3-x^2-x-2=(x-2)(x^2+x+1).$$For $x\neq2$ the quotient is $x^2+x+1$, which is continuous, so the limit is $4+2+1=7$.

(b) $\frac25$. $\frac{2x^2-x}{5x^2+3}=\frac{2-\frac1x}{5+\frac3{x^2}}\to\frac25$.

(c) For $x>0$, $\frac{|x|}x=1$; for $x<0$, it is $-1$. So $\lim_{x\searrow0}=1$ and $\lim_{x\nearrow0}=-1$. They differ, so $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{|x|}x$ does not exist (a jump, if we define a value at $0$).

(d) $-\infty$. Frame: “$\to-\infty$” means “Let $K>0$ … so the expression is $<-K$ for $0<x<\delta$”. $\frac1x-\frac1{x^2}=\frac{x-1}{x^2}$. For $0<x<\frac12$: $x-1<-\frac12$, so the expression is $<-\frac1{2x^2}$. Given $K>0$, take $\delta=\min\left\{\frac12,\frac1{\sqrt{2K}}\right\}$; for $0<x<\delta$, $\frac1{2x^2}>K$, so the expression is $<-K$.

Exercise 5 (sandwich for functions, Übung 6.38 (iii))

Let $x_0$ be an accumulation point of $D$ and $f\le g\le h$ on $D$, with $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=\lim_{x\to x_0}h(x)=A$. Prove that $\lim_{x\to x_0}g(x)=A$.

Solution

Frame: “Let $\varepsilon>0$ … so $|g(x)-A|<\varepsilon$ for $x\in D$, $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$”. The gap: one $\delta$ that works for $f$ and $h$ at once, then trap $g$.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $\delta_1,\delta_2>0$ with $|f(x)-A|<\varepsilon$ for $x\in D$, $0<|x-x_0|<\delta_1$, and $|h(x)-A|<\varepsilon$ for $x\in D$, $0<|x-x_0|<\delta_2$. Let $\delta=\min\{\delta_1,\delta_2\}$. For $x\in D$ with $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$:

$$A-\varepsilon<f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x)<A+\varepsilon,$$

so $|g(x)-A|<\varepsilon$. Hence $\lim_{x\to x_0}g(x)=A$. ∎

Moves used: minimum of two δs sandwich

Exercise 6 (Riemann sums)

Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n\left(1+\frac kn\right)$ in two ways: as an integral, and directly.

Solution

As an integral: it is the right-endpoint Riemann sum of $f(x)=1+x$ on $[0,1]$, so the limit is $\int_0^1(1+x)\,dx=1+\frac12=\frac32$ (linearity, and $\int_0^1x\,dx=\frac12$ from lesson 9).

Directly: $$\begin{aligned}\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n\left(1+\frac kn\right)&=1+\frac1{n^2}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}2\\&=1+\frac{n+1}{2n}\to\frac32.\end{aligned}$$

Bonus (Übung 6.38 (i))

Prove that $\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)$ is unique if it exists. Where do you use that $x_0$ is an accumulation point of $D$?

Solution

Frame: uniqueness by contradiction: “Suppose the limits $A\neq B$”, take $\varepsilon$ = half the distance, and find one $x$ close to both. Moves: assume the opposite, minimum of two δs.

Suppose $f(x)\to A$ and $f(x)\to B$ with $A\neq B$, and let $\varepsilon=\frac{|A-B|}2$. Choose $\delta_1,\delta_2$ for $A$ and $B$, and $\delta=\min\{\delta_1,\delta_2\}$. Since $x_0$ is an accumulation point of $D$, there is $x\in D$ with $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$. (This is where we use it: otherwise there might be no such $x$, and every number would be a “limit”.) Then $$\begin{aligned}|A-B|&\le |A-f(x)|+|f(x)-B|\\&<2\varepsilon\\&=|A-B|\end{aligned},$$ a contradiction. ∎

9. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Why mathematicians took 150 years to define a limit. Newton and Leibniz (around 1680) computed with “infinitely small” quantities and got correct answers, but couldn't say what those quantities were. Bishop Berkeley mocked them as “the ghosts of departed quantities”. The ε-δ definition you used today was finally written down by Bolzano (1817), Cauchy (1821) and, in today's precise form, Weierstrass (1860s). The trick that solved it is exactly the punctured neighbourhood: never look at the point, only near it.

Riemann sums run your computer's integrals. When software computes an area numerically, it adds up Riemann sums with a clever choice of tags. Choosing the midpoint of each piece instead of an endpoint makes the error shrink like $O\left(\frac1{n^2}\right)$ instead of $O\left(\frac1n\right)$ for smooth functions; Simpson's rule (weights $\frac16,\frac46,\frac16$ on each piece) gets $O\left(\frac1{n^4}\right)$. Landau's notation is how these speeds are compared.

A sum you'll be able to evaluate soon. $$\begin{aligned}\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1{n+k}&=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1{1+k/n}\to\int_0^1\frac{dx}{1+x}\\&=\log2\approx0.693\end{aligned}.$$ With logarithms (lesson 16) and the fundamental theorem (lesson 19), this will be a two-line computation.
