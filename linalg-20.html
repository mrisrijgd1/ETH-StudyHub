1. The story: the ledger turned sideways

The surveyors' measure of land exists, and they have two ways to compute it: tidy the rows of the ledger (lesson 18), or add up all the shuffled seatings (lesson 19). A young clerk turns a ledger sideways, so that its rows become columns, and asks: does the land change? It does not. Every seating of the turned ledger is the reverse seating of the original, and reversing a shuffle keeps its parity.

So everything the surveyors know about rows also holds for columns. That gives them a third way to compute: pick one row (or column), and split the land along it. Each entry of the row is multiplied by the land of the smaller ledger left when its row and column are struck out, with a chessboard of signs. Small ledgers inside large ones: a recursion.

Collecting all these smaller lands in one table gives the adjugate, and with it a formula for the inverse ledger and for the solution of any reversible system: Cramer's rule.

In mathematics: $\det A^T=\det A$; $\det A=\sum_{j}(-1)^{i+j}a_{ij}\det A_{ij}$ for every row $i$; and $A\,\mathrm{adj}(A)=\det A\cdot I_n$.

2. Rows and columns: the transpose and block matrices

Proof A: $\det A^T=\det A$ (script Theorem 4.3.25 and Corollary 4.3.26, p. 141)

Claim. For every $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$, $\det A^T=\det A$. Consequently the determinant is linear in each column, vanishes when two columns are equal, changes sign when two columns are swapped, and does not change when a multiple of one column is added to another.

The idea. In the Leibniz formula for $A^T$, the term for $\sigma$ is $\prod_ia_{\sigma(i)i}$. Reorder the factors by their row index: it becomes $\prod_ka_{k\sigma^{-1}(k)}$, the term of $\det A$ for $\sigma^{-1}$, with the same sign. As $\sigma$ runs through $S_n$, so does $\sigma^{-1}$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “By the Leibniz formula, $\det A^T=\sum_{\sigma}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\,a_{\sigma(1)1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n)n}$.”
  • Last line: “$=\sum_{\rho}\mathrm{sign}(\rho)\,a_{1\rho(1)}\cdots a_{n\rho(n)}=\det A$.”
  • The gap: reorder each product with $k=\sigma(i)$; $\mathrm{sign}(\sigma^{-1})=\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)$; reindex by $\rho=\sigma^{-1}$.
The storyWhat you write

A seating of the turned ledger picks the same entries as some seating of the original.

The $(i,j)$ entry of $A^T$ is $a_{ji}$, so by the Leibniz formula $\det A^T=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^na_{\sigma(i)i}$. Put $k=\sigma(i)$, i.e. $i=\sigma^{-1}(k)$; as $i$ runs through $1,\dots,n$, so does $k$. Hence $\prod_ia_{\sigma(i)i}=\prod_ka_{k\sigma^{-1}(k)}$.

It is the reverse seating, with the same parity.

$\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)=\mathrm{sign}(\sigma^{-1})$ (lesson 19, Proof C), so $\det A^T=\sum_{\sigma}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma^{-1})\prod_ka_{k\sigma^{-1}(k)}$. Since $\sigma\mapsto\sigma^{-1}$ is a bijection of $S_n$, substituting $\rho=\sigma^{-1}$ gives $\sum_\rho\mathrm{sign}(\rho)\prod_ka_{k\rho(k)}=\det A$.

So every rule for rows is a rule for columns.

The columns of $A$ are the rows of $A^T$. Each statement about columns of $A$ is the corresponding row statement (lesson 18, (D1), (D2), Proof A) applied to $A^T$, together with $\det A^T=\det A$. ∎

Moves used: reindex by a bijection

The proof in one piece

$\det A^T=\sum_\sigma\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\prod_ia_{\sigma(i)i}$. Reordering each product by $k=\sigma(i)$ gives $\prod_ka_{k\sigma^{-1}(k)}$, and $\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)=\mathrm{sign}(\sigma^{-1})$. Since $\sigma\mapsto\sigma^{-1}$ is a bijection of $S_n$, the sum is $\sum_\rho\mathrm{sign}(\rho)\prod_ka_{k\rho(k)}=\det A$. Column rules follow by applying the row rules to $A^T$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Write the Leibniz formula for $A^T$: factors $a_{\sigma(i)i}$.
  2. Reorder by the row index: $a_{k\sigma^{-1}(k)}$.
  3. Same sign, reindex by $\sigma^{-1}$.

In short: the transpose reverses each seating, and reversing keeps the sign.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Check $\det A^T=\det A$ for $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$.
    Answer

    $\det A=4-6=-2$, $\det A^T=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\2&4\end{pmatrix}=4-6=-2$ ✓.

  2. Rung 2. Give a second proof using lesson 18: write $A$ as a product of elementary matrices.
    Answer

    If $A$ is not invertible, neither is $A^T$ (same rank, lesson 16), so both determinants are $0$. Otherwise $A=T_1\cdots T_k$ and $A^T=T_k^T\cdots T_1^T$. Each $T^T$ is elementary of the same kind with the same determinant ($S_i(\mu)^T=S_i(\mu)$, $P_{i,j}^T=P_{i,j}$, $Q_{i,j}(\lambda)^T=Q_{j,i}(\lambda)$), and the product rule gives $\det A^T=\det A$.

Proof B: block triangular matrices (script, block matrices, p. 142)

Claim. If $A=\begin{pmatrix}B_1&C\\0&B_2\end{pmatrix}$ with square blocks $B_1\in M_{k\times k}(K)$ and $B_2\in M_{(n-k)\times(n-k)}(K)$, then $\det A=\det B_1\cdot\det B_2$. By Proof A the same holds for $\begin{pmatrix}B_1&0\\C&B_2\end{pmatrix}$.

The idea. In the Leibniz formula, a seating that sends one of the last $n-k$ rows into the first $k$ columns hits the zero block. So only seatings that keep both blocks survive, each of them is a pair (seating of $B_1$, seating of $B_2$), and its sign is the product of the two signs.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $\sigma\in S_n$ with a nonzero term $\prod_ia_{i\sigma(i)}$.”
  • Last line: “$=\det B_1\cdot\det B_2$.”
  • The gap: $\sigma$ maps $\{k+1,\dots,n\}$ into itself, hence also $\{1,\dots,k\}$; inversions only occur inside the blocks.
The storyWhat you write

The lower rows may not sit in the empty corner.

Let $X=\{1,\dots,k\}$ and $Y=\{k+1,\dots,n\}$. For $i\in Y$ and $j\in X$, $a_{ij}=0$. So if the term of $\sigma$ is nonzero, then $\sigma(Y)\subseteq Y$; as $\sigma$ is injective and $|\sigma(Y)|=|Y|$, $\sigma(Y)=Y$ and hence $\sigma(X)=X$.

Such a seating is two independent seatings, one per block, and its parity is the sum of their parities.

Such $\sigma$ correspond bijectively to pairs $(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)$, where $\sigma_1=\sigma|_X$ is a permutation of $X$ and $\sigma_2$ is $\sigma|_Y$ shifted to $\{1,\dots,n-k\}$. A pair $i<j$ with $i\in X$, $j\in Y$ is never an inversion, because $\sigma(i)\le k<\sigma(j)$. So the inversions of $\sigma$ are those of $\sigma_1$ and of $\sigma_2$, and $\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)=\mathrm{sign}(\sigma_1)\,\mathrm{sign}(\sigma_2)$.

So the total land is the product of the two blocks' lands.

Hence $\det A=\sum_{\sigma_1,\sigma_2}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma_1)\mathrm{sign}(\sigma_2)\prod_{i\in X}a_{i\sigma_1(i)}\prod_{i\in Y}a_{i\sigma(i)}$, and this double sum is the product $\det B_1\cdot\det B_2$. ∎

Moves used: reindex by a bijection

The proof in one piece

A nonzero Leibniz term needs $\sigma(Y)\subseteq Y$ for $Y=\{k+1,\dots,n\}$, because $a_{ij}=0$ for $i\in Y$, $j\le k$; so $\sigma(Y)=Y$ and $\sigma(X)=X$. Such $\sigma$ are pairs $(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)$ of permutations of the blocks, with no inversions between $X$ and $Y$, so $\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)=\mathrm{sign}(\sigma_1)\mathrm{sign}(\sigma_2)$. The sum factorises into $\det B_1\det B_2$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Zero block $\Rightarrow$ surviving $\sigma$ keep both blocks.
  2. No inversions across the blocks $\Rightarrow$ signs multiply.
  3. The sum factorises.

In short: a block triangular determinant is the product of the diagonal blocks' determinants. (Often allowed without proof, as in FS18, 6 (b).)

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Is $\det\begin{pmatrix}B_1&C\\D&B_2\end{pmatrix}=\det B_1\det B_2-\det C\det D$ for $2\times2$ blocks?
    Answer

    No. Take $B_1=B_2=0$, $C=D=I_2$: the matrix is a permutation matrix of $(3,4,1,2)$, which has $4$ inversions, so $\det=1$; the formula gives $0-1=-1$.

  2. Rung 2. Prove the claim with row operations instead, when $B_1$ is invertible.
    Answer

    Row operations inside the first $k$ rows bring $B_1$ to triangular form $R_1$ (factor $c_1$ with $\det B_1=c_1\det R_1$); operations inside the last rows bring $B_2$ to $R_2$. The whole matrix becomes triangular with diagonal those of $R_1$ and $R_2$, so $\det A=c_1c_2\det R_1\det R_2=\det B_1\det B_2$.

3. Expansion along a row or a column, the adjugate, Cramer's rule

Minors and the chessboard. For $A\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ let $A_{ij}\in M_{(n-1)\times(n-1)}(K)$ be the matrix obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$. The sign $(-1)^{i+j}$ follows a chessboard pattern, with $+$ in the top left corner:

$$\begin{pmatrix}+&-&+&\cdots\\-&+&-&\cdots\\+&-&+&\cdots\\\vdots&&&\ddots\end{pmatrix}$$

Proof C: the expansion along a row or a column (script Theorem 4.4.1, pp. 144–145)

Claim. For every row $i$ and every column $j$,

$$\begin{aligned}\det A&=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{i+k}a_{ik}\det A_{ik}\\&=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{k+j}a_{kj}\det A_{kj}.\end{aligned}$$

The idea. Split row $1$ into its $n$ entries by (D1). In the $k$-th piece, row 1 is $a_{1k}e_k^T$; move column $k$ to the front by $k-1$ neighbouring swaps, and the matrix becomes block triangular with blocks $a_{1k}$ and $A_{1k}$ (Proof B). For row $i$, first move row $i$ to the top by $i-1$ neighbouring swaps. Columns follow by transposing.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Row 1 of $A$ is $\sum_ka_{1k}e_k^T$, so by (D1) $\det A=\sum_k\det B_k$, where $B_k$ is $A$ with row 1 replaced by $a_{1k}e_k^T$.”
  • Last line: “$\det A=\sum_k(-1)^{i+k}a_{ik}\det A_{ik}$; columns by Proof A.”
  • The gap: $\det B_k=(-1)^{k-1}a_{1k}\det A_{1k}$ by column swaps and Proof B; then row swaps for row $i$.
The storyWhat you write

Split the land along the first edge, one piece per entry.

Row 1 of $A$ is $\sum_{k=1}^na_{1k}e_k^T$. By (D1) in row 1, $\det A=\sum_k\det B_k$, where $B_k$ is $A$ with row 1 replaced by $a_{1k}e_k^T$.

Slide the chosen column to the front, one neighbour at a time; each step flips the sign.

Swap column $k$ with column $k-1$, then with $k-2$, and so on: $k-1$ column swaps (Proof A) move it to the front and keep the order of the other columns. The result $B_k'$ has first row $(a_{1k},0,\dots,0)$, and deleting its first row and column leaves exactly $A_{1k}$. By Proof B (lower block triangular), $\det B_k'=a_{1k}\det A_{1k}$, so $\det B_k=(-1)^{k-1}a_{1k}\det A_{1k}=(-1)^{1+k}a_{1k}\det A_{1k}$.

For another row, first slide that row to the top.

Let $A'$ be $A$ with row $i$ moved to the top by $i-1$ neighbouring row swaps; the other rows keep their order. Then $\det A=(-1)^{i-1}\det A'$, row 1 of $A'$ is row $i$ of $A$, and $A'_{1k}=A_{ik}$. By the case $i=1$, $\det A=(-1)^{i-1}\sum_k(-1)^{1+k}a_{ik}\det A_{ik}=\sum_k(-1)^{i+k}a_{ik}\det A_{ik}$.

Columns are rows of the turned ledger.

Column $j$ of $A$ is row $j$ of $A^T$, and $(A^T)_{jk}=(A_{kj})^T$. By the row case for $A^T$ and Proof A, $\det A=\det A^T=\sum_k(-1)^{j+k}a_{kj}\det A_{kj}$. ∎

Moves used: expand a row by multilinearity

The proof in one piece

By (D1), $\det A=\sum_k\det B_k$, where $B_k$ has first row $a_{1k}e_k^T$ and the other rows of $A$. Moving column $k$ to the front by $k-1$ neighbouring swaps gives a lower block triangular matrix with blocks $a_{1k}$ and $A_{1k}$, so $\det B_k=(-1)^{1+k}a_{1k}\det A_{1k}$ (Proofs A and B). For row $i$, move it to the top by $i-1$ neighbouring row swaps; the minors along the new first row are the $A_{ik}$, which gives the factor $(-1)^{i-1}$ and the formula. For columns apply the row formula to $A^T$ and use $\det A^T=\det A$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Row 1: split by (D1) into $n$ matrices with one entry in row 1.
  2. Move that entry's column to the front: $k-1$ swaps, then block triangular.
  3. Row $i$: move it to the top with $i-1$ swaps. Columns: transpose.

In short: split the row, slide to the corner, count the swaps. HS18, 5 (a) asks exactly this for the last row (Exercise 2).

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Expand $\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\0&4&5\\0&0&6\end{pmatrix}$ along the first column.
    Answer

    Only $a_{11}=1$ is nonzero: $\det=1\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix}4&5\\0&6\end{pmatrix}=24$.

  2. Rung 2. Why do we move column $k$ to the front by neighbouring swaps instead of one swap $C_1\leftrightarrow C_k$?
    Answer

    One swap would put column $1$ in position $k$, and then the matrix left after deleting row 1 and column 1 would not be $A_{1k}$, whose columns are in the original order. Neighbouring swaps keep the order of the other columns.

Proof D: the adjugate, the inverse and Cramer's rule (script Theorem 4.4.6 and Corollaries 4.4.7–4.4.8, pp. 146–147)

Claim. Define the adjugate $\mathrm{adj}(A)\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ by $\mathrm{adj}(A)_{ij}:=(-1)^{i+j}\det A_{ji}$ (note the swapped indices). Then

$$A\,\mathrm{adj}(A)=\mathrm{adj}(A)\,A=\det A\cdot I_n.$$

Hence, if $\det A\ne0$: $A^{-1}=\frac1{\det A}\mathrm{adj}(A)$, and the unique solution of $Ax=b$ is given by Cramer's rule $x_i=\frac{\det A_i}{\det A}$, where $A_i$ is $A$ with column $i$ replaced by $b$.

The idea. The diagonal entries of $A\,\mathrm{adj}(A)$ are expansions of $\det A$ along a row. An off-diagonal entry is the expansion of a matrix in which one row has been replaced by another row, so it has two equal rows and determinant $0$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “$(A\,\mathrm{adj}(A))_{ij}=\sum_ka_{ik}\,\mathrm{adj}(A)_{kj}=\sum_k(-1)^{j+k}a_{ik}\det A_{jk}$.”
  • Last line (Cramer): “so $x_i=\det A_i/\det A$.”
  • The gap: recognise both sums as expansions along row $j$ (Proof C).
The storyWhat you write

On the diagonal the table of small lands rebuilds the whole land.

$(A\,\mathrm{adj}(A))_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^na_{ik}(-1)^{k+j}\det A_{jk}$. For $i=j$ this is the expansion of $\det A$ along row $i$ (Proof C), so it equals $\det A$.

Off the diagonal it measures a flat ledger with a copied edge.

Let $i\ne j$, and let $B$ be $A$ with row $j$ replaced by row $i$ (not swapped). Then $B_{jk}=A_{jk}$ (row $j$ is deleted anyway) and $b_{jk}=a_{ik}$. So the sum is $\sum_k(-1)^{j+k}b_{jk}\det B_{jk}=\det B$ by Proof C along row $j$, and $\det B=0$ by (D2), as rows $i$ and $j$ of $B$ are equal. Hence $A\,\mathrm{adj}(A)=\det A\cdot I_n$. The same argument with columns (Proof C, column version) gives $\mathrm{adj}(A)A=\det A\cdot I_n$.

Dividing by the land gives the inverse ledger, and solving becomes a quotient of lands.

If $\det A\ne0$, dividing by $\det A$ gives $A\cdot\frac{\mathrm{adj}(A)}{\det A}=I_n$, so $A^{-1}=\frac{\mathrm{adj}(A)}{\det A}$. Then $x=A^{-1}b$, so $x_i=\frac1{\det A}\sum_j(-1)^{i+j}\det A_{ji}\,b_j$. The sum is the expansion of $\det A_i$ along column $i$: column $i$ of $A_i$ is $b$, and deleting it leaves the minors $(A_i)_{ji}=A_{ji}$. So $x_i=\frac{\det A_i}{\det A}$. ∎

Moves used: check the axioms entry by entry · read a sum as a determinant

The proof in one piece

$(A\,\mathrm{adj}(A))_{ij}=\sum_k(-1)^{j+k}a_{ik}\det A_{jk}$. For $i=j$ this is $\det A$ expanded along row $i$. For $i\ne j$ it is the expansion along row $j$ of the matrix $B$ obtained from $A$ by replacing row $j$ with row $i$; $B$ has two equal rows, so the entry is $0$. Thus $A\,\mathrm{adj}(A)=\det A\cdot I_n$, and the same with columns gives $\mathrm{adj}(A)A=\det A\cdot I_n$. If $\det A\ne0$, $A^{-1}=\mathrm{adj}(A)/\det A$, and $x=A^{-1}b$ has $x_i=\frac1{\det A}\sum_j(-1)^{i+j}b_j\det A_{ji}=\frac{\det A_i}{\det A}$, by expanding $\det A_i$ along column $i$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Write the $(i,j)$ entry of $A\,\mathrm{adj}(A)$ as a sum.
  2. $i=j$: expansion along row $i$.
  3. $i\ne j$: the expansion of “$A$ with row $j$ replaced by row $i$”: two equal rows, $0$.
  4. Inverse: divide by $\det A$. Cramer: expand $\det A_i$ along column $i$.

In short: diagonal = expansion, off-diagonal = expansion of a matrix with a repeated row.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Compute $\mathrm{adj}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$.
    Answer

    $\mathrm{adj}(A)_{11}=\det A_{11}=d$, $\mathrm{adj}(A)_{12}=-\det A_{21}=-b$, $\mathrm{adj}(A)_{21}=-\det A_{12}=-c$, $\mathrm{adj}(A)_{22}=a$: $\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$, the familiar $2\times2$ inverse formula.

  2. Rung 2. Show $\det(\mathrm{adj}\,A)=(\det A)^{n-1}$ when $\det A\ne0$.
    Answer

    Take determinants in $A\,\mathrm{adj}(A)=\det A\cdot I_n$: $\det A\det(\mathrm{adj}\,A)=(\det A)^n$; divide by $\det A$.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof D from the frame alone.

Worked examples: a mixed set before the exercises

Worked example 1: one determinant, three routes (HS20, problem 2 (c))

Lesson 18 computed $\det\begin{pmatrix}7&6&1\\5&6&6\\2&4&5\end{pmatrix}=-28$ by elimination. Expand it along column 3.

  1. Which signs do the entries of column 3 get?
    Answer

    The chessboard: $(1,3)$: $+$; $(2,3)$: $-$; $(3,3)$: $+$.

  2. Expand.
    Answer

    $+1\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix}5&6\\2&4\end{pmatrix}-6\det\begin{pmatrix}7&6\\2&4\end{pmatrix}+5\det\begin{pmatrix}7&6\\5&6\end{pmatrix}=8-6\cdot16+5\cdot12=8-96+60=-28$ ✓. The explorer starts with this matrix.

  3. Combine routes: first a column operation, then expansion.
    Answer

    $C_2-C_1$ (Proof A: no change) gives columns $(7,5,2)$, $(-1,1,2)$, $(1,6,5)$. Now $C_1+7C_2$ and $C_3+C_2$ (no change) give first row $(0,-1,0)$: rows $(0,-1,0)$, $(12,1,7)$, $(16,2,7)$. Expand along row 1: only $a_{12}=-1$ with sign $-$: $\det=-(-1)\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix}12&7\\16&7\end{pmatrix}=84-112=-28$ ✓. Making zeros first and expanding second is the fastest method by hand.

Worked example 2: the adjugate and Cramer's rule for $2\times2$

Solve $x+2y=5$, $3x+4y=6$ with Cramer's rule, and check with the adjugate.

  1. Cramer.
    Answer

    $\det A=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}=-2$. $x=\det\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\6&4\end{pmatrix}/(-2)=8/(-2)=-4$, $y=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&5\\3&6\end{pmatrix}/(-2)=-9/(-2)=\tfrac92$. Check: $-4+9=5$ ✓, $-12+18=6$ ✓.

  2. Adjugate.
    Answer

    $A^{-1}=\frac1{-2}\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\-3&1\end{pmatrix}$, and $A^{-1}\binom56=\frac1{-2}\binom{8}{-9}=\binom{-4}{9/2}$ ✓. Cramer is the adjugate formula, one coordinate at a time.

  3. When is Cramer's rule a good idea?
    Answer

    For $2\times2$ and $3\times3$ systems with parameters, and for theory (the solution depends continuously, even rationally, on the entries). For larger numeric systems, Gaussian elimination is far cheaper: Cramer needs $n+1$ determinants.

Worked example 3: quick exam items (HS24, HS20)

  1. HS24, MC1: which is true for $\det:M_{n\times n}(\mathbb C)\to\mathbb C$? (A) $\det(A^T)=\det A$; (B) $\det(A+B)=\det A+\det B$; (C) $\det(-I_n)=-1$.
    Answer

    (A), Proof A. (B) fails for $A=B=I_2$: $4\ne2$. (C) $\det(-I_n)=(-1)^n$, which is $1$ for even $n$.

  2. HS20, (14): for every matrix $A$ with $-A^T=A$, $\det A=0$.
    Answer

    False. $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$ satisfies $-A^T=A$ and has $\det A=1$. It is true for odd $n$ over $\mathbb R$ (Exercise 6).

  3. True or false: if $A$ has a zero column, then $\det A=0$.
    Answer

    True: expand along that column, or note that $A^T$ has a zero row (Proof A).

Why it's cool

Cramer's rule says that the solution of $Ax=b$ is a quotient of two polynomials in the entries. So small changes of the data give small changes of the solution, as long as $\det A$ stays away from $0$. Engineers use exactly this to judge whether a system is safe to solve numerically: when $\det A$ is tiny compared to the entries, the system is “ill-conditioned” and rounding errors explode. The adjugate also proves FS26's surprise in one line: an integer matrix with determinant $\pm1$ has an integer inverse, which is the reason such matrices are the symmetries of a crystal lattice.

For wonder: tridiagonal determinants count tilings (optional, 5 min)

Let $T_n$ be the $n\times n$ tridiagonal matrix with $1$ on the diagonal, $1$ above and $-1$ below. Expanding along the first row gives $\det T_n=\det T_{n-1}+\det T_{n-2}$: the Fibonacci numbers $1,2,3,5,8,\dots$. They count the ways to tile a strip of length $n$ with squares and dominoes. Each tiling is a surviving term of the Leibniz formula: a square is a diagonal entry, a domino a swap of two neighbours, and the sign $-1$ of the swap is cancelled by the product $1\cdot(-1)$ of its two entries.

Exercise 1 (HS17, 6 (c), 4 points: a tridiagonal determinant)

Let $n\ge1$ and $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb C$. Let $A_n\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb C)$ have $\alpha+\beta$ on the diagonal, $\beta$ directly above it, $\alpha$ directly below it, and $0$ elsewhere. Compute $\det A_n$.

Hint

Expand along the first row, and the second minor along its first column. You get a recursion $D_n=(\alpha+\beta)D_{n-1}-\alpha\beta D_{n-2}$. Compute $D_1,D_2,D_3$ and guess.

Solution

Frame: expansion gives a recursion; guess the closed form; induction. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

Write $D_n=\det A_n$ and $D_0:=1$. Expanding along row 1: the entry $\alpha+\beta$ has minor $A_{n-1}$; the entry $\beta$ (sign $-$) has a minor whose first column is $(\alpha,0,\dots,0)^T$, and expanding that along its first column gives $\alpha D_{n-2}$. So for $n\ge2$

$$D_n=(\alpha+\beta)D_{n-1}-\alpha\beta\,D_{n-2},$$

with $D_1=\alpha+\beta$, $D_2=(\alpha+\beta)^2-\alpha\beta=\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\beta^2$. Claim: $D_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\alpha^k\beta^{n-k}$ (for $\alpha\ne\beta$ this is $\frac{\alpha^{n+1}-\beta^{n+1}}{\alpha-\beta}$; for $\alpha=\beta$ it is $(n+1)\alpha^n$).

Induction: true for $n=0,1$. Write $S_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\alpha^k\beta^{n-k}$. Then $\alpha S_{n-1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha^k\beta^{n-k}=S_n-\beta^n$ and $\beta S_{n-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha^k\beta^{n-k}=S_n-\alpha^n$, while $\alpha\beta S_{n-2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\alpha^k\beta^{n-k}=S_n-\alpha^n-\beta^n$. Hence $(\alpha+\beta)S_{n-1}-\alpha\beta S_{n-2}=2S_n-\alpha^n-\beta^n-(S_n-\alpha^n-\beta^n)=S_n$. $\blacksquare$ Check: $\alpha=\beta=1$, $n=2$: $\det\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}=3=(2+1)\cdot1$ ✓.

Exercise 2 (HS18, problem 5, 15 points)

Let $a_1,a_2,\dots\in K$ and $A_n:=\big(a_{\min\{i,j\}}\big)_{i,j=1,\dots,n}$. (a) Prove the formula for the expansion of a determinant along the last row. (b) Write out $A_n$. (c) Show $\det A_{n+1}=\det A_n\cdot(a_{n+1}-a_n)$ for all $n\ge1$. (d) Give an explicit formula for $\det A_n$ and prove it by induction.

Hint

(a) Proof C with $i=n$. (c) Rows $n$ and $n+1$ of $A_{n+1}$ agree except in the last entry. Subtract, then expand along the last row.

Solution

Frame: Proof C; one row operation; induction. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

(a) Proof C for $i=n$: split row $n$ by (D1); in the $k$-th piece move column $k$ to the last place by $n-k$ neighbouring swaps; the result is block triangular with blocks $A_{nk}$ and $a_{nk}$, so $\det A=\sum_k(-1)^{n-k}a_{nk}\det A_{nk}=\sum_k(-1)^{n+k}a_{nk}\det A_{nk}$.

(b) $A_n=\begin{pmatrix}a_1&a_1&a_1&\cdots&a_1\\a_1&a_2&a_2&\cdots&a_2\\a_1&a_2&a_3&\cdots&a_3\\\vdots&&&\ddots&\vdots\\a_1&a_2&a_3&\cdots&a_n\end{pmatrix}$.

(c) Row $n$ of $A_{n+1}$ is $(a_1,\dots,a_n,a_n)$ and row $n+1$ is $(a_1,\dots,a_n,a_{n+1})$. $L_{n+1}-L_n$ (no change) gives last row $(0,\dots,0,a_{n+1}-a_n)$. Expanding along it (a): $\det A_{n+1}=(a_{n+1}-a_n)\det A_n$, since deleting the last row and column leaves $A_n$.

(d) $\det A_n=a_1\prod_{k=2}^n(a_k-a_{k-1})$. Induction: $\det A_1=a_1$; the step is (c). $\blacksquare$

Exercise 3 (FS26, 7.Q1, 3 points)

Let $A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb R)$ have all entries in $\mathbb Z$ and $\det A=1$. Prove that $A^{-1}$ has all entries in $\mathbb Z$.

Hint

Proof D, and the Leibniz formula for the minors.

Solution

Frame: adjugate formula; integrality of minors. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

By Proof D, $A^{-1}=\frac1{\det A}\mathrm{adj}(A)=\mathrm{adj}(A)$. Each entry of $\mathrm{adj}(A)$ is $\pm\det A_{ji}$, and $A_{ji}$ has integer entries, so $\det A_{ji}\in\mathbb Z$ by the Leibniz formula (lesson 19). Hence $A^{-1}$ has integer entries. $\blacksquare$ (The same holds for $\det A=-1$.)

Exercise 4 (FS26, 7.Q2, 5 points)

Let $B\in M_{n\times n}(K)$ and $L_B:M_{n\times n}(K)\to M_{n\times n}(K)$, $L_B(A)=BA$. Prove $\det(L_B)=(\det B)^n$.

Hint

The determinant of a linear map is the determinant of its matrix in any basis (similar matrices have equal determinants, lesson 18, Exercise 7). Order the basis $E_{kl}$ column by column. Column $j$ of $BA$ is $B$ times column $j$ of $A$.

Solution

Frame: a good basis; block diagonal matrix; Proof B. (Checked twice, no official solution.)

Use the basis $E_{11},E_{21},\dots,E_{n1},E_{12},\dots,E_{n2},\dots,E_{nn}$: the coordinates of $A$ are its columns $a_1,\dots,a_n$ stacked. Since $BA$ has columns $Ba_1,\dots,Ba_n$, the matrix of $L_B$ is block diagonal with $n$ blocks equal to $B$. By Proof B (applied $n-1$ times, by induction), $\det(L_B)=(\det B)^n$. The determinant of $L_B$ does not depend on the basis, because the matrices of $L_B$ in two bases are similar. $\blacksquare$

Exercise 5 (the Vandermonde determinant; script Example 4.3.27)

Show that for $x_1,\dots,x_n\in K$

$$\det\big(x_i^{\,j-1}\big)_{i,j=1,\dots,n}=\prod_{1\le i<j\le n}(x_j-x_i).$$

Hint

Column operations $C_k-x_1C_{k-1}$ for $k=n,n-1,\dots,2$ (in this order). Then expand along row 1, take a factor out of each row, and use induction.

Solution

Frame: column operations clear row 1; expansion; induction on $n$.

Row $i$ is $(1,x_i,x_i^2,\dots,x_i^{n-1})$. Apply $C_k-x_1C_{k-1}$ for $k=n$ down to $2$ (each uses the unchanged column $k-1$; no change of $\det$, Proof A). Row $i$ becomes $(1,\;x_i-x_1,\;x_i(x_i-x_1),\;\dots,\;x_i^{n-2}(x_i-x_1))$; row 1 becomes $(1,0,\dots,0)$.

Expand along row 1 (Proof C): $\det V$ is the determinant of the $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ matrix with rows $(x_i-x_1)\cdot(1,x_i,\dots,x_i^{n-2})$, $i=2,\dots,n$. Take $x_i-x_1$ out of each row (D1): $\det V(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\prod_{i=2}^n(x_i-x_1)\cdot\det V(x_2,\dots,x_n)$. With $\det V(x_n)=1$, induction gives the product over all $i<j$. $\blacksquare$ For $n=3$ this is lesson 18, Exercise 6.

Exercise 6 (skew-symmetric matrices; HS20, (14) refined)

Let $A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb R)$ with $A^T=-A$. Show that $\det A=0$ if $n$ is odd. Does this hold for even $n$?

Hint

Apply Proof A to $A^T=-A$, and remember $\det(-A)=(-1)^n\det A$.

Solution

Frame: one chain of equalities; one counterexample.

$\det A=\det A^T=\det(-A)=(-1)^n\det A$. For odd $n$: $\det A=-\det A$, so $2\det A=0$ and $\det A=0$ (in $\mathbb R$, $2\ne0$). For even $n$ it fails: $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $1$ (Worked example 3). Over $\mathbb F_2$, skew-symmetric means symmetric, and $I_3$ is a counterexample for odd $n$.

Exercise 7 (an adjugate and Cramer's rule for $3\times3$)

Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\1&2&1\\1&1&2\end{pmatrix}$ ($\det A=4$, lesson 18). Compute $\mathrm{adj}(A)$ and $A^{-1}$, and solve $Ax=e_1$ with Cramer's rule.

Hint

$A$ is symmetric, so $\mathrm{adj}(A)$ is too. Each minor is $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ (determinant $3$) or has determinant $\pm1$.

Solution

Frame: nine $2\times2$ minors with the chessboard; then a check; then Cramer.

$\det A_{11}=3$, $\det A_{12}=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}=1$, $\det A_{13}=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=-1$; by symmetry the others follow. With the signs,

$$\mathrm{adj}(A)=\begin{pmatrix}3&-1&-1\\-1&3&-1\\-1&-1&3\end{pmatrix},$$

and $A^{-1}=\frac14\,\mathrm{adj}(A)$.

Check: row 1 of $A$ times column 1: $6-1-1=4$ ✓; times column 2: $-2+3-1=0$ ✓.

Cramer for $b=e_1$: $\det A_1=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\0&2&1\\0&1&2\end{pmatrix}=3$, $\det A_2=\det\begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\1&0&1\\1&0&2\end{pmatrix}=-1$, $\det A_3=\det\begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\1&2&0\\1&1&0\end{pmatrix}=-1$. So $x=\tfrac14(3,-1,-1)$, the first column of $A^{-1}$ ✓.
