1. Warm-up (5 min)

Three quick questions. Answer each in your head, then open it.

  1. A step function on $[0,4]$ is $3$ on $(0,1)$, $-2$ on $(1,4)$, and $100$ at $x=1$. What is its integral?

    Answer

    $3\cdot1+(-2)\cdot3=-3$. The value at a single point never enters: it is a rectangle of width $0$.

  2. True or false: every Riemann integrable function on $[a,b]$ is continuous.

    Answer

    False. A step function with a jump is integrable but not continuous. The true direction is the other one: continuous on $[a,b]$ implies integrable.

  3. The Dirichlet function ($1$ on rationals, $0$ on irrationals) is bounded and takes only two values. Is it integrable on $[0,1]$?

    Answer

    No. Its upper integral is $1$ and its lower integral is $0$: every piece of every partition contains a rational and an irrational point.

2. Recap card (10 min)

Read this once, slowly. Everything in bold must come out exactly in the exam.

Lesson 9: the Riemann integral

  • Step function: there is a partition $a=x_0<\dots<x_n=b$ with $f=c_k$ on each open piece $(x_{k-1},x_k)$. Its integral is $\sum_{k=1}^n c_k\,(x_k-x_{k-1})$, independent of the partition.
  • Lower and upper integral of a bounded $f$: $\underline I(f)=\sup\{\int_a^b u\,dx : u\le f \text{ step}\}$ and $\overline I(f)=\inf\{\int_a^b o\,dx : o\ge f\text{ step}\}$. Always $\underline I(f)\le\overline I(f)$. $f$ is Riemann integrable if they are equal; the common value is $\int_a^b f\,dx$.
  • The ε-criterion: a bounded $f$ is integrable if and only if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there are step functions $u\le f\le o$ with $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$.
  • Rules: linearity; monotonicity ($f\le g\Rightarrow\int f\le\int g$); triangle inequality $\left|\int_a^b f\,dx\right|\le\int_a^b|f|\,dx$; additivity $\int_a^c=\int_a^b+\int_b^c$; changing finitely many values changes nothing.
  • Monotone functions on $[a,b]$ are integrable. Continuous functions on $[a,b]$ are integrable. Piecewise monotone (and bounded) functions are integrable, so every polynomial is.
  • The Dirichlet function is bounded but not integrable. So neither “bounded” nor “finitely many values” is enough.

4. Write it on paper: three exam proofs (25 min)

For each proof: read the claim and the frame, close your eyes for ten seconds, then write the whole proof on paper without looking at the lesson. Only then open the fold and mark yourself with the grader's list.

Proofs 1 and 2 share one frame: “Let $\varepsilon>0$ … so $u\le f\le o$ and $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$. By the ε-criterion, $f$ is integrable.” Only the middle changes.

Proof 1: monotone functions are integrable (7 points)

Claim. Every monotone function $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ is Riemann integrable. (Lesson 9, Proof E.)

What the definitions force

The ε-criterion frame above, after two lines: “without loss of generality $f$ is increasing” and “so $f$ is bounded”. The gap: equal pieces $x_k=a+k\frac{b-a}n$, $u$ at the left ends and $o$ at the right ends, then a telescoping sum, then choose $n$ by Archimedes.

The proof and the grader's list

Without loss of generality $f$ is increasing (otherwise consider $-f$ and use linearity). Then $f(a)\le f(x)\le f(b)$ for all $x$, so $f$ is bounded. Let $\varepsilon>0$, let $n\in\mathbb N$ (chosen below) and $x_k=a+k\frac{b-a}n$ for $k=0,\dots,n$. Define step functions by $u=f(x_{k-1})$ on $[x_{k-1},x_k)$, $u(b)=f(b)$, and $o=f(x_k)$ on $(x_{k-1},x_k]$, $o(a)=f(a)$. Since $f$ is increasing, $u\le f\le o$: for $x\in[x_{k-1},x_k)$ we have $u(x)=f(x_{k-1})\le f(x)$, and likewise for $o$. Then, by the telescoping sum,

$$\begin{aligned}&\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx\\&=\frac{b-a}n\sum_{k=1}^n\big(f(x_k)-f(x_{k-1})\big)\\&=\frac{(b-a)\big(f(b)-f(a)\big)}n.\end{aligned}$$

By the Archimedean principle choose $n>\frac{(b-a)(f(b)-f(a))}{\varepsilon}$. Then $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$. As $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $f$ is Riemann integrable by the ε-criterion. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • “without loss of generality increasing” with the reason, and “bounded”;
  • $u$ and $o$ defined precisely (including the endpoints), with the check $u\le f\le o$;
  • the telescoping computation;
  • the explicit choice of $n$ (Archimedes) and the sentence “by the ε-criterion”. These two are where points are usually lost.

Proof 2: continuous functions are integrable (Figalli HS23 B1, 7 points)

Claim. Every continuous $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ is Riemann integrable. (Lesson 9, Worked example 2.)

What the definitions force

The same frame as Proof 1. What replaces monotonicity: on each piece, the extreme value theorem gives a minimum $m_k$ and a maximum $M_k$; put $u=m_k$ and $o=M_k$. What replaces the telescope: Heine's theorem gives one $\delta$ for $\frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}$, so pieces narrower than $\delta$ make every gap $M_k-m_k<\frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}$.

The proof and the grader's list

$f$ is bounded by the boundedness theorem. Let $\varepsilon>0$. By Heine's theorem $f$ is uniformly continuous, so there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)-f(y)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}$ for all $x,y\in[a,b]$ with $|x-y|<\delta$. By the Archimedean principle choose $n\in\mathbb N$ with $\frac{b-a}n<\delta$, and let $x_k=a+k\frac{b-a}n$. On each $[x_{k-1},x_k]$, $f$ is continuous, so by the extreme value theorem it attains a minimum $m_k=f(z_k)$ and a maximum $M_k=f(z_k')$ there. Since $|z_k-z_k'|\le\frac{b-a}n<\delta$, we get $M_k-m_k<\frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}$. Define step functions by $u=m_k$, $o=M_k$ on $[x_{k-1},x_k)$ and $u(b)=o(b)=f(b)$. Then $u\le f\le o$, and

$$\begin{aligned}\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx&=\sum_{k=1}^n(M_k-m_k)\frac{b-a}n\\&<\frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{b-a}n=\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$

By the ε-criterion, $f$ is Riemann integrable. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • naming Heine's theorem (uniform continuity) and using it with $\frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}$;
  • the partition finer than $\delta$, chosen by Archimedes;
  • the extreme value theorem on each piece, giving $m_k$ and $M_k$, and $u\le f\le o$;
  • the estimate of $\int(o-u)$ and the sentence “by the ε-criterion”.

Proof 3: the Dirichlet function is not integrable

Claim. Let $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb R$ with $f(x)=1$ for rational $x$ and $f(x)=0$ for irrational $x$. Then $\overline I(f)=1$ and $\underline I(f)=0$, so $f$ is not Riemann integrable. (Lesson 9, Proof C.)

What the definitions force

“$\overline I(f)=1$” is about an infimum, so two lines: every upper sum is $\ge1$, and some upper sum equals $1$. Start with “Let $o\ge f$ be a step function with partition $0=x_0<\dots<x_n=1$ and values $c_k$.” The gap: density of $\mathbb Q$ in every piece, then a telescoping sum. The lower integral is the mirror image with the irrationals.

The proof and the grader's list

Let $o\ge f$ be a step function, $o=c_k$ on $(x_{k-1},x_k)$ for a partition $0=x_0<\dots<x_n=1$. By density of $\mathbb Q$, each piece $(x_{k-1},x_k)$ contains a rational $x$, so $c_k=o(x)\ge f(x)=1$. Hence $$\begin{aligned}\int_0^1 o\,dx&=\sum_{k=1}^n c_k(x_k-x_{k-1})\\&\ge\sum_{k=1}^n(x_k-x_{k-1})\\&=x_n-x_0=1.\end{aligned}$$ So $1$ is a lower bound of the upper sums, and the constant step function $o=1\ge f$ has integral $1$. Hence $\overline I(f)=1$.

Likewise, each piece contains an irrational $x$ (for example $q+\sqrt2$ for a suitable rational $q$), so every step function $u\le f$ has values $d_k=u(x)\le f(x)=0$ on every piece and $\int_0^1u\,dx\le0$, while $u=0$ gives $0$. Hence $\underline I(f)=0$. Since $0\neq1$, $f$ is not Riemann integrable. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • an arbitrary upper step function with its partition, and density of $\mathbb Q$ named;
  • the telescoping sum giving $\ge1$, and the step function $o=1$ showing the infimum is exactly $1$;
  • the lower half with the irrationals (not just “similarly”: at least one sentence on why each piece contains an irrational);
  • the conclusion $\underline I(f)\neq\overline I(f)$.

5. Spot the mistake (5 min)

A student tries Proof 2 and hands in this. The claim is true, but the proof is wrong. Where exactly does it break?

“Proof.” Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $x_k=a+k\frac{b-a}n$. For each $k$, $f$ is continuous at $x_k$, so there is $\delta_k>0$ with $|f(x)-f(x_k)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2(b-a)}$ for $|x-x_k|<\delta_k$. Choose $n$ so large that $\frac{b-a}n<\delta_k$ for every $k$. Then on each piece $M_k-m_k<\frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}$, so $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$, and $f$ is integrable by the ε-criterion. ∎

The mistake

A circle: the points $x_k$ depend on $n$, the numbers $\delta_k$ depend on the points $x_k$, and then $n$ is chosen depending on the $\delta_k$. Changing $n$ changes the points, so changes the $\delta_k$, and there's no guarantee the new $\delta_k$ are still bigger than $\frac{b-a}n$. Plain continuity gives a $\delta$ that depends on the point; here one $\delta$ for all points is needed before the partition is chosen.

That's exactly what Heine's theorem (uniform continuity) provides. Exam lesson: whenever you choose a partition after a $\delta$, the $\delta$ must not depend on the points of that partition.

6. New: zeros of polynomials and interpolation (10 min)

This is in the script (§3.2.2, p. 137–138) and the interpolation formula is on the official formula sheet, but no lesson covers it. The script itself says the formulas “won't be particularly important for us”, so learn what the statements say and how to use the formula; don't memorise it.

The three facts

  • Zeros and factors (Wichtige Übung 3.19). For a polynomial $f$ and $z\in\mathbb C$: $(T-z)$ divides $f$ if and only if $f(z)=0$. Consequently a polynomial of degree $n$ that is not the zero polynomial has at most $n$ different zeros.
  • Comparing coefficients (Wichtige Übung 3.20). If $f,g$ have degree at most $n$ and agree at more than $n$ points, then $f=g$ (same degree, same coefficients).
  • Lagrange interpolation. Given $n$ different points $z_1,\dots,z_n$ and any values $w_1,\dots,w_n$, there is exactly one polynomial $f$ of degree at most $n-1$ with $f(z_k)=w_k$ for all $k$: $$\begin{aligned}f(z)&=\sum_{k=1}^n w_k\,p_k(z),\\ p_k(z)&=\prod_{j\neq k}\frac{z-z_j}{z_k-z_j}.\end{aligned}$$ The trick: $p_k(z_k)=1$ and $p_k(z_\ell)=0$ for $\ell\neq k$, so the sum picks out exactly $w_\ell$ at $z_\ell$. Uniqueness is the second fact: two such polynomials agree at $n$ points and have degree at most $n-1$.

Why the first fact holds (one paragraph, worth knowing)

Divide with remainder by $T-z$: $f=q\cdot(T-z)+r$ with $\deg r<1$, so $r$ is a constant. Putting in $T=z$ gives $f(z)=r$. So $f(z)=0$ exactly when $r=0$, that is, when $(T-z)$ divides $f$. For the count, use induction on the degree: if $z_1$ is a zero, then $f=(T-z_1)q$ with $\deg q=n-1$, and every other zero $z\neq z_1$ has $0=(z-z_1)q(z)$, so $q(z)=0$. So $f$ has at most $1+(n-1)=n$ zeros.

Worked example: the polynomial through $(0,1)$, $(1,3)$, $(2,7)$

  1. Write down $p_1,p_2,p_3$.

    Answer

    $p_1(x)=\frac{(x-1)(x-2)}{(0-1)(0-2)}=\frac{(x-1)(x-2)}2$, $p_2(x)=\frac{(x-0)(x-2)}{(1-0)(1-2)}=-x(x-2)$, $p_3(x)=\frac{(x-0)(x-1)}{(2-0)(2-1)}=\frac{x(x-1)}2$.

  2. Combine and expand.

    Answer

    $$\begin{aligned}f(x)&=1\cdot\tfrac{(x-1)(x-2)}2\\&\quad+3\cdot\big(-x(x-2)\big)\\&\quad+7\cdot\tfrac{x(x-1)}2\\&=\tfrac{8x^2-10x+2}2-3x^2+6x\\&=x^2+x+1.\end{aligned}$$

  3. Check, and say why there's no other polynomial of degree at most 2 through these points.

    Answer

    $f(0)=1$, $f(1)=3$, $f(2)=7$. If $g$ also had degree at most $2$ and these values, then $f$ and $g$ agree at $3>2$ points, so $f=g$ by comparing coefficients.

7. Exercises on paper (about 25 min)

Four exercises: the most exam-like ones. Redo them on paper, in full sentences. Each link opens the exercise with its solution folded underneath.

New exercise: interpolation

(a) Find the polynomial of degree at most $2$ with $f(-1)=2$, $f(0)=1$, $f(1)=2$, using the Lagrange formula. (b) Explain in one sentence why it is the only one. (c) A polynomial $g$ of degree at most $5$ has $g(k)=k^2+1$ for $k=0,1,\dots,5$. What is $g$?

Solution

(a) $p_1(x)=\frac{(x-0)(x-1)}{(-1-0)(-1-1)}=\frac{x(x-1)}2$, $p_2(x)=\frac{(x+1)(x-1)}{(0+1)(0-1)}=1-x^2$, $p_3(x)=\frac{(x+1)(x-0)}{(1+1)(1-0)}=\frac{x(x+1)}2$. So $$\begin{aligned}f(x)&=2\cdot\tfrac{x(x-1)}2+1\cdot(1-x^2)\\&\quad+2\cdot\tfrac{x(x+1)}2\\&=x^2-x+1-x^2+x^2+x\\&=x^2+1.\end{aligned}$$ Check: $f(-1)=2$, $f(0)=1$, $f(1)=2$.

(b) Any other polynomial of degree at most $2$ with these values agrees with $f$ at $3$ points, more than its degree, so it equals $f$ (comparing coefficients).

(c) $g(x)=x^2+1$. Both $g$ and $x^2+1$ have degree at most $5$ and agree at the $6$ points $0,\dots,5$, which is more than $5$, so they are equal. No Lagrange computation needed: guess, then use uniqueness.
