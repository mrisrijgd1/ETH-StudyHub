Recap of lesson 5 (3 min)

  1. How do you compute the work of a force along a curved path $\vec r(t)$?

    Answer

    $W=\int\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\vec F(\vec r(t))\cdot\dot{\vec r}(t)\,dt$.

  2. What is the work–energy theorem?

    Answer

    The work of the total force equals the change of kinetic energy: $W_{1\to2}=T_2-T_1$, with $T=\frac12mv^2$.

  3. Which way does $\nabla u$ point, relative to the level lines of $u$?

    Answer

    Perpendicular to them, towards increasing $u$ (steepest ascent).

1. The story: the tumbler's loop

For the spring festival, the carpenters build a wooden track in the courtyard: a long ramp down, then a full vertical circle, a loop. A tumbler will ride a little cart through it, upside down at the top. “From how high must I start?” she asks the old engineer. The carpenters want to measure speeds and angles at every plank. The engineer only asks two questions: how high is the start, and how high is the top of the loop?

“Height buys speed, and speed buys height back,” he says. “On a smooth track, nothing else is spent. At the top the cart must be fast enough that the track still has to push on it. Start lower than that, and she falls.”

In physics: for gravity, the work done between two points depends only on the heights, not on the shape of the track (lesson 5). Such forces are called conservative. For them you can define a potential energy $U$, and the sum $T+U$ never changes: conservation of mechanical energy. That turns most motion problems into one line of algebra. The engineer's second condition (the track must still push) is the circular-motion condition of lesson 1.

2. Conservative forces and potential energy

Definition (section 4.4.1, page 113). A force field $\vec F(\vec r)$ is conservative if there is a scalar function $U(\vec r)$ with

$$\vec F(\vec r)=-\nabla U(\vec r).$$

$U$ is the potential energy. The script also calls $U$ the potential and the difference $U(\vec r_2)-U(\vec r_1)$ the potential energy of $\vec r_2$ relative to $\vec r_1$; you'll also see $E_{\text{pot}}$. The minus sign is a convention: the force points “downhill”, towards smaller $U$.

Work of a conservative force (section 4.4.2, page 113). With $du=\nabla u\cdot d\vec r$ from lesson 5,

$$W_{1\to2}=\int_{\vec r_1}^{\vec r_2}\vec F\cdot d\vec r=-\int_{\vec r_1}^{\vec r_2}\nabla U\cdot d\vec r=-\int dU=U(\vec r_1)-U(\vec r_2).$$

So the work depends only on the endpoints, never on the path. Equivalently, $U(\vec r_2)-U(\vec r_1)=-\int_{\vec r_1}^{\vec r_2}\vec F\cdot d\vec r$: that's the formula sheet's $\Delta U_{AB}$, and it's how you find $U$ from $\vec F$.

Four equivalent ways to say “conservative”
  1. $\vec F=-\nabla U$ for some function $U$.
  2. The work $\int_{\vec r_1}^{\vec r_2}\vec F\cdot d\vec r$ is the same for every path between the same two points.
  3. The work around every closed path is zero: $\oint\vec F\cdot d\vec r=0$.
  4. $\nabla\times\vec F=0$ everywhere (in a region without holes). The script proves (1) ⇒ (3) with Stokes's theorem: $\oint\nabla U\cdot d\vec r=\int(\nabla\times\nabla U)\cdot d\vec A=0$, since a gradient never curls.

Not conservative: friction and drag (they depend on the velocity and always oppose it, so a closed loop costs energy), and in general forces that change with time.

Watch the sign. $U(\vec r_1)-U(\vec r_2)$ is the work done by the field. Lifting a body against gravity, you do the same amount with the opposite sign. Positive work by the field means the potential energy goes down.

The zero is free (page 114). $U$ and $U+c$ give the same force, since $\nabla c=0$. Only differences of $U$ are measurable, so you choose the zero wherever it's convenient: the ground, the rest length of a spring, or infinity for gravity.

Recipe: is this force conservative, and what is $U$?

  1. Compute the curl. In two dimensions, with $\vec F=(F_x,F_y)$, check $\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}$. If not equal: not conservative, stop.
  2. If equal, integrate: $U=-\int F_x\,dx+g(y)$, then fix $g(y)$ so that $-\frac{\partial U}{\partial y}=F_y$.
  3. For a one-dimensional or radial force, simply $U(x)=-\int F_x\,dx$ or $U(r)=-\int F_r\,dr$, plus the constant you choose.
  4. Check: differentiate back, $-\nabla U$ must give $\vec F$.

Worked example 1: three force fields

Which of these are conservative? Find $U$ where it exists. (a) The whirl of lesson 5, $\vec F=(-y,\,x)$. (b) $\vec F=(x,\,y)$. (c) $\vec F=(2xy,\,x^2)$.

  1. (a) Compare $\partial F_y/\partial x$ and $\partial F_x/\partial y$.

    Answer

    $\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x}=1$, $\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}=-1$: not equal. The curl is $1-(-1)=2\neq0$, so the whirl is not conservative. That's why the work in lesson 5's “Try it” changed with the path.

  2. (b) Same test, then find $U$.

    Answer

    $\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x}=0=\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}$: conservative. $U=-\int x\,dx+g(y)=-\frac{x^2}2+g(y)$, and $-\frac{\partial U}{\partial y}=-g'(y)=y$ gives $g=-\frac{y^2}2$. So $U=-\frac12(x^2+y^2)$, up to a constant.

  3. (c) Same.

    Answer

    $\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x}=2x=\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}$: conservative. $U=-\int2xy\,dx=-x^2y+g(y)$; $-\frac{\partial U}{\partial y}=x^2-g'(y)=x^2$, so $g$ is constant. $U=-x^2y$. Check: $-\nabla U=(2xy,\,x^2)$.

3. The important potential energies

Gravity near the ground (section 4.4.2.1, page 115). With $z$ up, $\vec F=(0,0,-mg)$, so $U(z)-U(z_1)=-\int_{z_1}^z(-mg)\,dz'$ $=mg(z-z_1)$:

$$U=mgz\quad(\text{zero at }z=0).$$

The spring (section 4.4.2.2, page 115). With the restoring force $F=-D(x-x_0)$ and the zero at the rest length $x_0$:

$$U(x)=\tfrac12D(x-x_0)^2,$$

a parabola: the potential of the harmonic oscillator (lessons 12–13). The script writes $k$ here; Rupp and the formula sheet write $D$.

The central field of gravity (section 4.4.2.4, pages 117–119). For two masses at distance $r$,

$$U(r)=-G\frac{m_1m_2}{r},\qquad\vec F=-\nabla U=-G\frac{m_1m_2}{r^2}\,\hat r.$$

The step in between is $\nabla\frac1r=-\frac{\nabla r}{r^2}$ with $\nabla r=\frac{\vec r}{r}=\hat r$. The zero is chosen at infinity, $U(\infty)=0$, so $U$ is negative everywhere: that's fine, only differences matter. Near the ground, with $r=R+z$ and $z\ll R$, it becomes $mgz$ plus a constant (Exercise 6 of lesson 5). Gravitation gets its own lesson 9.

The script also shows the potential of an ion in a salt crystal (section 4.4.2.3): strongly repulsive at short distance, attractive further out, with a minimum in between where the ion sits. Every chemical bond looks like this.

4. Reading a potential: force, equilibrium, turning points

The force is the downhill slope (section 4.4.3, page 119). In one dimension, $F_x=-\frac{dU}{dx}$. In two or three, $\vec F=-\nabla U$ points against the gradient and is perpendicular to the equipotential lines (lesson 5): picture a ball on a landscape of height $U$.

Equilibrium (section 4.4.4, page 122). The force vanishes where $U'(x)=0$. At a minimum the equilibrium is stable (a small push gives a force back), at a maximum unstable (labil: the force pushes further away), on a flat stretch neutral (indifferent).

Turning points. With energy conservation (next section), $T=E-U(x)\geq0$, so the body can only be where $U(x)\le E$. Where $U(x)=E$ it stops and turns back.

Try it: a ball in a potential landscape

The curve is a potential energy $U(x)$ with two valleys. Choose the total energy $E$ (the horizontal line). The ball moves without friction; the bar shows how $E$ splits into $U$ (orange) and $T$ (blue) right now.

Below the middle hump the ball is trapped in one valley and swings between two turning points. Above it, it runs across both. The tops and bottoms are the equilibria: stable in the valleys, unstable on the hump.

5. Conservation of mechanical energy

The energy theorem of mechanics (section 4.5.1, page 123). If only conservative forces do work, lesson 5's theorem says $W=T_2-T_1$, and section 2 says $W=U_1-U_2$. Equal them:

$$\tfrac12mv_1^2+U(\vec r_1)=\tfrac12mv_2^2+U(\vec r_2)=E.$$

The mechanical energy $E=T+U$ is a constant of the motion. (Forces that do no work, like the normal force of a smooth track or a string's tension, may be present: they add nothing to $W$.)

With non-conservative forces (page 124). Split the total force into conservative and non-conservative parts, $\vec F=\vec F_K+\vec F_{NK}$. Then $W_K+W_{NK}=\Delta T$ and $W_K=-\Delta U$, so

$$W_{NK}=\Delta T+\Delta U=\Delta E.$$

Rupp writes exactly this, $W_{NK}=\Delta T+\Delta U$. Friction does negative work, so it lowers $E$: the missing energy becomes heat. The full energy theorem (formula sheet, page 3) keeps track of it: $E=mc^2+T+U+Q+\ldots$ is constant in a closed system. One consequence: no machine can deliver work forever without an energy supply (no perpetual motion machine of the first kind).

Recipe: the energy method

  1. Pick two moments: one where you know everything (often the start), one you ask about.
  2. Choose the zero of $U$ (the lowest point is usually easiest). List $T_1,U_1,T_2,U_2$.
  3. Only conservative forces doing work? Write $T_1+U_1=T_2+U_2$. Friction? Write $T_1+U_1+W_{NK}=T_2+U_2$, with $W_{NK}=-F_R\,s$ for friction along the path length $s$.
  4. Solve for the unknown speed, height or distance. The mass usually cancels.
  5. Need a force (normal force, string tension) at a point of a curved path? Energy gives you $v$ there; then use Newton radially: $\sum F_{\text{towards centre}}=m\frac{v^2}R$.

Worked example 2: the ball in the loop (script, section 4.5.2)

A small ball slides without friction down a ramp from height $h$ and into a vertical circle of radius $R$, whose lowest point is at height $0$. What is the smallest $h$ for which it goes all the way round?

  1. At the top of the loop (point B, height $2R$), which forces act and what must they provide?

    Answer

    Gravity $mg$ and the track's normal force $N_B$, both pointing down, towards the centre. Together they must provide the centripetal force: $N_B+mg=m\frac{v_B^2}R$.

  2. What is the condition for just making it?

    Answer

    The track must still touch the ball: $N_B\geq0$. In the limiting case $N_B=0$, gravity alone provides the centripetal force: $mg=m\frac{v_B^2}R$, so $v_B^2=gR$.

  3. Use energy conservation between the start A and B.

    Answer

    $E_A=0+mgh$ and $E_B=\frac12mv_B^2+mg\cdot2R$ $=\frac12mgR+2mgR=\frac52mgR$. Setting them equal:

    $$h_{\min}=\tfrac52R.$$

    Independent of the mass and even of $g$. Starting from $2R$ is not enough, because the ball must still be moving at the top.

  4. How hard does the track push at the bottom, starting from $h_{\min}$?

    Answer

    At the bottom, $v_1^2=2gh=5gR$. Normal force up, gravity down: $N_1-mg=m\frac{v_1^2}R=5mg$, so $N_1=6mg$. The tumbler feels six times her weight: the reason real loops are not circles.

Try it: the tumbler's loop

Choose the start height in units of the loop radius $R$ and press Go. The cart slides without friction. The bars show $U$, $T$ and their sum.

Below $h=R$ the cart rolls back before the side of the loop. Between $R$ and $\frac52R$ the normal force reaches zero somewhere in the upper half: the cart leaves the track and falls. From $\frac52R$ on, it goes round.

Worked example 3: sliding with friction

A sledge of mass $m$ starts at rest at height $h$ on a slope of angle $\alpha$ and slides down with the sliding friction coefficient $\mu_G$. How fast is it at the bottom?

  1. How long is the slope, and what work does friction do?

    Answer

    The length is $s=\frac h{\sin\alpha}$. Friction $F_R=\mu_Gmg\cos\alpha$ opposes the motion all the way: $W_{NK}=-\mu_Gmg\cos\alpha\cdot\frac h{\sin\alpha}$ $=-\mu_Gmg\frac h{\tan\alpha}$.

  2. Write the energy balance and solve for $v$.

    Answer

    $0+mgh+W_{NK}=\frac12mv^2+0$, so

    $$v=\sqrt{2gh\left(1-\frac{\mu_G}{\tan\alpha}\right)}.$$

    It only starts at all if the static friction is overcome (lesson 3); if $\mu_G\ge\tan\alpha$ the formula would give no real speed: the sledge would stop.

Specific potentials (section 4.4.5, page 122). If a force is a coupling constant times a field, $\vec F=\gamma\vec f(\vec r)$ (for gravity $\gamma=m$), the script defines the specific potential $V=U/\gamma$: for gravity the potential energy per mass, $V=-\frac{GM}r$. One field then serves all bodies. You'll meet it again in lesson 9.

Why it's cool. In 1915 Emmy Noether proved why energy is conserved at all: because the laws of physics are the same today as tomorrow. Every symmetry of nature gives a conserved quantity: time gives energy, position gives momentum (lesson 2), direction gives angular momentum (lesson 7). Rupp starts her chapter on conservation laws with exactly this theorem.

6. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed.

Exercise 1 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.1(b), 1 point, original)

A force field $\vec F$ is conservative if (A) work is done along closed paths in the field; (B) the derivative of the force field leads to a potential; (C) line integrals of the field between any two points are independent of the chosen path; (D) the curl of the force field is $\nabla\times\vec F\neq0$.

Solution

C. Path independence is one of the four equivalent definitions. (A) The opposite: along closed paths the work is zero. (B) The other way round: the force is minus the gradient (a derivative) of the potential. (D) The opposite: the curl must vanish.

Exercise 2 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.3(a), 1 point, original)

A body moves in a non-conservative force field. Which is correct? (A) If start and end point are the same, the total work done is always zero. (B) The work done can depend on the chosen path. (C) The force field can be written as the gradient of a potential. (D) The work is computed as the integral of the force field over time.

Solution

B. Without a potential, nothing forces the work to be path-independent (the whirl, friction). (A) and (C) are properties of conservative fields. (D) The integral of force over time is the impulse (lesson 2); work is the integral over the path.

Exercise 3 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.3(c), 1 point, original)

A particle goes round a closed path and returns to its starting point. A sensor measures that the total work of all forces on the particle during the round trip was strictly positive ($W_{\text{ges}}>0$). Which is correct? (A) The force field must be purely conservative. (B) The potential energy of the particle is larger at the end than at the start. (C) The forces were exclusively sliding friction forces. (D) A non-conservative force must have acted.

Solution

D. Conservative forces do zero work on a closed path, so something non-conservative must have contributed. (A) Contradicts the zero closed-loop work. (B) Same point, same $U$ (if $U$ exists at all). (C) Sliding friction always does negative work; positive work needs a driving force (a motor, a whirl-like field).

Exercise 4 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.3(c), 1 point, original)

A body moves in a conservative force field along a closed path. Which statement is false? (A) The total energy in the system is conserved. (B) Potential energy can be converted into kinetic energy and back. (C) The potential energy of the body must be constant. (D) The total work done by the conservative field along the closed path is zero.

Solution

C is false. Along the way $U$ changes (a pendulum, a planet on an ellipse); only at the end is it back to its starting value. (A), (B), (D) are true statements about conservative fields.

Exercise 5 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.3(d), 1 point, original)

Which statement is correct? (A) Energy conservation requires that kinetic and potential energy are each conserved separately. (B) Kinetic energy is proportional to the speed. (C) The kinetic energy of a perfectly rolling ball does not depend on its radius. (D) The potential energy in a gravitational field always decreases with increasing distance.

Solution

C. A rolling ball has translational and rotational energy, $T=\frac12mv^2+\frac12I\omega^2$, with $I=\frac25mR^2$ and $\omega=\frac vR$ (lesson 11). So $T=\frac12mv^2+\frac15mv^2=\frac7{10}mv^2$: the radius cancels. (A) Only the sum is conserved. (B) $T\propto v^2$. (D) $U=-\frac{GMm}r$ increases (towards $0$) with distance.

Exercise 6 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.3(b), 1 point, original)

A mass is hung on an unstretched, vertical ideal spring. Released suddenly, the mass stretches the spring by $s_1$ at its lowest point. Lowered slowly into the static rest position, it stretches it by $s_2$. Which relation holds? (A) $s_1=s_2$ (B) $s_1=2s_2$ (C) $s_1=\sqrt2\,s_2$ (D) $s_1=\frac12s_2$

Solution

B. Static: the forces balance, $Ds_2=mg$, so $s_2=\frac{mg}D$. Sudden release: at the lowest point the mass is momentarily at rest again, so energy conservation from start to there gives $mgs_1=\frac12Ds_1^2$ (gravity's potential energy lost = spring energy gained), so $s_1=\frac{2mg}D=2s_2$. The mass overshoots the equilibrium and oscillates around $s_2$.

Exercise 7 (FS23, problem 2 “Newtonpendel”, part (a), 2 points, original)

A ball of mass $M$ hangs on a string of length $L$. It is pulled out to the angle $\varphi=\frac\pi2$ (string horizontal) and released. Find its speed $v_0$ at the lowest point, in terms of $L$ and $g$.

Solution

The string's tension is perpendicular to the motion and does no work; gravity is conservative. Zero of $U$ at the lowest point: at the start $T=0$, $U=MgL$; at the bottom $T=\frac12Mv_0^2$, $U=0$. So $MgL=\frac12Mv_0^2$ and

$$v_0=\sqrt{2gL}.$$

Exercise 8 (FS25, problem 2 “Halfpipe”, parts (a)–(d), 7 points, original)

A skateboarder (point mass $m$) is at rest at the highest point of a parabolic halfpipe of height $h$ and width $d$. The origin O is at the lowest point; the edges are at $x=\pm\frac d2$, height $h$. He slides down without friction or air drag.

(a) Give the shape $y(x)$ and his potential energy $V(x)$ with $V(0)=0$, using $d$ and $h$. [2 points] (b) Give the component $F_\parallel(x)$ of the weight tangential to the halfpipe in terms of the slope angle $\alpha(x)$. What is $\alpha(x)$? Hint: look at $y'(x)$. [1 point] (c) What are $F_\parallel(x)$ and $\alpha(x)$ for $x/d\ll1$? Compare $F_\parallel$ with $F_x=-\frac{dV}{dx}$. Use $\arctan\xi\approx\xi$ and $\sin\xi\approx\xi$. [2 points] (d) For $x/d\ll1$ the equation of motion is $m\ddot x=-\frac{dV}{dx}$. Solve it with $x(0)=x_0$, $\dot x(0)=0$. What kind of motion is it? [2 points]

Solution

(a) A parabola through the origin, $y=cx^2$, with $y(\frac d2)=h$: $c\frac{d^2}4=h$, so

$$y(x)=\frac{4h}{d^2}x^2,\qquad V(x)=mgy(x)=\frac{4mgh}{d^2}x^2.$$

(b) On a slope of angle $\alpha$, the weight's component along the surface is $mg\sin\alpha$, pointing downhill (lesson 3). The slope of the curve is $\tan\alpha=y'(x)=\frac{8h}{d^2}x$, so $\alpha(x)=\arctan\frac{8hx}{d^2}$ and $F_\parallel(x)=-mg\sin\alpha(x)$ (negative for $x>0$: back towards the middle).

(c) For $x/d\ll1$ the angle is small: $\alpha\approx\frac{8hx}{d^2}$ and $F_\parallel\approx-mg\alpha=-\frac{8mgh}{d^2}x$. And $F_x=-\frac{dV}{dx}=-\frac{8mgh}{d^2}x$: the same. Near the bottom the halfpipe is almost flat, so the tangential force is almost horizontal, and it is the force from the potential.

(d) $m\ddot x=-\frac{8mgh}{d^2}x$, i.e. $\ddot x+\omega^2x=0$ with $\omega=\frac{\sqrt{8gh}}d$. With the initial conditions:

$$x(t)=x_0\cos(\omega t),\qquad\omega=\frac{2\sqrt{2gh}}{d}.$$

A harmonic oscillation (lesson 12): every potential looks like a parabola near its minimum.

Exercise 9 (FS26, problem 2 “Der schnellste Weg”, parts (a) and (f), 3 points, original)

A point mass $m$ starts at rest at A $=(0,h)$ and reaches C $=(l,0)$. Path 1: a straight frictionless ramp from A to C. Path 2: free fall from A to B $=(0,0)$, then an infinitely small frictionless bend into the horizontal that keeps all its kinetic energy, then sliding to C. (a) Find the speeds $|v_1|$ and $|v_2|$ at C. [1 point] (f) Now from B on, path 2 has sliding friction with $|F_R|=\mu mg$. Find the braking deceleration and the distance $d$ from B until the mass stops. [2 points]

Solution

(a) No friction, and the ramp's and the bend's normal forces do no work. Both paths go down by $h$: $mgh=\frac12mv^2$, so $|v_1|=|v_2|=\sqrt{2gh}$. The paths differ in time (parts (b)–(e), lesson 1's kinematics), not in final speed.

(f) The friction force $\mu mg$ is the only horizontal force: deceleration $a=\mu g$. Energy: $\frac12mv^2-\mu mg\,d=0$ with $v^2=2gh$ gives $mgh=\mu mg\,d$, so

$$d=\frac h\mu.$$

Exercise 10 (FS23, problem 3 “Zylinder als Rampe”, parts (a), (c), (d), 9 points, original)

A large cylinder of radius $R$ is fixed to the ground. A small cube of mass $m$ and side $2r$ starts at rest at the very top ($\theta=0$, measured from the vertical at the cylinder's centre) and slides down clockwise without friction. Before it leaves the cylinder, its centre is at distance $R+r$ from the cylinder's axis. Neglect the cube's rotational energy.

(a) What is the cube's potential energy at angle $\theta$, before it leaves the cylinder? [2 points] (c) Write energy conservation between the start and the angle $\theta$, in terms of $\theta$ and the tangential speed $v_\theta$. [2 points] (d) Find the angle at which the cube leaves the cylinder, and its speed there. Show that $\frac{v_\theta^2}{R+r}=g\cos\theta$ at that angle. [5 points]

Solution

(a) Zero of $U$ at the height of the cylinder's axis: the cube's centre is at height $(R+r)\cos\theta$, so $U(\theta)=mg(R+r)\cos\theta$.

(c) At the start $T=0$ and $U=mg(R+r)$. The normal force does no work, there's no friction:

$$mg(R+r)=\tfrac12mv_\theta^2+mg(R+r)\cos\theta.$$

(d) The cube moves on a circle of radius $R+r$. Towards the centre act the component of gravity $mg\cos\theta$ and, outwards, the normal force $N$: $mg\cos\theta-N=m\frac{v_\theta^2}{R+r}$. The cube leaves when $N=0$:

$$\frac{v_\theta^2}{R+r}=g\cos\theta.$$

From (c), $v_\theta^2=2g(R+r)(1-\cos\theta)$. Insert: $2g(1-\cos\theta)=g\cos\theta$, so $\cos\theta=\frac23$, $\theta=\arccos\frac23\approx48^\circ$. The speed there is $v_\theta=\sqrt{\frac23g(R+r)}$.

The same two steps as the loop: energy for the speed, Newton radially for the normal force, then set $N=0$.

Scoring yourself: the exercises make 27 points. With 18 you're done. Exercises 8 and 10 are full exam problems: if you can do those, energy conservation is yours.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

The impossible waterfall. M. C. Escher's print “Waterfall” (1961) shows water that flows downhill all the way round a channel and still ends up on top, driving a mill wheel forever. Energy conservation says it must be a trick, and it is: the drawing combines three impossible “Penrose triangles”. A conservative force can't do net work around a loop, so no landscape of heights can do this.

Why the moon doesn't fall. The moon has $T$ and a negative $U=-\frac{GMm}r$, and its total energy is negative: it's bound, like the ball in a valley of the “Try it” above. To escape, a body needs $E\geq0$, which gives the escape velocity $11.2\,\mathrm{km/s}$ from the earth's surface. That's lesson 9.
