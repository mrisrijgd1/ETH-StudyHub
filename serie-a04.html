Aufgabe 1 · Examples on continuity

Find an example of

  1. an unbounded continuous function on a bounded interval;
  2. an unbounded continuous function on a closed interval;
  3. an unbounded function on a compact interval that is discontinuous in at most one point.

You don't need to give proofs.

The idea

The big theorem says: continuous on a compact (closed and bounded) interval implies bounded. Each example breaks exactly one of the three ingredients and shows the conclusion can then fail. Break “closed”: $(0,1)$. Break “bounded”: $[0,\infty)$. Break “continuous” at a single point: $\frac1x$ with any value at $0$.

Example 3: $f(x)=\frac1x$ for $x>0$ and $f(0)=0$ on $[0,1]$. Continuous everywhere except at $0$, and unbounded.

You need

Continuous functions on compact intervals are bounded (lesson 8, Proof B; script, page 165). This task is the script's exercise “Gegenbeispiele” on page 166.

Hint 1

Which function do you know that blows up near a point? Use it on an interval that doesn't contain that point.

Hint 2

“Closed” does not mean “bounded”: $[0,\infty)$ is closed. Which continuous function is unbounded there?

Hint 3

For 3, take $\frac1x$ on $(0,1]$ and give it any value at $0$ to get a function on $[0,1]$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Bounded but not closed: the endpoint where $\frac1x$ blows up is left out.

1. $f:(0,1)\to\mathbb R$, $f(x)=\frac1x$.

Closed but not bounded: the identity just keeps growing.

2. $f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$, $f(x)=x$. The interval is closed, but not bounded.

Compact, but one bad point.

3. $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb R$, $f(0)=0$ and $f(x)=\frac1x$ for $x>0$. It is discontinuous exactly at $0$.

Why these are the only ways: all three ingredients are needed.

A function that is continuous everywhere on a compact interval would be bounded. ∎

Trap

In 2, answering $\frac1x$ on $[0,1]$: it isn't defined at $0$, so it is not a function on $[0,1]$ at all. A function on a closed interval must have a value at every point, including the ends.

Aufgabe 2 · Discontinuity in predicate logic

Let $D\subseteq\mathbb R$ be non-empty and $f:D\to\mathbb R$. Write “$f$ is not continuous at $x_0\in D$” and “$f$ is not continuous” in predicate logic. Use this to show that $$f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R,\quad f(x)=\begin{cases}x+1,&x\ge0,\\x,&x<0\end{cases}$$ is not continuous.

The idea

Continuity at $x_0$ is a game: for every tolerance $\varepsilon$ the defender finds a window $\delta$. To negate it, swap every quantifier and negate the end: there is one bad tolerance $\varepsilon_0$ such that every window contains a point that misses it. For the given $f$, the jump at $0$ has height $1$, so $\varepsilon_0=1$ is bad: points just left of $0$ have values near $0$, while $f(0)=1$.

Left of $0$ the values are close to $0$, a distance of about $1$ from $f(0)=1$, however small the window.

You need

The ε-δ definition of continuity at $x_0$ (script, page 153; lesson 7): $$\begin{aligned}&\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x\in D:\\&\quad|x-x_0|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$ Negating quantifiers and implications (script, pages 11–15; lesson 1): $\neg(A\Rightarrow B)\iff A\wedge\neg B$.

Hint 1

Negate from the outside in: $\forall\varepsilon$ becomes $\exists\varepsilon_0$, $\exists\delta$ becomes $\forall\delta$, $\forall x$ becomes $\exists x$, and the implication becomes “and not”.

Hint 2

“$f$ is not continuous” means “there is a point where it is not continuous”: put $\exists x_0\in D$ in front. For the example: try $x_0=0$ and $\varepsilon_0=1$.

Hint 3

Given any $\delta>0$, take $x=-\frac\delta2$. Compute $|f(x)-f(0)|=|-\frac\delta2-1|$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Swap every quantifier; the implication “close ⇒ close” becomes “close and far”.

Not continuous at $x_0$: $$\begin{aligned}&\exists\varepsilon_0>0\ \forall\delta>0\ \exists x\in D:\\&\quad|x-x_0|<\delta\\&\quad\wedge\ |f(x)-f(x_0)|\ge\varepsilon_0.\end{aligned}$$

Not continuous at all means: at least one bad point (not: bad at every point).

Not continuous: $$\begin{aligned}&\exists x_0\in D\ \exists\varepsilon_0>0\\&\forall\delta>0\ \exists x\in D:\\&\quad|x-x_0|<\delta\\&\quad\wedge\ |f(x)-f(x_0)|\ge\varepsilon_0.\end{aligned}$$

Now play the attacker against the example: the bad point is the jump, the bad tolerance is the jump height.

The example. Let $x_0:=0$ and $\varepsilon_0:=1$; note $f(0)=1$.

Whatever window the defender picks, step half-way into it on the left side.

Let $\delta>0$ and $x:=-\frac\delta2<0$. Then $|x-0|=\frac\delta2<\delta$ and $$\begin{aligned}|f(x)-f(0)|&=\Big|-\frac\delta2-1\Big|\\&=1+\frac\delta2\ge1=\varepsilon_0.\end{aligned}$$

This is exactly the negated statement, so $f$ fails at $0$.

So the statement “not continuous at $x_0$” holds for $x_0=0$. Hence $f$ is not continuous at $0$, and therefore not continuous on $\mathbb R$. ∎

Trap

Negating “$<\varepsilon$” as “$>\varepsilon$”: the negation is “$\ge\varepsilon_0$”. And choosing $\delta$ yourself: in the negation, $\delta$ is given to you (“for all $\delta$”), you only choose $\varepsilon_0$ and $x$.

Aufgabe 3 · Binomial coefficients

For $n,k\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb N$ with $k\le n$, $\binom nk:=\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$. We also set $\binom nr=0$ for integers $r<0$ or $r>n$; an empty sum is $0$.

  1. Prove the combinatorial interpretation: $\binom nk$ is the number of ways to choose $k$ out of $n$ objects (without repetition, order irrelevant), i.e. the number of $k$-element subsets of an $n$-element set.
  2. Let $k,m,n\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb N$. Find combinatorial proofs of $$\sum_{j=0}^k\binom mj\binom n{k-j}=\binom{m+n}k,$$ $$\sum_{j=k}^n\binom jk\binom nj=\binom nk2^{n-k}.$$

(The sheet shows the model: $\binom{n+1}k=\binom nk+\binom n{k-1}$ by counting choices of $k$ balls out of $n$ blue and $1$ red, split by whether the red ball is chosen.)

The idea

A combinatorial proof counts one set of objects in two ways. Both answers count the same thing, so they are equal. For the first identity: choose a team of $k$ from $m$ women and $n$ men; sort the teams by how many women ($j$) they contain. For the second: choose a club $T$ of the $n$ people and a committee $S$ of $k$ inside the club. Either pick the club first, or pick the committee first and then decide for each of the other $n-k$ people whether they join the club.

You need

Binomial coefficients and their properties: script, pages 141–143; the combinatorial meaning is the script's exercise on page 143. Counting rules: if a set splits into disjoint cases, add the counts; if a choice is made in independent stages, multiply.

Hint 1

1: First count ordered choices of $k$ different elements: $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)$. How many ordered lists give the same subset? 2, first identity: take disjoint sets $M$, $N$ with $|M|=m$, $|N|=n$ and count $k$-subsets of $M\cup N$.

Hint 2

1: each $k$-subset appears $k!$ times as an ordered list. Don't forget $k=0$ and $k>n$. First identity: a subset with exactly $j$ elements from $M$ has $k-j$ from $N$, giving $\binom mj\binom n{k-j}$ subsets. Second identity: count pairs $(S,T)$ with $S\subseteq T\subseteq U$, $|U|=n$, $|S|=k$.

Hint 3

Second identity: $T$ first: $|T|=j$ gives $\binom nj$ choices of $T$ and then $\binom jk$ choices of $S$; sum over $j$. $S$ first: $\binom nk$ choices, then each of the $n-k$ elements outside $S$ is in $T$ or not: $2^{n-k}$.

Solution

1. The interpretation.

Why this stepWhat you write

Ordered lists are easy to count: $n$ choices for the first, $n-1$ for the second, and so on.

Let $U$ have $n$ elements and $1\le k\le n$. The number of ordered choices of $k$ different elements of $U$ is $$\begin{aligned}&n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\\&\quad=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}.\end{aligned}$$

Forget the order: each subset was counted once per arrangement, i.e. $k!$ times.

Each $k$-element subset is counted exactly $k!$ times, once for each ordering of its elements. So the number of $k$-element subsets is $\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}=\binom nk$.

Edge cases match the conventions.

For $k=0$ there is exactly one subset, $\emptyset$, and $\binom n0=1$. For $k>n$ there is none, matching $\binom nk=0$.

2. First identity (Vandermonde).

One set of objects: $k$-subsets of a union of two disjoint groups. Count it directly.

Let $M,N$ be disjoint with $|M|=m$, $|N|=n$. By part 1, $M\cup N$ has $\binom{m+n}k$ subsets with $k$ elements.

Count again, sorted by how many elements come from $M$. The cases don't overlap and cover everything, so add.

A $k$-subset with exactly $j$ elements from $M$ has $k-j$ from $N$; there are $\binom mj\binom n{k-j}$ such subsets. Every $k$-subset falls into exactly one case $j\in\{0,\dots,k\}$, so $$\sum_{j=0}^k\binom mj\binom n{k-j}=\binom{m+n}k.$$ Impossible cases contribute $0$ by the zero convention (this also covers $k>m+n$).

2. Second identity.

One set of objects: a club $T$ with a committee $S$ inside it.

Let $|U|=n$ and $0\le k\le n$. We count pairs $(S,T)$ with $S\subseteq T\subseteq U$ and $|S|=k$.

Club first: choose its size $j$, then the club, then the committee inside.

Choosing $T$ with $|T|=j$ first and then $S\subseteq T$ gives $\binom nj\binom jk$ pairs for fixed $j$; summing over $j=k,\dots,n$ gives the left side.

Committee first: then each remaining person independently joins the club or not.

Choosing $S$ first gives $\binom nk$ choices; each of the $n-k$ elements of $U\setminus S$ is in $T$ or not, independently, giving $2^{n-k}$ sets $T$. Hence $$\sum_{j=k}^n\binom jk\binom nj=\binom nk2^{n-k}.$$

The leftover case.

For $k>n$ the left sum is empty and $\binom nk=0$, so both sides are $0$. ∎

Trap

Writing two counts that count different things. Name the set you count in one sentence (“pairs $(S,T)$ with $S\subseteq T\subseteq U$, $|S|=k$”) before counting it twice. And say why the cases are disjoint and cover everything; that is what allows you to add.

Aufgabe 4 · Divisibility and composition of polynomials

Let $K$ be a field and $A,B,P\in K[T]$, with $P$ not constant. Consider $(A\circ P)(T):=A(P(T))$ and $(B\circ P)(T):=B(P(T))$. Suppose $A\circ P\mid B\circ P$. Show that $A\mid B$. (Here $F\mid G$ means $G=FQ$ for some $Q\in K[T]$.)

The idea

Divide $B$ by $A$ with remainder: $B=AQ+R$ with $R$ small (degree below $\deg A$). Plugging in $P$ keeps this structure, and multiplies all degrees by $\deg P$. Now $A\circ P$ divides $B\circ P$ and $(A\circ P)(Q\circ P)$, so it divides $R\circ P$. But $R\circ P$ has smaller degree than $A\circ P$, and a non-zero polynomial can't be divided by one of bigger degree. So $R=0$.

You need

Polynomials, degree, and division with remainder (script, pages 133–137): for $A\ne0$ there are $Q,R$ with $B=AQ+R$ and $R=0$ or $\deg R<\deg A$. In a field, the leading coefficient of a product is the product of the leading coefficients, so $\deg(FH)=\deg F+\deg H$ for $F,H\ne0$.

Hint 1

First prove a helper fact: for $F\ne0$, $F\circ P\ne0$ and $\deg(F\circ P)=\deg F\cdot\deg P$. Look at the leading term of $F(P(T))$.

Hint 2

Treat $A=0$ separately. For $A\ne0$, divide: $B=AQ+R$. Plug in $P$: $B\circ P=(A\circ P)(Q\circ P)+R\circ P$. Conclude $A\circ P\mid R\circ P$.

Hint 3

If $R\ne0$: $\deg(R\circ P)=\deg R\cdot d$, which is $<\deg A\cdot d=\deg(A\circ P)$. But if $G=FH\ne0$ then $\deg G\ge\deg F$. Contradiction, so $R=0$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Helper fact: plugging $P$ into $F$ multiplies the degree by $d=\deg P$, because the top term survives (no zero divisors in a field).

Let $d:=\deg P\ge1$. For every $F\ne0$ in $K[T]$: $$\begin{aligned}&F\circ P\ne0\quad\text{and}\\&\deg(F\circ P)=(\deg F)\,d.\quad(1)\end{aligned}$$ Indeed, if $m=\deg F$ and $a_m$, $p_d$ are the leading coefficients of $F$ and $P$, then $F(P(T))$ has leading term $a_mp_d^{\,m}T^{md}$ with $a_mp_d^{\,m}\ne0$; all other terms have smaller degree. (This also holds for $m=0$.)

Degenerate case: if $A=0$, divisibility by $0$ forces $B\circ P=0$, hence $B=0$.

Case $A=0$. Then $0\mid B\circ P$ means $B\circ P=0$, so $B=0$ by (1), and $A\mid B$ since $0=0\cdot0$.

Main case: divide with remainder and plug in $P$; composition respects sums and products.

Case $A\ne0$. Division with remainder gives $Q,R\in K[T]$ with $B=AQ+R$, where $R=0$ or $\deg R<\deg A$. Substituting $P$: $$B\circ P=(A\circ P)(Q\circ P)+R\circ P.$$

$A\circ P$ divides both $B\circ P$ and the first summand, hence their difference.

Since $A\circ P\mid B\circ P$, also $A\circ P\mid R\circ P$, because $$\begin{aligned}R\circ P=\ &B\circ P\\&-(A\circ P)(Q\circ P).\end{aligned}$$

If $R\ne0$, the degrees don't fit: the divisor is bigger than what it divides.

Suppose $R\ne0$. By (1), $R\circ P\ne0$ and $$\begin{aligned}\deg(R\circ P)&=(\deg R)d\\&<(\deg A)d\\&=\deg(A\circ P).\end{aligned}$$ But if $G=FH\ne0$, then $\deg G=\deg F+\deg H\ge\deg F$. So $A\circ P$ cannot divide $R\circ P$, a contradiction.

So the remainder is zero.

Hence $R=0$ and $B=AQ$, i.e. $A\mid B$. ∎

Trap

“Substituting $T$ for $P$” backwards, as if $P$ had an inverse. It need not. The remainder trick avoids that. Also: the degree formula (1) needs $P$ non-constant ($d\ge1$); for constant $P$ the statement is false (take $P=1$, $A=T$, $B=T+1$: then $A\circ P=1$ divides $B\circ P=2$, but $T\nmid T+1$).

Aufgabe 5 · Dense subsets

Let $X\subseteq\mathbb R$. A subset $D\subseteq X$ is called dense in $X$ if for every open set $O\subseteq\mathbb R$ with $O\cap X\ne\emptyset$ also $O\cap D\ne\emptyset$. Show that the following are equivalent:

  1. $D$ is dense in $X$.
  2. Every point of $X\setminus D$ is an accumulation point of $D$.
  3. Every closed subset of $\mathbb R$ that contains $D$ also contains $X$.
The idea

Three ways to say “$D$ is everywhere in $X$”: every open window that sees $X$ also sees $D$; every missing point of $X$ is approached by $D$; you can't fence in $D$ with a closed set without also fencing in all of $X$. Prove it as a circle $1\Rightarrow2\Rightarrow3\Rightarrow1$. Each arrow uses one definition: open windows, accumulation points, or “closed = complement is open”.

You need

Open and closed sets (script, page 101; lesson 5); accumulation point: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $d\in D$ with $0<|d-x|<\varepsilon$ (script, page 113; lesson 6). Open intervals $(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)$ are open. The script defines density in general metric spaces on page 484.

Hint 1

$1\Rightarrow2$: for $x\in X\setminus D$ and $\varepsilon>0$, use $O=(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)$. $2\Rightarrow3$ and $3\Rightarrow1$: argue by contradiction.

Hint 2

$2\Rightarrow3$: if $F\supseteq D$ is closed and some $x\in X$ is not in $F$, then $x\notin D$, so $x$ is an accumulation point of $D$; but $\mathbb R\setminus F$ is open around $x$. $3\Rightarrow1$: if $O\cap D=\emptyset$, look at $F=\mathbb R\setminus O$.

Hint 3

$2\Rightarrow3$: the window $(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq\mathbb R\setminus F$ contains no point of $D$, contradicting “accumulation point”. $3\Rightarrow1$: $F=\mathbb R\setminus O$ is closed and contains $D$, so it contains $X$, i.e. $O\cap X=\emptyset$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

$1\Rightarrow2$: a small open window around $x$ sees $X$ (it contains $x$), so it sees $D$; the point found is not $x$ itself because $x\notin D$.

(1)⇒(2). Let $x\in X\setminus D$ and $\varepsilon>0$. The open set $O:=(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)$ meets $X$, since $x\in O\cap X$. By density there is $d\in O\cap D$. Since $x\notin D$, $d\ne x$, so $0<|d-x|<\varepsilon$. Hence $x$ is an accumulation point of $D$.

$2\Rightarrow3$: a point of $X$ outside the fence would be approached by $D$, but it has a $D$-free window around it.

(2)⇒(3). Let $F\subseteq\mathbb R$ be closed with $D\subseteq F$. Suppose there is $x\in X\setminus F$. Then $x\notin D$, so by (2) $x$ is an accumulation point of $D$. Since $\mathbb R\setminus F$ is open and contains $x$, there is $\varepsilon>0$ with $(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq\mathbb R\setminus F$. This interval contains no point of $D\subseteq F$, a contradiction. So $X\subseteq F$.

$3\Rightarrow1$: if a window missed $D$, its complement would be a closed fence around $D$ that misses part of $X$.

(3)⇒(1). Let $O\subseteq\mathbb R$ be open with $O\cap X\ne\emptyset$. If $O\cap D=\emptyset$, then the closed set $F:=\mathbb R\setminus O$ contains $D$. By (3), $X\subseteq F$, i.e. $O\cap X=\emptyset$, a contradiction. So $O\cap D\ne\emptyset$, and $D$ is dense in $X$. ∎

Trap

In $1\Rightarrow2$, forgetting to show $d\ne x$. An accumulation point needs points at distance strictly greater than $0$; here that comes for free from $x\notin D$, but you have to say it.

Aufgabe 6 · Monotonicity and intermediate values

  1. Let $I$ be an interval and $f:I\to\mathbb R$ monotone with this property: for all $a,b\in I$ and every number $\xi$ between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ there is $x$ between $a$ and $b$ with $f(x)=\xi$. Show that $f$ is continuous. Compare this with the intermediate value theorem.
  2. Show by a counterexample that 1 fails without monotonicity: give a function on an interval with this intermediate value property that is not continuous, and justify both properties.
The idea

A monotone function can only be discontinuous by jumping, and a jump skips all the values in between. The intermediate value property forbids skipping values, so there are no jumps. Concretely: to keep $f(x)$ within $\varepsilon$ of $f(x_0)$, find a point $u$ left of $x_0$ whose value is already within $\varepsilon$ (the intermediate value property hands you one), and the same on the right; monotonicity traps everything in between. Without monotonicity, $\sin\frac1x$ oscillates so wildly near $0$ that it takes every value in $[-1,1]$ in any window, yet has no limit at $0$.

$f(x)=\sin\frac1x$ for $x>0$, $f(0)=0$: every window $[0,b]$ contains a full swing from $-1$ to $1$.

You need

The ε-δ definition (script, page 153), monotone functions (script, page 150), the intermediate value theorem (script, page 159; lesson 7, Proof A). For 2: $\sin$ is continuous and $\sin(\frac\pi2+2\pi n)=1$, $\sin(\frac{3\pi}2+2\pi n)=-1$.

Hint 1

1: Assume $f$ is increasing (for decreasing, use $-f$). Fix $x_0$ and $\varepsilon$. Find $u<x_0$ with $f(u)>f(x_0)-\varepsilon$ and $v>x_0$ with $f(v)<f(x_0)+\varepsilon$ (where such sides exist in $I$).

Hint 2

Take any $a\in I$ with $a<x_0$. If $f(a)>f(x_0)-\varepsilon$, use $u=a$. Otherwise $f(x_0)-\frac\varepsilon2$ lies between $f(a)$ and $f(x_0)$, so the property gives $u$ with $f(u)=f(x_0)-\frac\varepsilon2$. Then $\delta:=\min(x_0-u,v-x_0)$.

Hint 3

2: $f(x)=\sin\frac1x$ for $x\in(0,1]$, $f(0)=0$. Points $u_n=\frac1{\pi/2+2\pi n}$ and $v_n=\frac1{3\pi/2+2\pi n}$ go to $0$ with $f(u_n)=1$, $f(v_n)=-1$. For the property with $a=0$, apply the intermediate value theorem on $[v_n,u_n]$.

Solution

1. Let $f$ be increasing; continuity is meant relative to $I$ (one-sided at endpoints that belong to $I$). Let $x_0\in I$ and $\varepsilon>0$.

Why this stepWhat you write

Left of $x_0$: find a point whose value is already close. Either a given point works, or the intermediate value property produces one.

Left comparison point. If there is $a\in I$ with $a<x_0$: if $f(a)>f(x_0)-\varepsilon$, set $u:=a$. Otherwise $\eta:=f(x_0)-\frac\varepsilon2$ lies strictly between $f(a)$ and $f(x_0)$, and the property gives $u\in(a,x_0)\subseteq I$ with $f(u)=\eta$. In both cases $$u<x_0,\qquad f(u)>f(x_0)-\varepsilon.$$

Same on the right.

Right comparison point. If there is $b\in I$ with $b>x_0$: set $v:=b$ if $f(b)<f(x_0)+\varepsilon$; otherwise the property gives $v\in(x_0,b)$ with $f(v)=f(x_0)+\frac\varepsilon2$. In both cases $v>x_0$ and $f(v)<f(x_0)+\varepsilon$.

Choose the window smaller than both distances. Monotonicity squeezes every value in the window between $f(u)$ and $f(v)$.

Choose $\delta>0$ smaller than the existing distances $x_0-u$ and $v-x_0$. Let $x\in I$ with $|x-x_0|<\delta$. If $x<x_0$, then $u<x$, and $$f(x_0)-\varepsilon<f(u)\le f(x)\le f(x_0).$$ If $x>x_0$, then $x<v$, and $$f(x_0)\le f(x)\le f(v)<f(x_0)+\varepsilon.$$ So $|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ (trivially also for $x=x_0$), and $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.

Decreasing functions: flip the sign.

If $f$ is decreasing, $-f$ is increasing and also has the property, so $-f$, hence $f$, is continuous.

The comparison: the IVT is the converse direction.

Comparison. The intermediate value theorem says: continuous on an interval ⇒ intermediate value property (no monotonicity needed). Here we proved the converse for monotone $f$. So for monotone functions on an interval, continuity and the intermediate value property are equivalent.

2. Let $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb R$, $f(x)=\sin\frac1x$ for $x>0$, $f(0)=0$.

Points where $f$ is $1$ and $-1$, arbitrarily close to $0$.

For $n\ge0$ let $u_n:=\frac1{\pi/2+2\pi n}$, $v_n:=\frac1{3\pi/2+2\pi n}$. Then $0<v_n<u_n\le1$, $f(u_n)=1$, $f(v_n)=-1$, and $u_n<r$ for $n$ large, for every $r>0$.

Not continuous at $0$: every window contains a point with value $1$, far from $f(0)=0$.

Not continuous at $0$: let $\varepsilon_0:=\frac12$. For every $\delta>0$ choose $n$ with $u_n<\delta$; then $|f(u_n)-f(0)|=1\ge\varepsilon_0$.

Away from $0$, $f$ is continuous, so the IVT works there.

Intermediate value property: let $0\le a<b\le1$ and $\xi$ between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$. If $a>0$, $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and the IVT gives $x\in[a,b]$ with $f(x)=\xi$.

At $a=0$: a full swing from $-1$ to $1$ fits inside $(0,b)$, and $\xi\in[-1,1]$ anyway.

If $a=0$, choose $n$ with $u_n<b$. Then $[v_n,u_n]\subseteq(0,b)$, $f$ is continuous there with $f(v_n)=-1$, $f(u_n)=1$, so by the IVT $f$ takes every value of $[-1,1]$ there, in particular $\xi$ (as $f$ only takes values in $[-1,1]$). The case $a=b$ is trivial, and $a>b$ is the same with the roles swapped. ∎

$f$ is not monotone either: $f(v_1)=-1$, $f(u_1)=1$, $f(v_0)=-1$ at $v_1<u_1<v_0$.

Trap

In 1, assuming $x_0$ has points on both sides. At an endpoint of $I$ only one side exists, and you need only that side. In 2, checking the property only for intervals away from $0$: the interesting case is exactly $a=0$.
