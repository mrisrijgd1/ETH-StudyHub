Aufgabe 1 · Integral of the sign function

Let $a,b\in\mathbb R$ with $a<b$. Compute $\displaystyle\int_a^b\operatorname{sgn}(x)\,dx$ in terms of $a$ and $b$, where $\operatorname{sgn}(x)=1$ for $x>0$, $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$, $\operatorname{sgn}(x)=-1$ for $x<0$.

The idea

$\operatorname{sgn}$ is a step function: $-1$ on the left of $0$, $+1$ on the right. Its integral is “signed length”: the length of $[a,b]$ to the right of $0$ minus the length to the left of $0$. The single value at $0$ doesn't matter. Three cases depending on where $0$ sits, and they all collapse into $|b|-|a|$.

Case $a<0<b$: the integral is $b-|a|=a+b$.

You need

Step functions and their integral (script, pages 178–181; lesson 9): on a partition, the integral is $\sum c_k(x_k-x_{k-1})$, where $c_k$ is the value on the open piece $(x_{k-1},x_k)$; values at partition points don't matter. A step function is Riemann integrable with the same integral (that is Aufgabe 2).

Hint 1

Is $\operatorname{sgn}$ restricted to $[a,b]$ a step function? Which partition works? Distinguish $b\le0$, $a\ge0$ and $a<0<b$.

Hint 2

For $a<0<b$ take the partition $a<0<b$: value $-1$ on $(a,0)$, value $1$ on $(0,b)$.

Hint 3

$-1\cdot(0-a)+1\cdot(b-0)=a+b$. Now check that $|b|-|a|$ gives $a-b$, $b-a$, $a+b$ in the three cases.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

$\operatorname{sgn}$ is a step function, so its Riemann integral is its step integral (Aufgabe 2), and single values don't count.

The restriction of $\operatorname{sgn}$ to $[a,b]$ is a step function, so it is Riemann integrable and its integral is the step-function integral (Aufgabe 2). Values at partition points do not affect it.

All on one side of $0$: constant $-1$ or constant $+1$ on the open interval.

If $b\le0$: $\operatorname{sgn}=-1$ on $(a,b)$, so the integral is $-(b-a)=a-b$. If $a\ge0$: $\operatorname{sgn}=1$ on $(a,b)$, so it is $b-a$.

$0$ inside: split there.

If $a<0<b$: with the partition $a<0<b$ the integral is $-(0-a)+(b-0)=a+b$.

One formula for all three cases.

In all three cases $$\int_a^b\operatorname{sgn}(x)\,dx=|b|-|a|.$$ (Check: $b\le0$ gives $-b+a$; $a\ge0$ gives $b-a$; $a<0<b$ gives $b+a$.) ∎

Trap

Using an antiderivative ($|x|$) and the fundamental theorem. At this point of the course that theorem isn't available, and $\operatorname{sgn}$ isn't continuous anyway. Work with the step-function definition.

Aufgabe 2 · Step functions are Riemann integrable

Let $a<b$ and $t\in TF([a,b])$ be a step function. Show that $t$ is Riemann integrable and that its Riemann integral equals its integral as a step function.

The idea

The Riemann integral squeezes $f$ between step functions from below and above. If $f$ is itself a step function, it can be its own lower and upper step function. So the number $J(t)$ is both a lower sum and an upper sum, and every lower sum is $\le J(t)\le$ every upper sum. The squeeze closes exactly at $J(t)$.

You need

Definitions of $U(f)$, $O(f)$, lower and upper integral, and Riemann integrability (script, page 183; lesson 9). Monotonicity of the step integral: $u\le o\Rightarrow J(u)\le J(o)$ (script, page 182).

Hint 1

Is $t$ bounded? Can you use $u=t$ in $U(t)$ and $o=t$ in $O(t)$?

Hint 2

So $J(t)\in U(t)\cap O(t)$. For any admissible $u\le t\le o$, what does monotonicity give?

Hint 3

$J(t)$ is the largest element of $U(t)$ and the smallest of $O(t)$. A largest element is the supremum, a smallest element is the infimum.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

The definition needs a bounded function; step functions take finitely many values.

$t$ is bounded, since it takes only finitely many values.

$t$ is its own lower and upper step function.

Since $t\le t\le t$ with $t\in TF([a,b])$, we have $J(t)\in U(t)$ and $J(t)\in O(t)$.

Monotonicity of the step integral: everything below $t$ integrates to at most $J(t)$, everything above to at least $J(t)$.

For all step functions $u\le t\le o$, monotonicity of the step integral gives $J(u)\le J(t)\le J(o)$.

A largest element is the supremum; a smallest one is the infimum.

So $J(t)$ is the largest element of $U(t)$ and the smallest element of $O(t)$. Hence $$\begin{aligned}\underline I(t)&=\sup U(t)=J(t)\\&=\inf O(t)=\overline I(t).\end{aligned}$$

Lower = upper is the definition of integrable.

By definition, $t$ is Riemann integrable and $\int_a^bt(x)\,dx=J(t)$. ∎

Trap

Writing “obviously the integral is the same”. The point of the exercise is that two different definitions agree; you have to go through $U(t)$ and $O(t)$. The key word is monotonicity of the step integral.

Aufgabe 3 · Modified Dirichlet function

Show that $g:[0,1]\to[0,1]$, $g(x)=0$ if $x$ is irrational and $g(x)=\frac1q$ if $x=\frac pq$ with $p,q$ coprime, is Riemann integrable.

The idea

The graph is a dust of points: tall points only at fractions with small denominators, and there are only finitely many of those. Above height $\frac1N$ there are finitely many points; cover each by a very thin tower, and put a low floor of height $\frac1N$ everywhere else. That upper step function has tiny integral. Since $0\le g$, the lower integral is $\ge0$, so both integrals are squeezed to $0$.

Points $(\frac pq,\frac1q)$ for $q\le12$. Only finitely many points lie above the dashed line at height $\frac1N$.

You need

Riemann integrability via lower/upper integrals (script, page 183), and the criterion: integrable iff for every $\varepsilon>0$ there are step functions $u\le g\le o$ with $J(o-u)<\varepsilon$ (script, page 184; lesson 9, Proof B). This task is the script's exercise on page 192. Compare the Dirichlet function, which is not integrable (lesson 9, Proof C).

Hint 1

$u=0$ is a lower step function, so $\underline I(g)\ge0$. It is enough to find, for every $\varepsilon>0$, an upper step function $o_\varepsilon\ge g$ with $J(o_\varepsilon)<\varepsilon$.

Hint 2

Pick $N$ with $\frac1N<\frac\varepsilon2$. The set $E_N$ of fractions in $[0,1]$ with reduced denominator $\le N$ is finite, say $m$ points. Outside $E_N$, $g\le\frac1N$.

Hint 3

$o_\varepsilon:=\frac1N+\sum_{j=1}^m\mathbb 1_{I_j}$, with $I_j$ a closed interval of length $\le2\eta$ around the $j$-th point of $E_N$, where $\eta=\frac\varepsilon{4m}$. Then $J(o_\varepsilon)\le\frac1N+2m\eta<\varepsilon$.

Solution

Write fractions in lowest terms with positive denominator; in particular $g(0)=g(1)=1$, and $0\le g\le1$.

Why this stepWhat you write

The floor: $0\le g$ gives a lower bound for the lower integral, and lower $\le$ upper always.

The zero function is a step function with $0\le g$, so $0\in U(g)$ and $0\le\underline I(g)\le\overline I(g)$. It suffices to construct, for every $\varepsilon>0$, a step function $o_\varepsilon\ge g$ with $J(o_\varepsilon)<\varepsilon$.

Only finitely many points are “tall”: those with denominator at most $N$.

Let $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $N\in\mathbb N$ with $\frac1N<\frac\varepsilon2$. Let $E_N$ be the set of $\frac pq\in[0,1]$ in lowest terms with $q\le N$. It is finite (for each $q\le N$ only $p=0,\dots,q$ are possible); write $E_N=\{r_1,\dots,r_m\}$. Outside $E_N$, $g(x)\le\frac1N$.

Thin towers of height 1 over the tall points, a floor of height $\frac1N$ everywhere.

Let $\eta:=\frac\varepsilon{4m}$, let $I_j$ be the closed interval $[r_j-\eta,r_j+\eta]\cap[0,1]$ (length $\ell_j\le2\eta$), and $$o_\varepsilon:=\frac1N+\sum_{j=1}^m\mathbb 1_{I_j}.$$ It is constant between the finitely many interval endpoints, so it is a step function.

Check it really lies above $g$ everywhere.

For $x\in E_N$, some indicator is $1$, so $o_\varepsilon(x)\ge1\ge g(x)$. For $x\notin E_N$, $o_\varepsilon(x)\ge\frac1N\ge g(x)$.

Its integral is floor area plus the towers' widths: small.

By linearity of the step integral and $J(\mathbb 1_{I_j})=\ell_j$: $$\begin{aligned}J(o_\varepsilon)&=\frac1N+\sum_{j=1}^m\ell_j\le\frac1N+2m\eta\\&<\frac\varepsilon2+\frac\varepsilon2=\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$

Squeeze: both integrals are between $0$ and any $\varepsilon$.

Since $J(o_\varepsilon)\in O(g)$, for every $\varepsilon>0$: $0\le\underline I(g)\le\overline I(g)\le J(o_\varepsilon)<\varepsilon$. Hence $\underline I(g)=\overline I(g)=0$: $g$ is Riemann integrable with $\int_0^1g(x)\,dx=0$. ∎

Note. The intervals $I_j$ may overlap; that's fine, since we use the sum of indicators, not the indicator of the union.

Trap

Arguing “$g=0$ except on the rationals, which are countable, so the integral is $0$”. Countability alone proves nothing here: the Dirichlet function is also $\ne0$ only on the rationals and is not integrable. What matters is that only finitely many points are above each height $\frac1N$.

Aufgabe 4 · Infinitely many preimages

Let $f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ be continuous and surjective.

  1. Show that $0$ has infinitely many preimages.
  2. More generally, show that every real number has infinitely many preimages.
The idea

On any piece $[0,T]$, a continuous $f$ is bounded, say by $M_T$. But $f$ is onto, so it must reach $M_T+1$ and $-(M_T+1)$ somewhere, and both places lie beyond $T$. Between them the graph crosses $0$, again beyond $T$. Since $T$ was arbitrary, the zeros go on forever.

You need

A continuous function on a compact interval is bounded and attains its maximum (script, pages 165–166; lesson 8, Proof C), and the intermediate value theorem (script, page 159; lesson 7, Proof A).

Hint 1

It is enough to show: for every $T\ge0$ there is a zero $c>T$. Why does that give infinitely many zeros?

Hint 2

Let $M_T:=\max_{[0,T]}|f|$. By surjectivity there are $u,v$ with $f(u)=M_T+1$ and $f(v)=-(M_T+1)$. Where can $u$ and $v$ not be?

Hint 3

$u,v>T$; apply the IVT between $u$ and $v$. For part 2, apply part 1 to $g=f-y$.

Solution
Why this stepWhat you write

Strategy: zeros beyond every $T$ means the zero set is unbounded, hence infinite.

1. We show: for every $T\ge0$ there is $c>T$ with $f(c)=0$. Then the zero set is unbounded above, hence infinite.

On a compact piece, $|f|$ has a maximum.

Let $T\ge0$. Since $|f|$ is continuous on the compact interval $[0,T]$, $M_T:=\max_{x\in[0,T]}|f(x)|$ exists.

Surjectivity gives values that are too big and too small for $[0,T]$, so they occur beyond $T$.

By surjectivity there are $u,v\in[0,\infty)$ with $f(u)=M_T+1>0$ and $f(v)=-(M_T+1)<0$. Since $|f(u)|,|f(v)|>M_T$, neither lies in $[0,T]$, so $u,v>T$.

A sign change between two points beyond $T$: the IVT gives a zero there.

By the intermediate value theorem on the interval between $u$ and $v$ there is $c$ between them with $f(c)=0$; in particular $c>T$.

Shift the problem: preimages of $y$ are zeros of $f-y$, and $f-y$ is again continuous and onto.

2. Let $y\in\mathbb R$. Then $g:=f-y$ is continuous and surjective, and $g(x)=0\iff f(x)=y$. By part 1, $f^{-1}(\{y\})$ is unbounded, hence infinite. ∎

Trap

Finding one zero and saying “repeat”. You need a reason why the zeros are different; producing a zero beyond any given $T$ is exactly that reason.

Aufgabe 5 · Intermediate values and equal function values

Let $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb R$ be continuous with $f(0)=f(1)$.

  1. Show that for every integer $n\ge2$ there is $c_n\in[0,1-\frac1n]$ with $f(c_n)=f\big(c_n+\frac1n\big)$.
  2. Show that this fails in general if $\frac1n$ is replaced by $\alpha\in(0,1)$ with $\frac1\alpha\notin\mathbb Z$. Hint from the sheet: consider on $[0,1]$ $$\begin{aligned}f(x)=\ &\cos\Big(\frac{2\pi x}\alpha\Big)\\&-x\Big(\cos\Big(\frac{2\pi}\alpha\Big)-1\Big).\end{aligned}$$
The idea

Walk from $0$ to $1$ in $n$ equal steps of length $\frac1n$. The total change is $f(1)-f(0)=0$, so the step changes can't all be positive or all negative. If some step goes up and another goes down, the “change over one step” function $g(x)=f(x+\frac1n)-f(x)$ changes sign, and the IVT gives a step with change $0$. For other step lengths $\alpha$ the steps don't fit evenly into $[0,1]$, and one can build a function that rises on every step of length $\alpha$ and still returns to its start.

$\alpha=0.4$: $f(x)=\cos(5\pi x)+2x$, with $f(0)=f(1)=1$, but $f(x+0.4)-f(x)=0.8>0$ for every $x$.

You need

Intermediate value theorem (script, page 159; lesson 7, Proof A), telescoping sums (script, page 129), and that $\cos$ is $2\pi$-periodic with $\cos t=1\iff t\in2\pi\mathbb Z$.

Hint 1

1: Let $g(x):=f(x+\frac1n)-f(x)$ on $[0,1-\frac1n]$. Compute $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\frac kn)$.

Hint 2

1: The sum telescopes to $f(1)-f(0)=0$. If one summand is $0$, done. Otherwise there are a positive and a negative one. 2: Compute $f_\alpha(0)$, $f_\alpha(1)$, and $f_\alpha(x+\alpha)-f_\alpha(x)$.

Hint 3

2: Since $\cos\big(\frac{2\pi(x+\alpha)}\alpha\big)=\cos\big(\frac{2\pi x}\alpha+2\pi\big)$, the difference is $\alpha\big(1-\cos\frac{2\pi}\alpha\big)$, which is $>0$ exactly because $\frac1\alpha\notin\mathbb Z$.

Solution

1.

Why this stepWhat you write

Turn the question into “find a zero of $g$”.

Let $n\ge2$ and $g:[0,1-\frac1n]\to\mathbb R$, $g(x):=f(x+\frac1n)-f(x)$. It is continuous, and we need a zero of $g$.

The $n$ steps add up to the total change, which is $0$.

The sum telescopes: $$\begin{aligned}&\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g\Big(\frac kn\Big)\\&=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big[f\Big(\frac{k+1}n\Big)-f\Big(\frac kn\Big)\Big]\\&=f(1)-f(0)=0.\end{aligned}$$

Either a step is already flat, or there are steps of both signs, and the IVT finds a zero between them.

If some $g(\frac kn)=0$, take $c_n=\frac kn$. Otherwise the summands, which add up to $0$, include a positive and a negative one. The IVT applied to $g$ between these two points (both in $[0,1-\frac1n]$) gives $c_n\in[0,1-\frac1n]$ with $g(c_n)=0$, i.e. $f(c_n)=f(c_n+\frac1n)$.

2.

The suggested function returns to its start value.

Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ with $\frac1\alpha\notin\mathbb Z$ and $f_\alpha(x):=\cos\frac{2\pi x}\alpha-x\big(\cos\frac{2\pi}\alpha-1\big)$. It is continuous on $[0,1]$, with $f_\alpha(0)=1$ and $f_\alpha(1)=\cos\frac{2\pi}\alpha-\cos\frac{2\pi}\alpha+1=1$.

Over one step of length $\alpha$ the cosine part repeats exactly, so only the linear part changes.

For $x\in[0,1-\alpha]$, by $2\pi$-periodicity, $\cos\frac{2\pi(x+\alpha)}\alpha=\cos\frac{2\pi x}\alpha$, hence $$\begin{aligned}&f_\alpha(x+\alpha)-f_\alpha(x)\\&\quad=\alpha\Big(1-\cos\frac{2\pi}\alpha\Big).\end{aligned}$$

That change is never zero, exactly because $\frac1\alpha$ is not an integer.

Since $\cos\frac{2\pi}\alpha=1\iff\frac1\alpha\in\mathbb Z$, the right side is $>0$. So there is no $c\in[0,1-\alpha]$ with $f_\alpha(c)=f_\alpha(c+\alpha)$, although $f_\alpha(0)=f_\alpha(1)$. ∎

Trap

Applying the IVT to $g$ on $[0,1-\frac1n]$ using only its two endpoint values. $g(0)$ and $g(1-\frac1n)$ can have the same sign; you need the whole telescoping sum to find two points of opposite sign.

Aufgabe 6 · Discontinuities of monotone functions

Let $I$ be a non-empty interval and $f:I\to\mathbb R$ monotone. Show that $f$ has at most countably many points of discontinuity.

The idea

An increasing function can only be discontinuous by jumping up. Each jump leaves an open gap in the values, between the left-hand and the right-hand limit. Different jumps leave gaps that don't overlap. Every gap contains a rational number, and different gaps contain different rationals. So jumps can be labelled injectively by rationals, and there are only countably many rationals.

Each jump at $x$ leaves an open gap $J_x$ of values; different jumps give disjoint gaps, each containing its own rational.

You need

Supremum and infimum (script, page 105), $\mathbb Q$ is dense in $\mathbb R$ (lesson 6, Proof D), $\mathbb Q$ is countable, and subsets of countable sets are finite or countable (script, page 51; lesson 3). One-sided limits and jumps appear later in the script (pages 276–277; lesson 13), but here sup and inf are enough.

Hint 1

Assume $f$ increasing. For an interior point $x$, define $f_-(x):=\sup\{f(t):t<x\}$ and $f_+(x):=\inf\{f(t):t>x\}$. Show $f_-(x)\le f(x)\le f_+(x)$.

Hint 2

Show: $f$ is continuous at an interior $x$ iff $f_-(x)=f_+(x)$. So discontinuity means the open interval $J_x=(f_-(x),f_+(x))$ is non-empty. For $x<y$, pick $t\in(x,y)$: $f_+(x)\le f(t)\le f_-(y)$.

Hint 3

Enumerate $\mathbb Q=\{q_1,q_2,\dots\}$ and map each interior discontinuity $x$ to the smallest $k$ with $q_k\in J_x$. This map is injective. Endpoints of $I$ add at most two more points.

Solution

It suffices to treat increasing $f$: if $f$ is decreasing, $-f$ is increasing with the same discontinuities. A one-point interval is trivial.

Why this stepWhat you write

Left and right “limits” as sup and inf. They exist because $f(x)$ bounds the sets.

For an interior point $x$ of $I$ let $$f_-(x):=\sup\{f(t):t\in I,\ t<x\},$$ $$f_+(x):=\inf\{f(t):t\in I,\ t>x\}.$$ Both sets are non-empty and bounded by $f(x)$ (above, resp. below), so both numbers exist and $f_-(x)\le f(x)\le f_+(x)$.

Continuous ⇒ no gap: values just left and right are within $\varepsilon$ of $f(x)$.

If $f$ is continuous at $x$: for $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ with $f(t)>f(x)-\varepsilon$ for $t\in(x-\delta,x)\cap I$, so $f_-(x)\ge f(x)-\varepsilon$; similarly $f_+(x)\le f(x)+\varepsilon$. As $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, $f_-(x)=f(x)=f_+(x)$.

No gap ⇒ continuous: sup and inf give comparison points, monotonicity traps everything between them.

Conversely, let $f_-(x)=f_+(x)$ $(=f(x))$ and $\varepsilon>0$. There are $u<x<v$ in $I$ with $f(u)>f(x)-\varepsilon$ and $f(v)<f(x)+\varepsilon$. For $\delta:=\min\{x-u,v-x\}$ and $|t-x|<\delta$: $$\begin{aligned}f(x)-\varepsilon&<f(u)\le f(t)\\&\le f(v)<f(x)+\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$ So $f$ is discontinuous at interior $x$ iff $J_x:=(f_-(x),f_+(x))\ne\emptyset$.

Different jumps, disjoint gaps: a point between them separates the gaps.

For interior points $x<y$ choose $t\in(x,y)$. Then $f_+(x)\le f(t)\le f_-(y)$, so $J_x$ and $J_y$ are disjoint.

Label each gap with a rational inside it; disjoint gaps get different labels.

Fix an enumeration $\mathbb Q=\{q_1,q_2,\dots\}$. Each non-empty $J_x$ contains a rational (density of $\mathbb Q$). Map each interior discontinuity $x$ to $k(x):=\min\{k: q_k\in J_x\}$. By disjointness, $x\mapsto k(x)$ is injective, so the interior discontinuities form a finite or countable set.

The endpoints add at most two.

$I$ has at most two endpoints, adding at most two more discontinuities. Hence $f$ has at most countably many points of discontinuity. ∎

Trap

Choosing “a rational $q_x\in J_x$” without saying why different $x$ give different rationals. The injectivity comes from the gaps being disjoint; prove that, then the counting is one line. (Picking the smallest index $k$ also avoids an arbitrary choice.)
