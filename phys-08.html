Why it matters for the exam

Collisions are the one topic that appears in every single exam: the first multiple-choice questions (FS24, FS26, HS25, HS23), a whole multiple-choice block in FS25, and full problems built around a collision (the Newton's cradle in FS23, the ball caught in mid-air in HS23, the two pendulums in FS24). All of them are exercises below. The pattern is always the same: energy before, momentum during, energy after.

Recap of lesson 7 (3 min)

  1. What does the centre of mass of a system do if no external force acts?

    Answer

    It moves in a straight line at constant speed: $m_{\text{tot}}\ddot{\vec r}_S=\vec F_{\text{ext}}=0$, and the total momentum $\vec P=m_{\text{tot}}\vec v_S$ is conserved.

  2. What is special about the total momentum in the centre-of-mass frame?

    Answer

    It is zero: $\sum\vec p_i\,'=0$. Today that makes collisions easy.

  3. How does the kinetic energy of a system split?

    Answer

    $T=\frac12m_{\text{tot}}v_S^2+\sum\frac{p_i'^2}{2m_i}$: the energy of the centre of mass plus the internal energy of motion. For two bodies the internal part is $\frac12\mu v_{12}^2$.

1. The story: the skittles alley

On a rainy afternoon the castle guards play skittles in the long hall. Tomas rolls a heavy wooden ball at a single pin; the pin flies off, and the ball rolls on, a little slower. Mira rolls a ball of exactly the pin's weight: it stops dead where the pin stood, and the pin takes over its whole speed. The children cheer and try a tiny ball against a big pin. It bounces back.

In the corner the armourer tests arrows. He shoots into a sandbag hanging on two ropes. The bag swings up a hand's width, and from that height alone he reads off how fast the arrow flew. “The arrow loses almost all its energy in the sand,” he says, “but not its push.”

In physics: in a collision two bodies exchange momentum for a short time through large internal forces. The total momentum is conserved, always (external forces are negligible during the short impact). The kinetic energy is conserved only in an elastic collision, like the hard balls and pins; in the sandbag it is turned into heat and deformation, a completely inelastic collision. Momentum during the impact, energy before and after it: that's the armourer's method, and the method of every exam problem.

2. What is conserved in a collision

Momentum, always (section 6.8.1, page 148, with the momentum theorem of lesson 2). During the collision the bodies push on each other with internal forces $\vec F_{12}=-\vec F_{21}$, which cancel in the total momentum (lesson 7). External forces (gravity, a rope) act too, but the impact lasts only a very short time $\delta t$, so their impulse $\vec F_{\text{ext}}\,\delta t$ is negligible. So

$$\vec p_1+\vec p_2=\vec p_3+\vec p_4,$$

where the script numbers the particles $1,2$ before and $3,4$ after the collision (Rupp and the formula sheet mark “before” and “after” with $A$ and $E$, for Anfang and Ende: $\sum\vec p_i^{\,A}=\sum\vec p_i^{\,E}$). In one dimension this is one equation; in two dimensions, one for each component.

Kinetic energy, depending on the type (section 6.8.3.2, page 153). The script writes the energy balance with the Q-value $Q$:

$$T_1+T_2=T_3+T_4-Q.$$
  • Elastic ($Q=0$): the total kinetic energy is unchanged. Hard balls, billiards, atoms at low energy.
  • Inelastic ($Q<0$): kinetic energy decreases; it becomes internal energy (heat, deformation). The extreme case is the completely inelastic collision: the bodies stick together and move on with one common velocity.
  • Superelastic ($Q>0$): kinetic energy increases, taken from internal energy. An explosion, a released spring between two carts, a chemical or nuclear reaction.
The rule to remember

Momentum is conserved in every collision. Kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic ones. Total energy (including heat) is always conserved. A collision never conserves the momentum or the energy of each body separately: that would mean nothing happened.

A ball bouncing off a wall. The wall is fixed to the earth, an external body of enormous mass. If the ball comes back with the same speed, its kinetic energy is unchanged, but its momentum has flipped from $m\vec v$ to $-m\vec v$: the wall (and the earth) took the momentum $2m\vec v$. The momentum of the ball alone is not conserved; the momentum of ball plus earth is.

3. The central elastic collision

Ensslin's script starts with the simplest case (section 6.8.2, page 149): a body of mass $m_1$ with velocity $v_1$ hits a body of mass $m_2$ at rest, head-on, so everything happens on one line. Velocities are signed numbers along that line. The script calls the velocities after the collision $u_1$ and $u_2$.

$$\begin{aligned}\text{momentum:}\quad&m_1v_1=m_1u_1+m_2u_2\\ \text{energy:}\quad&\tfrac12m_1v_1^2=\tfrac12m_1u_1^2+\tfrac12m_2u_2^2\end{aligned}$$

Two equations, two unknowns. The script eliminates $u_1$ and gets a quadratic for $u_2$. A shorter way, the one to use in the exam: bring the $m_1$ terms to one side.

$$\begin{aligned}m_1(v_1-u_1)&=m_2u_2\\ m_1(v_1-u_1)(v_1+u_1)&=m_2u_2^2\end{aligned}$$

Divide the second line by the first (for $u_2\neq0$): $v_1+u_1=u_2$. Put this back into the momentum equation, $m_1v_1=m_1u_1+m_2(v_1+u_1)$, and solve:

Central elastic collision, body 2 at rest (script, page 150) $$u_1=\frac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}\,v_1,\qquad u_2=\frac{2m_1}{m_1+m_2}\,v_1.$$

The other solution, $u_2=0$, $u_1=v_1$, means the bodies missed each other. The formula sheet (page 4, left) has the general version with body 2 also moving: set $v_2^A=0$ there and you get exactly this.

The three cases of the story (script, page 150):

  • $m_1>m_2$: $0<u_1<v_1$ and $u_2>v_1$. The heavy ball rolls on, slower; the light pin flies off faster than the ball came in (up to $2v_1$).
  • $m_1=m_2$: $u_1=0$, $u_2=v_1$. Complete exchange of velocities: Mira's ball stops dead. This is the Newton's cradle.
  • $m_1<m_2$: $u_1<0$. The light ball bounces back; for $m_2\gg m_1$ (a wall) $u_1\approx-v_1$ and $u_2\approx0$.

A useful check. The middle step $v_1+u_1=u_2$ says $v_1-0=-(u_1-u_2)$: in an elastic central collision, the relative velocity reverses. The bodies separate as fast as they approached. If the second body also moves before, $v_2\neq0$, the same steps give $v_1-v_2=-(u_1-u_2)$; together with momentum conservation that's a linear system, quicker than the quadratic.

Recipe: a collision problem

  1. Before the collision: find the speeds, usually with energy conservation (a pendulum swinging down, a ball rolling down a ramp, lesson 6).
  2. During the collision: write momentum conservation, per component, with signs for the directions. Elastic? Add energy conservation (or “relative velocity reverses” for a central collision). Sticking? Use one common final velocity.
  3. Solve for the velocities just after.
  4. After the collision: energy conservation again (how high does it swing, how far does it slide).
  5. Check: momentum before = after; kinetic energy after ≤ before (unless something explodes); limiting cases like $m_1=m_2$ or $m_2\to\infty$.

Worked example 1: the skittles

A ball of mass $m_1=3m$ rolls at $v_1=2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ into a pin of mass $m_2=m$ at rest. Treat it as a central elastic collision of point masses.

  1. Find the velocities after the collision.

    Answer

    $u_1=\frac{3m-m}{4m}\cdot2\,\mathrm{m/s}=1\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $u_2=\frac{6m}{4m}\cdot2\,\mathrm{m/s}=3\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

  2. Check momentum, energy and the relative velocity.

    Answer

    Momentum: $3m\cdot2=6m$ before, $3m\cdot1+m\cdot3=6m$ after. Energy: $\frac12\cdot3m\cdot4=6m$ before, $\frac12\cdot3m\cdot1+\frac12m\cdot9=6m$ after. Relative velocity: $2-0=2$ before, $3-1=2$ after, with the order reversed.

  3. What fraction of the ball's energy went to the pin?

    Answer

    $\frac{T_4}{T_1}=\frac{4.5m}{6m}=75\,\%$. In general $\frac{T_4}{T_1}=\frac{4m_1m_2}{(m_1+m_2)^2}$, which is $100\,\%$ only for equal masses.

4. The completely inelastic collision

The bodies stick together and move on with one velocity $u$. Momentum is the only equation, and it is enough:

$$m_1\vec v_1+m_2\vec v_2=(m_1+m_2)\,\vec u\quad\Rightarrow\quad\vec u=\frac{m_1\vec v_1+m_2\vec v_2}{m_1+m_2}=\vec v_S.$$

The common velocity is the centre-of-mass velocity of lesson 7, which no collision can change. The lost kinetic energy is exactly the internal part $\frac12\mu v_{12}^2$ from lesson 7:

$$-Q=T_{\text{before}}-T_{\text{after}}=\frac12\,\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}\,(v_1-v_2)^2.$$

This is the largest possible loss: the energy of the centre of mass, $\frac12m_{\text{tot}}v_S^2$, can never be destroyed in a collision, because $\vec v_S$ stays the same. Everything else can.

Worked example 2: the armourer's sandbag (ballistic pendulum)

An arrow of mass $m$ flies horizontally with speed $v$ into a sandbag of mass $M$ hanging on ropes, and sticks. The bag swings up to height $h$. Find $v$ from $h$.

  1. During the impact: which law, and what is the speed $u$ just after?

    Answer

    Momentum (the ropes are vertical, the impact is short): $mv=(m+M)u$, so $u=\frac{m}{m+M}v$. Energy is not conserved here: the arrow tears the sand.

  2. After the impact: which law, and how does $h$ follow?

    Answer

    Now only gravity does work (the rope tension is perpendicular to the motion): $\frac12(m+M)u^2=(m+M)gh$, so $u=\sqrt{2gh}$.

  3. Combine.

    Answer
    $$v=\frac{m+M}{m}\sqrt{2gh}.$$

    With $m=50\,\mathrm g$, $M=4.95\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $h=5\,\mathrm{cm}$: $u=\sqrt{2\cdot10\cdot0.05}\,\mathrm{m/s}=1\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $v=100\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Only $\frac{m}{m+M}=1\,\%$ of the arrow's kinetic energy survives the impact.

5. The elastic collision in two dimensions: the centre-of-mass trick

Most collisions are not head-on: the bodies fly apart at angles. Momentum gives two equations, energy one, but there are four unknowns (two velocity vectors), so one quantity stays free: the scattering angle. It depends on how exactly the bodies hit, and the conservation laws can't predict it. What they do fix is everything else once the angle is known. The script does this in the centre-of-mass frame (section 6.8.4, pages 153–157), because there it becomes almost trivial.

In the centre-of-mass frame (marked with a star in the script): body 2 rests in the lab, body 1 comes in with $\vec v_1$. The centre of mass moves with $\vec v_S=\frac{m_1}{m_1+m_2}\vec v_1$. Before the collision, $\vec p_1^{\,*}+\vec p_2^{\,*}=0$, so the two momenta are equal and opposite, both of size $p^*$. After the collision they are again equal and opposite, and energy conservation, $\frac{p^{*2}}{2m_1}+\frac{p^{*2}}{2m_2}=\frac{p_3^{*2}}{2m_1}+\frac{p_3^{*2}}{2m_2}$, forces $|\vec p_3^{\,*}|=p^*$:

Elastic collision in the centre-of-mass frame (script, page 155)

The two momenta keep their size and stay opposite; they are only turned by the same angle $\vartheta^*$. All the physics of the collision is in that one angle.

Back to the lab (page 156): add the centre-of-mass velocity, $\vec v_3=\vec v_3^{\,*}+\vec v_S$ and $\vec v_4=\vec v_4^{\,*}+\vec v_S$. Picture it: the tip of $\vec v_3$ lies on a circle of radius $v_1^*$ around the tip of $\vec v_S$.

  • Equal masses ($\xi=m_1/m_2=1$, script, page 158): the two bodies always fly apart at a right angle, $\vartheta_{34}=90^\circ$. A quick proof without the frame change: momentum gives $\vec v_1=\vec v_3+\vec v_4$, energy gives $v_1^2=v_3^2+v_4^2$. Squaring the first, $v_1^2=v_3^2+v_4^2+2\,\vec v_3\cdot\vec v_4$, so $\vec v_3\cdot\vec v_4=0$. Pythagoras in a billiard table. The energies split as $T_3=T_1\cos^2\vartheta_3$ and $T_4=T_1\cos^2\vartheta_4$.
  • Heavy on light ($\xi>1$, page 160): the incoming body can only be deflected forwards, up to a maximum lab angle with $\cos\vartheta_3^{\max}=\sqrt{1-1/\xi^2}$, i.e. $\sin\vartheta_3^{\max}=\frac{m_2}{m_1}$. A bowling ball hitting a golf ball barely changes direction.
  • Light on heavy ($\xi<1$): every angle is possible, including straight back.

Try it: the velocity circle

Body 1 (blue) hits body 2 (orange) at rest, elastically. Choose the mass ratio and the angle $\vartheta^*$ by which the collision turns the momenta in the centre-of-mass frame. The grey arrow is $\vec v_S$; the circles are all possible lab velocities after the collision.

At $\xi=1$ the angle between the two lab arrows is always $90^\circ$, whatever $\vartheta^*$. Push $\xi$ above 1: the blue circle shrinks inside the reach of $\vec v_S$, and the blue arrow can no longer point backwards.

Why it's cool. In 1909 Rutherford's assistants shot alpha particles at a thin gold foil, and a few bounced straight back. By this lesson's rule, a light body only bounces back from a much heavier one: the atom had to hide almost all its mass in a tiny, heavy core. That was the discovery of the atomic nucleus, made with the kinematics of billiard balls. Particle physicists still learn what's inside protons the same way, by looking at the angles and energies after collisions.

6. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed.

Exercise 1 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.1(a), 1 point, original)

What is conserved in the elastic collision of two particles? (A) The total energy of the system. (B) The velocity of each particle. (C) The momentum of each particle. (D) The energy of each particle.

Solution

A. In an elastic collision the total kinetic energy (and of course the total energy) is conserved, together with the total momentum. (B), (C), (D): the individual quantities change; otherwise there would be no collision.

Exercise 2 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.1(a), 1 point, original)

Two balls collide elastically. Which statement is true? (A) The total energy of the two balls is higher after the collision than before. (B) The energy of each ball is lower after the collision than before. (C) Both the total momentum and the total energy are conserved. (D) Total momentum and total energy cannot be conserved at the same time.

Solution

C. That's the definition of an elastic collision plus momentum conservation. (A) would be superelastic. (B) is impossible: if both lost energy, the total would drop. (D) Worked example 1 conserves both.

Exercise 3 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.1(b), 1 point, original)

A ball hits a wall. After the collision it moves with the same speed as before, in the opposite direction. Which statement is correct? (A) Energy and momentum of the ball are unchanged. (B) Neither the energy nor the momentum of the ball is conserved. (C) The energy of the ball is conserved, its momentum is not. (D) The energy of the ball is not conserved, its momentum is.

Solution

C. Same speed, so the same $T=\frac12mv^2$. But $\vec p$ flips from $m\vec v$ to $-m\vec v$: the change $2m\vec v$ went into the wall and the earth (section 2).

Exercise 4 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.1(a), 1 point, original)

A light golf ball hits a heavy bowling ball at rest, head-on and ideally elastically ($m_{\text{Golf}}\ll M_{\text{Bowling}}$). How are the magnitude of the momentum and the kinetic energy shared between the two bodies just after the collision? (A) The golf ball carries most of the kinetic energy and most of the momentum. (B) The bowling ball carries most of the kinetic energy and most of the momentum. (C) The bowling ball carries most of the kinetic energy, but the golf ball most of the momentum. (D) The golf ball carries most of the kinetic energy, but the bowling ball most of the momentum.

Solution

D. For $m_1\ll m_2$: $u_1\approx-v_1$, so the golf ball bounces back with almost all its kinetic energy, and its momentum is $\approx-mv_1$. Momentum conservation, $mv_1=-mv_1+p_2$, gives the bowling ball $p_2\approx2mv_1$: twice the golf ball's. Its energy $\frac{p_2^2}{2M}$ is tiny because $M$ is huge.

Exercise 5 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.1(b), 1 point, original)

A ball of mass $m$ hits a second ball of the same mass at rest, head-on. What percentage of the original kinetic energy of the system can at most be lost (dissipated) in this collision? (A) $0\,\%$ (B) $50\,\%$ (C) $66.7\,\%$ (D) $100\,\%$

Solution

B. The maximum loss is the completely inelastic collision. The common velocity is $u=\frac v2$, so $T_{\text{after}}=\frac12(2m)\frac{v^2}4=\frac14mv^2$, half of $T_{\text{before}}=\frac12mv^2$. The other half is the energy of the centre of mass, which no collision can destroy (section 4).

Exercise 6 (HS25, Konzeptfrage 1.1(c), 1 point, original)

Two identical balls of mass $m$ move towards each other with the same speed $v$ ($v_1=v$, $v_2=-v$) and stick together after the collision. How large is the energy loss? (A) $0$ (B) $\frac12mv^2$ (C) $mv^2$ (D) $2mv^2$

Solution

C. The total momentum is $mv-mv=0$, so the stuck pair is at rest: all the kinetic energy, $2\cdot\frac12mv^2=mv^2$, is lost. Check with the formula: $\frac12\mu(v_1-v_2)^2=\frac12\cdot\frac m2\cdot(2v)^2=mv^2$.

Exercise 7 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.3(a), 1 point, original)

Two discs of radius $R$ slide without friction on a table. Disc 1 (mass $M$) moves to the right with $v_1$ and spins clockwise with $\omega_1$. Disc 2 (mass $2M$) moves to the left with $v_2$ and spins counter-clockwise with $\omega_2$. They collide centrally and stick together, so that afterwards they move with $v'$ to the right and spin with $\omega'$ counter-clockwise about their common centre of mass. Which statement about the collision is correct? (A) The collision is completely elastic. (B) Whether angular momentum is conserved depends on the choice of the reference point. (C) The angular momentum of the stuck discs after the collision is independent of $v_1$ and $v_2$. (D) Since the discs stick together, the total momentum is not conserved.

Solution

C. The collision is central: both centres move on the same line, so the orbital angular momentum about any point on that line is zero before and after. What remains is the spin of the discs, which doesn't involve $v_1,v_2$. (A) They stick: completely inelastic. (B) Without external torque, $\vec L$ is conserved about every fixed point (its value depends on the point, its conservation doesn't). (D) Momentum is conserved in every collision.

Exercise 8 (FS25, Konzeptfrage 1.3(b), 1 point, original)

Same discs. What is the centre-of-mass velocity $v'$ of the two discs after the collision (positive to the right) for $v_1=v_2=v$? (A) $-v$ (B) $-\frac13v$ (C) $\frac13v$ (D) $\frac23v$

Solution

B. Momentum, positive to the right: $Mv+2M(-v)=3Mv'$, so $v'=-\frac v3$. The heavier disc wins: they move to the left.

Exercise 9 (FS23, problem 2 “Newtonpendel”, parts (b)–(d), 15 points, original)

Pendulums with string length $L$ in vacuum; all collisions are elastic. A ball of mass $M$ is pulled out to $\varphi=\frac\pi2$ and released; at the bottom it hits a ball of mass $m$ at rest with the speed $v_0=\sqrt{2gL}$ (part (a), lesson 6).

(b) Show explicitly that the velocities just after the collision are $v_0'=\frac{M-m}{M+m}v_0$ (ball $M$) and $v_1'=\frac{2M}{M+m}v_0$ (ball $m$). [4 points]

(c) Now a Newton's cradle of five balls with masses $16m$, $8m$, $4m$, $2m$ and $m$, in this order. The ball $16m$ is pulled out to $\varphi=\frac\pi2$ and released. Find the speed $v_4'$ of the fifth ball (mass $m$) just after its first collision, in terms of $v_0$. Compare with $v_1'$ from (b) for $M=16m$ hitting $m$ directly. Explain why they differ or don't. [7 points]

(d) A standard Newton's cradle of four balls of mass $m$. Balls 1 and 2 are pulled out to $\varphi=\frac\pi2$ and released together. Find the momenta of all balls just after balls 1 and 2 hit balls 3 and 4. [4 points]

Solution

(b) Momentum and energy, ball $m$ at rest:

$$Mv_0=Mv_0'+mv_1',\qquad\tfrac12Mv_0^2=\tfrac12Mv_0'^2+\tfrac12mv_1'^2.$$

Rewrite as $M(v_0-v_0')=mv_1'$ and $M(v_0-v_0')(v_0+v_0')=mv_1'^2$. Dividing (for $v_1'\neq0$): $v_0+v_0'=v_1'$. Insert into the momentum equation: $Mv_0=Mv_0'+m(v_0+v_0')$, so $(M+m)v_0'=(M-m)v_0$, which gives $v_0'=\frac{M-m}{M+m}v_0$, and then $v_1'=v_0+v_0'=\frac{2M}{M+m}v_0$.

(c) Each collision is a ball of mass $2k$ hitting a ball of mass $k$ at rest. By (b) with $M=2k$, $m=k$: the hit ball gets $\frac{2\cdot2k}{3k}=\frac43$ times the incoming speed. Four collisions in a row:

$$v_4'=\left(\tfrac43\right)^4v_0=\tfrac{256}{81}\,v_0\approx3.16\,v_0.$$

Directly, $16m$ on $m$: $v_1'=\frac{32m}{17m}v_0=\frac{32}{17}v_0\approx1.88\,v_0$. They differ: the chain is much better. Reason: the fraction of kinetic energy handed over in one collision is $\frac{4Mm}{(M+m)^2}$. For the ratio $2$ that's $\frac89$ per step, $\left(\frac89\right)^4\approx62\,\%$ after four steps. For the ratio $16$ it's only $\frac{64}{289}\approx22\,\%$: the heavy ball keeps most of its energy and rolls on. The intermediate masses act like a gearbox that matches the masses step by step.

(d) Balls 1 and 2 arrive together with $v_0=\sqrt{2gL}$. After the collision, balls 1 and 2 are at rest and balls 3 and 4 move on with $v_0$ each:

$$p_1=p_2=0,\qquad p_3=p_4=m\sqrt{2gL}\ \text{(in the original direction)}.$$

Justification: treat it as a sequence of central elastic collisions of equal masses, each of which exchanges the velocities (section 3). Check: momentum $2mv_0$ and energy $mv_0^2$ before and after. One ball leaving with $2v_0$ would conserve momentum but carry $2mv_0^2$: twice the energy, impossible.

Exercise 10 (HS23, problem 2 “Schiefer Wurf und Stoss”, parts (d)–(f), 7 points, original)

Two balls of mass $m_1=m_2=1\,\mathrm{kg}$ meet at the point $x=d$, $y=h=10\,\mathrm m$; $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Just before the collision (parts (a)–(c), the projectile motion of lesson 1) ball 1 flies horizontally with $\vec v_1=(10\sqrt2,\,0)\,\mathrm{m/s}$ (it is at the top of its throw), and ball 2 flies straight up with $\vec v_2=(0,\,10\sqrt2)\,\mathrm{m/s}$. They hit at an angle $\theta$, so that momentum $\Delta p_x$ and $\Delta p_y$ is transferred. Afterwards ball 1 lands at $x=d$, after an unknown time $t_1$; ball 2 lands after $t_2=\sqrt2\,\mathrm s$ at $x=d+x_2$ with $x_2=20\,\mathrm m$.

(d) Write momentum conservation in $x$ and find the horizontal momentum $\Delta p_x$ transferred from ball 1 to ball 2. [2 points] (e) Write momentum conservation in $y$, find $\Delta p_y$ and the time $t_1$ after the collision when ball 1 lands. [3 points] (f) At what angle $\theta$ to the vertical did the balls collide? Was energy lost? [2 points]

Solution

(d) Ball 1 lands at $x=d$, so after the collision $v_{1x}'=0$. Ball 2 covers $x_2=20\,\mathrm m$ in $t_2=\sqrt2\,\mathrm s$, so $v_{2x}'=\frac{20}{\sqrt2}=10\sqrt2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Momentum in $x$: $m_1\cdot10\sqrt2+0=m_1\cdot0+m_2\cdot10\sqrt2$. ✓. Ball 1 gave its whole horizontal momentum to ball 2: $\Delta p_x=10\sqrt2\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}\approx14\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}$.

(e) Ball 2 falls from $h$ in $t_2$: $0=h+v_{2y}'t_2-\frac g2t_2^2$ $=10+\sqrt2\,v_{2y}'-10$, so $v_{2y}'=0$. Momentum in $y$: $0+m_2\cdot10\sqrt2=m_1v_{1y}'+0$, so $v_{1y}'=10\sqrt2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ upwards: $\Delta p_y=10\sqrt2\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}$, from ball 2 to ball 1. Ball 1 then lands when $0=10+10\sqrt2\,t_1-5t_1^2$, i.e. $t_1^2-2\sqrt2\,t_1-2=0$:

$$t_1=\sqrt2+\sqrt{2+2}=(2+\sqrt2)\,\mathrm s\approx3.4\,\mathrm s.$$

(f) The momentum given to ball 2 is $\Delta\vec p=(+10\sqrt2,\,-10\sqrt2)$: equal parts sideways and down, so the push acted along a line at $\theta=45^\circ$ to the vertical. Energy: before $\frac12\cdot1\cdot200+\frac12\cdot1\cdot200=200\,\mathrm J$; after, ball 1 has $(0,10\sqrt2)$ and ball 2 has $(10\sqrt2,0)$, again $200\,\mathrm J$. No energy was lost: an elastic collision of equal masses, and indeed the two velocities after are perpendicular (section 5).

Exercise 11 (FS24, problem 4 “Pendel”, part (f), 2 points, original)

Two identical pendulums (length $l$, mass $m$) hang side by side and touch at rest. Pendulum 1 is pulled out to $-\varphi_i$ (small angle) and released; pendulum 2 is at rest. Write momentum and energy conservation for the elastic collision at $\varphi=0$ and derive the velocities of the pendulums just after it.

Solution

At the bottom pendulum 1 has the speed $v=l\,\omega\varphi_i=\varphi_i\sqrt{gl}$ (from $\varphi(t)=-\varphi_i\cos\omega t$, $\omega=\sqrt{g/l}$; or from energy, $mgl(1-\cos\varphi_i)\approx\frac12mgl\varphi_i^2=\frac12mv^2$). The collision, with $u_1,u_2$ after:

$$mv=mu_1+mu_2,\qquad\tfrac12mv^2=\tfrac12mu_1^2+\tfrac12mu_2^2.$$

The first gives $u_1+u_2=v$; squaring and comparing with the second gives $u_1u_2=0$. Since pendulum 1 can't pass through pendulum 2, $u_1=0$ and $u_2=v=\varphi_i\sqrt{gl}$: the velocities are exchanged, and pendulum 2 swings out to $+\varphi_i$.

Exercise 12 (the ballistic pendulum, exam style)

A bullet of mass $m=10\,\mathrm g$ is shot horizontally into a wooden block of mass $M=990\,\mathrm g$ hanging on a string of length $\ell=1.8\,\mathrm m$, and sticks. The block swings out to the angle $\varphi=60^\circ$. Take $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. (a) How fast was the bullet? (b) What fraction of its kinetic energy was turned into heat? (c) What is the string tension just after the impact?

Solution

(a) After the impact, energy: the block rises by $h=\ell(1-\cos60^\circ)=0.9\,\mathrm m$, so $u=\sqrt{2gh}=\sqrt{18}\,\mathrm{m/s}$ $=3\sqrt2\,\mathrm{m/s}\approx4.2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. During the impact, momentum: $v=\frac{m+M}mu=100\cdot3\sqrt2\,\mathrm{m/s}\approx420\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

(b) $\frac{T_{\text{after}}}{T_{\text{before}}}=\frac{\frac12(m+M)u^2}{\frac12mv^2}=\frac m{m+M}=1\,\%$, so $99\,\%$ became heat and deformation.

(c) Just after the impact the block moves on a circle of radius $\ell$ with speed $u$: $F_S-(m+M)g=(m+M)\frac{u^2}\ell$, so $F_S=1\,\mathrm{kg}\cdot\left(10+\frac{18}{1.8}\right)\mathrm{m/s^2}=20\,\mathrm N$, twice the weight.

Scoring yourself: Exercises 1–11 make 32 points. With 21 you're done. Exercise 12 combines all three steps of the recipe, the typical shape of a problem 2.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Stealing speed from Jupiter. A space probe flying past a planet is an elastic “collision” without contact: gravity does the pushing. In the planet's frame the probe leaves with the same speed it came in. But the planet moves with speed $U$ around the sun, and in the sun's frame the probe can leave up to $2U$ faster than it arrived: the light ball bouncing off a moving wall. Voyager 2 used Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune in turn, and Jupiter's orbit around the sun shrank by an immeasurably small amount to pay for it.

Why a cue ball stops dead. A billiard player who hits the object ball exactly head-on, without spin, sees the cue ball stop on the spot: equal masses, central, elastic. Hit off-centre, and the two balls always leave at $90^\circ$: players use this to plan where the cue ball will roll.
