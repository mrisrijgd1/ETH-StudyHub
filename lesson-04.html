1. The story: the knight's promise

A knight swears: “If the dragon comes, I will fight.” When has the knight broken the oath? Only if the dragon comes and the knight doesn't fight. If the dragon never comes, the oath is kept, whatever the knight does.

A siege engineer has three ways to take a fortress (a claim): the direct assault, the back gate, and letting the enemy hang themselves. And for fortresses that stretch on forever, there is a siege tower: a line of signal fires along the mountains.

In maths: the oath is the implication $A\Rightarrow B$ (A = the dragon comes, B = the knight fights). The three weapons are the direct proof, the contrapositive and the proof by contradiction, and the signal fires are induction.

2. Implication

$A\Rightarrow B$ is false only when $A$ is true and $B$ is false. Three consequences you'll use constantly:

  • Negation: $\neg(A\Rightarrow B)\iff A\wedge\neg B$. (“The dragon came and the knight didn't fight.”) This is what you assume in a proof by contradiction.
  • Contrapositive = same oath: $(A\Rightarrow B)\iff(\neg B\Rightarrow\neg A)$. “If I didn't fight, the dragon didn't come.” The same promise, said from the other side.
  • Converse ≠ same oath: $B\Rightarrow A$ (“if I fight, the dragon came”) is a completely different promise. Mixing these up is one of the most common exam mistakes, and the true/false part loves to test it.

3. The three weapons, and the siege tower

  • Weapon 1: the direct assault (direct proof). Start from what you know and march straight to the conclusion. Most proofs in lessons 2 and 3 were direct.
  • Weapon 2: the back gate (contrapositive, Section 1.8.2, p. 54). To prove $A\Rightarrow B$, prove $\neg B\Rightarrow\neg A$ instead. Use it when “not $B$” gives you something concrete to hold, and “$A$” doesn't.
  • Weapon 3: let the enemy hang themselves (contradiction, Section 1.8.1, p. 53). Assume the claim is false and follow the logic until something impossible appears, like $1=0$ or “$p$ is even and odd”. Then the assumption was wrong. Use it when the claim says something doesn't exist or can't happen (“$\sqrt2$ is not a fraction”, “there is no largest prime”).
  • The siege tower: induction (Satz 2.15, p. 83). For claims about every $n\in\mathbb N$. If (1) the first fire is lit (base case: $A(1)$) and (2) every lit fire lights the next one (induction step: $A(n)\Rightarrow A(n+1)$ for every $n$), then every fire is burning. The fires can't skip anything, because $\mathbb N$ has nothing besides $1,2,3,\dots$ (the script defines $\mathbb N$ as the smallest “inductive” subset of $\mathbb R$).

The trap with induction: in the step, you assume $A(n)$ and prove $A(n+1)$. You are not assuming what you want to prove; you are proving the link between two fires, for an arbitrary $n$.

Proof A: by contrapositive, if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even (the back gate)

Claim. For $n\in\mathbb Z$: if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.

The idea. “$n^2$ is even” is hard to use directly: there's no easy way to take a square root of $2k$. But “$n$ is odd” gives a formula, $n=2k+1$, that we can square. So go through the back gate.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Contrapositive: the claim is equivalent to “if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd”.
  • Odd means $n=2k+1$ for some $k\in\mathbb Z$. So the proof starts “Let $n$ be odd, so $n=2k+1$” and ends “so $n^2=2(\dots)+1$ is odd”.
The storyWhat you write

Go round to the back gate.

We prove the contrapositive: if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd.

An odd number has a concrete shape.

Let $n$ be odd, so $n=2k+1$ with $k\in\mathbb Z$.

Square it and look at the shape again.

Then $n^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1$ with $2k^2+2k\in\mathbb Z$, so $n^2$ is odd. By contraposition, the claim follows. ∎

Moves used: go through the back gate

The proof in one piece

We show the contrapositive. Let $n$ be odd, $n=2k+1$ with $k\in\mathbb Z$. Then $n^2=2(2k^2+2k)+1$ is odd. Hence, if $n^2$ is even, $n$ is even. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why is $2k^2+2k$ allowed as the “$k$” of the odd-number formula?

    Answer

    “Odd” means $2m+1$ for some integer $m$. Here $m=2k^2+2k$, and that is an integer because $k$ is one. Say it in the proof: graders check it.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Why not prove the claim directly, from “$n^2=2k$”?

    Answer

    To get to $n$ you would have to take a square root of $2k$, and nothing tells you that $\sqrt{2k}$ is even, or even an integer. The contrapositive starts from $n=2k+1$, a formula you can square.

  3. Rung 3 (2 min). Write the proof from the frame alone.

Proof B: $\sqrt2$ is irrational (Lemma 2.30, p. 89–90)

Claim. There is no $x\in\mathbb Q$ with $x^2=2$.

The idea. A “there is no” claim: weapon 3. Assume $\sqrt2=\frac pq$ in lowest terms. Squaring shows $p$ is even (Proof A), then $q$ is even, so the fraction wasn't in lowest terms after all.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Contradiction: “Suppose, for contradiction, that $x=\frac pq$ with $p,q\in\mathbb Z$, $q\neq0$, satisfies $x^2=2$.”
  • The choice that makes it work: “and the fraction is fully reduced” (every fraction can be reduced). The contradiction will be with exactly this.
The storyWhat you write

The enemy claims $\sqrt2$ is a fraction, as simple as can be.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $\left(\frac pq\right)^2=2$ with $p,q\in\mathbb Z$, $q\neq0$, and $p,q$ without common factor.

Square and clear the denominator.

Then $p^2=2q^2$, so $p^2$ is even, and by Proof A $p$ is even: $p=2m$ with $m\in\mathbb Z$.

The same trap springs on $q$.

Substituting: $4m^2=2q^2$, so $q^2=2m^2$ is even, and by Proof A $q$ is even.

Both even: the fraction wasn't as simple as claimed.

So $2$ divides both $p$ and $q$, contradicting that they have no common factor. Hence no rational $x$ has $x^2=2$. ∎

Moves used: assume the opposite go through the back gate (inside Proof A)

The proof in one piece

Suppose $\left(\frac pq\right)^2=2$ with $p,q\in\mathbb Z$, $q\neq0$, fully reduced. Then $p^2=2q^2$ is even, so $p=2m$ is even (Proof A). Then $4m^2=2q^2$, so $q^2=2m^2$ is even and $q$ is even. So $p$ and $q$ have the common factor $2$, a contradiction. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Assume $\sqrt2=\frac pq$, fully reduced.
  2. Square: $p^2=2q^2$, so $p$ even, $p=2m$.
  3. Substitute: $q^2=2m^2$, so $q$ even.
  4. Contradiction with “fully reduced”.

In short: “assume, square, $p$ even, $q$ even, contradiction”. Proof A is reused twice: proofs are built from smaller proofs.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Where exactly does the contradiction come from? What would go wrong if we hadn't said “fully reduced”?

    Answer

    From “$p$ and $q$ both even” versus “no common factor”. Without “fully reduced” there is nothing to contradict: $\frac{2m}{2m'}$ is a perfectly fine fraction.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Does the same proof show that $\sqrt3$ is irrational? What would you need instead of Proof A?

    Answer

    Yes, with “if $3\mid n^2$ then $3\mid n$” (contrapositive: if $n=3k+1$ or $3k+2$, then $n^2=3(\dots)+1$). Then $p^2=3q^2$ gives $3\mid p$, then $3\mid q$.

  3. Rung 3 (4 min). Write the proof from the exam box alone.

Proof C: Bernoulli's inequality, by induction

Claim (Bernoulli's inequality). For every $x\ge-1$ and every $n\in\mathbb N$: $(1+x)^n\ge1+nx$.

You'll use this to compute limits in lessons 10 and 11.

The idea. Going from $n$ to $n+1$ multiplies the left side by $1+x$. Multiply the hypothesis by $1+x$ (allowed, since it's $\ge0$), expand, and throw away the extra term $nx^2\ge0$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Fix $x\ge-1$ first. Then $A(n)$ is “$(1+x)^n\ge1+nx$”, and we prove $A(n)$ for all $n$ by induction.
  • Two labelled parts: base case $A(1)$; induction step “let $n\in\mathbb N$ and assume $A(n)$” … “so $A(n+1)$ holds”.
  • The step must end at $(1+x)^{n+1}\ge1+(n+1)x$. Write that target down before you start.
The storyWhat you write

Light the first fire.

Fix $x\ge-1$, and let $A(n)$ be $(1+x)^n\ge1+nx$. Base case ($n=1$): $(1+x)^1=1+x=1+1\cdot x$, so $A(1)$ holds.

Suppose fire $n$ burns.

Induction step: let $n\in\mathbb N$ and assume $A(n)$, i.e. $(1+x)^n\ge1+nx$ (induction hypothesis).

Carry the flame one step further, with care: the multiplier must not be negative.

Since $1+x\ge0$, multiplying the hypothesis by $1+x$ keeps the inequality: $$\begin{aligned}(1+x)^{n+1}&=(1+x)^n(1+x)\\&\ge(1+nx)(1+x).\end{aligned}$$

Expand, and drop what can only help.

$$\begin{aligned}(1+nx)(1+x)&=1+(n+1)x+nx^2\\&\ge1+(n+1)x,\end{aligned}$$ since $nx^2\ge0$. So $A(n+1)$ holds.

All the fires burn.

By induction, $A(n)$ holds for all $n\in\mathbb N$. ∎

Moves used: induction with labelled parts drop a non-negative term

The proof in one piece

Fix $x\ge-1$. For $n=1$ both sides equal $1+x$. Assume $(1+x)^n\ge1+nx$ for some $n\in\mathbb N$. Since $1+x\ge0$, $$\begin{aligned}(1+x)^{n+1}&\ge (1+nx)(1+x)\\&=1+(n+1)x+nx^2\\&\ge 1+(n+1)x\end{aligned}.$$ By induction the inequality holds for all $n\in\mathbb N$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Fix $x\ge-1$ and name $A(n)$.
  2. Base case $n=1$, labelled.
  3. Step: multiply the hypothesis by $1+x\ge0$ (say why it's allowed), expand, drop $nx^2\ge0$.
  4. Conclude “by induction”.

What graders look for: both parts labelled, the hypothesis visibly used, and every step's reason given (here: why we may multiply by $1+x$).

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Where is $x\ge-1$ used? Which axiom is behind it?

    Answer

    In multiplying the inequality by $1+x$: that keeps “$\ge$” only because $1+x\ge0$. It's the order axiom on multiplication (axiom (15)); multiplying by a negative number would flip the inequality.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Where is the induction hypothesis used? Mark the exact “$\ge$”.

    Answer

    In $$(1+x)^n(1+x)\ge(1+nx)(1+x):$$the factor $(1+x)^n$ is replaced by the smaller $1+nx$.

  3. Rung 3 (4 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

4. The sixteen laws of the realm (Section 2.1)

The script defines $\mathbb R$ not by “what numbers are”, but by the 16 laws they obey.

  • Laws (1)–(9), the field axioms (p. 71–74): the usual rules of $+$ and $\cdot$ (associative, commutative, $0$ and $1$, inverses, distributive). Every rule of school algebra follows from these. $\mathbb Q$ obeys them too.
  • Laws (10)–(15), the order axioms (p. 77): $\le$ is reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive and total (any two numbers can be compared), and compatible with $+$ and $\cdot$. Together with (1)–(9) this makes an ordered field. $\mathbb Q$ obeys these too!
  • Law (16), the completeness axiom (p. 79–80): if $X$ and $Y$ are non-empty sets of reals and every $x\in X$ is $\le$ every $y\in Y$, then some $c\in\mathbb R$ sits between them: $x\le c\le y$ for all $x\in X$, $y\in Y$. Picture two armies on the number line, $X$ entirely to the left of $Y$. Law 16 says there's always a real number standing in the no-man's-land between them. $\mathbb R$ has no gaps.

Why law 16 is the one that matters: it's the only law that separates $\mathbb R$ from $\mathbb Q$. In $\mathbb Q$, take $X=\{q>0:q^2<2\}$ and $Y=\{q>0:q^2>2\}$. Every element of $X$ is below every element of $Y$, but the only number that could stand between them is $\sqrt2$, and that's not in $\mathbb Q$ (Proof B). So $\mathbb Q$ has a gap exactly where $\sqrt2$ should be. Everything big in this course rests on law 16: sup and inf (lesson 6), limits of monotone sequences (lesson 11), the intermediate value theorem (lesson 7), the existence of $e$ and $\pi$.

Deep dive: reading the completeness axiom symbol by symbol

This is the formal version from the script (p. 80):

$$\begin{aligned}\forall X,Y\subseteq\mathbb R:\ \Big(X\neq\emptyset\wedge Y\neq\emptyset\wedge\forall x\in X\ \forall y\in Y:\ x\le y\Big)\Rightarrow\exists c\in\mathbb R\ \forall x\in X\ \forall y\in Y:\ x&\le c\\&\le y\end{aligned}$$

It looks scary, but it's just a duel from lesson 1 with a knight's oath inside. Read it left to right.

  1. “$\forall X,Y\subseteq\mathbb R$”. The Doubter picks any two sets of real numbers, as nasty as they like.
  2. The bracket before “$\Rightarrow$”: the conditions. It's a knight's oath. The Champion only has to deliver if the Doubter's sets pass three checks: $X$ has an element, $Y$ has an element, and every element of $X$ is $\le$ every element of $Y$ (on the number line, $X$ lies entirely to the left of $Y$; touching is allowed). If any check fails, the oath is kept automatically (the dragon never came).
  3. “$\exists c\in\mathbb R$”. If the checks pass, the Champion must name one number $c$. The Champion sees $X$ and $Y$ first, so $c$ may depend on them.
  4. “$\forall x\in X\ \forall y\in Y:\ x\le c\le y$”. Now the Doubter tests $c$ against every $x$ and every $y$. $c$ must be $\ge$ all of $X$ and $\le$ all of $Y$: it stands in the no-man's-land.

Why the order “$\exists c$, then $\forall x\,\forall y$” is the whole point. If the axiom said $\forall x\,\forall y\,\exists c:\ x\le c\le y$, it would be trivial: for each pair you could just take $c=x$. The power is that one single $c$ works against all pairs at once. That's the uniform idea from lesson 1.

Why the two “$\neq\emptyset$” conditions are needed. Drop “$X\neq\emptyset$” and take $X=\emptyset$, $Y=\mathbb R$. The third check is then vacuously true (lesson 1's “$\forall$ over the empty set”). The axiom would demand a $c$ with $c\le y$ for every real $y$, including $y=c-1$: impossible. So without the condition the axiom would be false even in $\mathbb R$. The same goes for $Y=\emptyset$ and $X=\mathbb R$.

Three examples to calibrate your intuition.

  • $X=[0,1]$, $Y=[2,3]$: any $c\in[1,2]$ works. So $c$ doesn't have to be unique.
  • $X=[0,1]$, $Y=[1,2]$: only $c=1$ works, and it's in both sets. So $c$ may belong to $X$ or $Y$.
  • $X=(-\infty,0)$, $Y=(0,\infty)$: only $c=0$ works, and it's in neither set. So the axiom fills a single missing point. This case is what makes it powerful.

Why you never need it for finite sets. If $X$ is finite, take $c=\max X$. It's $\ge$ all of $X$, and $\le$ all of $Y$ because it is an element of $X$. The axiom only adds something new for infinite sets, where a largest element might not exist (like $X=(-\infty,0)$).

How it's used in proofs (preview of lesson 6). To show that some number exists: (1) build two sets $X$ and $Y$ cleverly, (2) check the three conditions, (3) the axiom hands you $c$, (4) show that $c$ has the property you wanted. For example, the supremum of a bounded set $X$ is what you get by choosing $Y=$ “all upper bounds of $X$”: the $c$ in between is then the smallest upper bound.

Worked example 1: watching $\mathbb Q$ fail, with actual numbers

Work inside $\mathbb Q$: $X=\{q\in\mathbb Q: q\le0\text{ or }q^2<2\}$ and $Y=\{q\in\mathbb Q: q>0\text{ and }q^2>2\}$.

  1. Check the three conditions of the completeness axiom.

    Answer

    $0\in X$ and $2\in Y$, so both are non-empty. Every element of $X$ is below every element of $Y$: if $x\le0$ this is clear; if $x>0$ with $x^2<2<y^2$, then $x<y$.

  2. The Champion tries $c=1.4$. What does the Doubter answer?

    Answer

    $1.41\in X$, since $1.41^2=1.9881<2$, and $1.41>c$. The Doubter wins.

  3. The Champion tries $c=1.42$. What now?

    Answer

    $1.415\in Y$, since $1.415^2=2.002225>2$, and $1.415<c$. The Doubter wins again.

  4. Why does every rational $c$ lose?

    Answer

    If $c^2<2$, a slightly bigger rational is still in $X$. If $c^2>2$, a slightly smaller rational is still in $Y$. And $c^2=2$ is impossible in $\mathbb Q$ (Proof B). In $\mathbb R$ the Champion simply names $c=\sqrt2$.

Worked example 2: choosing the weapon

  1. “There is no largest prime.” Which weapon?

    Answer

    Contradiction: a “there is no” claim. Assume there are only finitely many primes and build a number that breaks the list (Exercise 3).

  2. “If $3n+1$ is even, then $n$ is odd.” Which weapon?

    Answer

    Contrapositive: if $n$ is even, $n=2k$, then $3n+1=2(3k)+1$ is odd. “Not $B$” gave a formula to compute with.

  3. “$1+2+\dots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$.” Which weapon?

    Answer

    Induction: a claim about every $n$, where the case $n+1$ is the case $n$ plus one more term.

Why it's cool. Sixteen short laws are all of $\mathbb R$: every fact you used in school, from $0\cdot x=0$ to the intermediate value theorem, follows from them. And the one law that $\mathbb Q$ lacks, completeness, is why $\sqrt2$ exists in $\mathbb R$; every big theorem of this course (intermediate value theorem, extreme value theorem, Bolzano–Weierstrass) uses it somewhere.

6. Exercises

Each solution is folded under its exercise. Exercises 1, 2 and 4 count for scoring.

Exercise 1 (true/false block, 3 points)

For statements $A,B$, which are always true? (a) $\neg(A\Rightarrow B)\iff A\wedge\neg B$. (b) $(A\Rightarrow B)\iff(B\Rightarrow A)$. (c) $(A\Rightarrow B)\iff(\neg B\Rightarrow\neg A)$. (d) $(A\Rightarrow B)\iff(\neg A\vee B)$.

Solution
  • (a) True. $A\Rightarrow B$ is false in exactly one situation: $A$ true and $B$ false. So “$A\Rightarrow B$ is false” says exactly “$A$ and not $B$”.
  • (b) False. $B\Rightarrow A$ is the converse. Take $A=$ “$n=2$” and $B=$ “$n$ is even”. Then $A\Rightarrow B$ is true, but $B\Rightarrow A$ is false for $n=4$. So the two sides can have different truth values.
  • (c) True. $A\Rightarrow B$ is false only when $A$ is true and $B$ false. $\neg B\Rightarrow\neg A$ is false only when $\neg B$ is true and $\neg A$ false, i.e. $B$ false and $A$ true. The same single situation.
  • (d) True. $\neg A\vee B$ is false only when $\neg A$ and $B$ are both false, i.e. $A$ true and $B$ false. Again the same situation. Handy for negating: $\neg(\neg A\vee B)=A\wedge\neg B$ by De Morgan.

So T, F, T, T.

Exercise 2 (induction, linked to a real exam question)

Prove by induction that for all $n\in\mathbb N$: $$\frac1{1\cdot2}+\frac1{2\cdot3}+\dots+\frac1{n(n+1)}=\frac n{n+1}.$$ Then: what do you think happens to the left side as $n\to\infty$? (Einsiedler's HS21 #4 asked you to show $\sum\frac1{4k^2-1}=\frac12$. The same idea works there. We'll do it properly in lesson 14.)

Solution

Frame: name $A(n)$; label the base case; the step starts “let $n\in\mathbb N$ and assume $A(n)$” and must end at $\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\frac1{k(k+1)}=\frac{n+1}{n+2}$. Moves: induction with labelled parts.

Let $A(n)$ be the statement $\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1{k(k+1)}=\frac n{n+1}$.

Base case ($n=1$): the left side is $\frac1{1\cdot2}=\frac12$, the right side $\frac1{1+1}=\frac12$. So $A(1)$ holds.

Induction step: let $n\in\mathbb N$ and assume $A(n)$. Split off the last term and use $A(n)$ (say so explicitly: this is where the hypothesis is used): $$\begin{aligned}\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\frac1{k(k+1)}&=\frac n{n+1}+\frac1{(n+1)(n+2)}\\&=\frac{n(n+2)+1}{(n+1)(n+2)}\\&=\frac{(n+1)^2}{(n+1)(n+2)}\\&=\frac{n+1}{n+2}\end{aligned},$$ cancelling one factor $n+1\neq0$. That is $A(n+1)$. By induction, $A(n)$ holds for all $n\in\mathbb N$. ∎

The limit question. $\frac n{n+1}=1-\frac1{n+1}$, and $\frac1{n+1}$ gets as small as you like, so the sums approach $1$. Why it works so neatly: $\frac1{k(k+1)}=\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}$, so the sum is $$\left(1-\frac12\right)+\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)+\dots+\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\right),$$and everything in the middle cancels. That's a telescoping sum, exactly the trick for HS21 #4, since $\frac1{4k^2-1}=\frac12\left(\frac1{2k-1}-\frac1{2k+1}\right)$.

Exercise 3 (contradiction)

Prove that there are infinitely many prime numbers. Hint: assume there are only finitely many, $p_1,\dots,p_k$, and look at $N=p_1p_2\cdots p_k+1$. What's the remainder when you divide $N$ by any $p_i$? (You may use that every integer $\ge2$ has at least one prime factor.)

Solution

Frame: “there are infinitely many” is a “there is no last one” claim, so the first line is “Assume, for contradiction, that $p_1,\dots,p_k$ are all the primes” and the last is “Contradiction”. The gap: build a number that no $p_i$ divides. Moves: assume the opposite.

  • Assume for contradiction that there are only finitely many primes, and list all of them: $p_1,\dots,p_k$.
  • Let $N=p_1p_2\cdots p_k+1$. Since every $p_i\ge2$, $N\ge3$.
  • Remainder: $N$ divided by $p_i$ leaves remainder $1$, because the product is a multiple of $p_i$. So $p_i$ does not divide $N$, for every $i$.
  • But $N\ge2$ has a prime factor $q$. Since the list contains all primes, $q=p_i$ for some $i$, so $p_i$ divides $N$. Contradiction.
  • So there are infinitely many primes. ∎

Common mistake: claiming “$N$ is prime”. It doesn't have to be: $$\begin{aligned}2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13+1&=30031\\&=59\cdot509\end{aligned}.$$The proof only needs that $N$ has some prime factor that isn't on the list.

Exercise 4 (lesson 1 strikes back)

Negate the completeness axiom, with $\neg$ pushed all the way in. Then say in one sentence what the negation means on the number line.

Solution

Write the axiom as $\forall X,Y:\ (P\Rightarrow Q)$, where $P=$ “$X\neq\emptyset$ and $Y\neq\emptyset$ and $\forall x\in X\ \forall y\in Y:\ x\le y$” and $Q=$ “$\exists c\in\mathbb R\ \forall x\in X\ \forall y\in Y:\ x\le c\le y$”. Negate step by step:

  1. $\neg\forall X,Y:(P\Rightarrow Q)$ becomes $\exists X,Y:\neg(P\Rightarrow Q)$.
  2. $\neg(P\Rightarrow Q)$ becomes $P\wedge\neg Q$ ($P$ stays unchanged!).
  3. $$\neg Q=\neg\exists c\,\forall x\,\forall y:(x\le c\wedge c\le y)$$becomes $\forall c\,\exists x\,\exists y:\neg(x\le c\wedge c\le y)$.
  4. $\neg(x\le c\wedge c\le y)$ becomes $x>c\vee c>y$ (De Morgan).

Result: $$\exists X,Y\subseteq\mathbb R:\ X\neq\emptyset\wedge Y\neq\emptyset\wedge(\forall x\in X\ \forall y\in Y:\ x\le y)\wedge(\forall c\in\mathbb R\ \exists x\in X\ \exists y\in Y:\ x>c\vee c>y).$$

In words: there are two non-empty sets, one entirely to the left of the other, such that for every $c\in\mathbb R$ some $x$ is to the right of $c$ or some $y$ is to the left of $c$ (every number the Champion names is beaten). No number fits in between, so the line has a gap. This is exactly what happens in $\mathbb Q$ with the $\sqrt2$ sets from Worked example 1.

Bonus (spot the mistake)

Find the mistake in this “proof” that all horses have the same colour. $A(n)$: any $n$ horses have the same colour. Base case: one horse has one colour. Step: given $n+1$ horses, the first $n$ have the same colour by $A(n)$, and so do the last $n$. They overlap, so all $n+1$ have the same colour.

Solution

The base case is fine. The problem is the step, which must work for every $n$, including $n=1$: the link from “1 horse” to “2 horses”.

  • With $n+1=2$ horses $H_1,H_2$: “the first $n$” $=\{H_1\}$ and “the last $n$” $=\{H_2\}$. Each group has one colour, trivially.
  • The argument needs a horse in both groups to carry the colour across. Here the groups don't overlap, so nothing connects $H_1$'s colour to $H_2$'s.
  • So $A(1)\Rightarrow A(2)$ is never proven, and without it the induction proves nothing about $n\ge2$, even though the step really does work from $n=2$ on (the chain of fires breaks at the very first link).

Lesson: when you write an induction step, check its smallest case separately. Steps that “obviously” work often hide an assumption like “there are at least 3 elements”.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Induction is recursion. A recursive function that handles the base case and reduces $n+1$ to $n$ is an induction proof that the program works. Computer scientists call the “what stays true at every step” part a loop invariant. It's the same signal-fire idea.

The proof that may have cost a life. Legend says the Pythagoreans believed everything was built from whole-number ratios. When one of them, Hippasus, showed that the diagonal of a square can't be such a ratio (Proof B, in geometric form), he was supposedly thrown overboard at sea. Historians doubt the details, but the shock was real. It took about 2,000 years, until Dedekind and Cantor in the 1870s, to say what irrational numbers actually are. Law 16 is the modern answer.
