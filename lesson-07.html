Recap of lesson 6 (5 min)

  • $\sup X$ (for $X\neq\emptyset$ bounded above) exists by completeness: (1) an upper bound, (2′) for every $\varepsilon>0$ some $x\in X$ has $x>\sup X-\varepsilon$.
  • Max exists exactly when $\sup X\in X$.
  • Archimedes: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $n$ with $\frac1n<\varepsilon$. $\mathbb Q$ is dense: a fraction in every gap. Nested closed intervals have a common point, so $\mathbb R$ is uncountable.

  1. $\sup\{x: x^2<3\}$? Is it a maximum?

    Answer

    $\sqrt3$, not attained (the set is $(-\sqrt3,\sqrt3)$), so no maximum.

  2. Which of the two sup properties does “no number $t<s$ is an upper bound” express?

    Answer

    (2), “least”, in its negated-bound form; (2′) is the ε-version of the same thing. Today's main proof uses (1) in one case and (2) in the other.

1. The story: the road with no gaps

A messenger rides the road from the village at $a$ to the fortress at $b$. The road's height above sea level at position $x$ is $f(x)$.

The village lies at $100$ metres, the fortress at $800$. Somewhere along the way, did the messenger pass through exactly $450$ metres?

If the road is unbroken, obviously yes. You cannot get from $100$ to $800$ without passing every height in between. But if the road has a cliff, where it jumps from $300$ straight to $600$, then the messenger might never stand at $450$.

So the whole question is: what exactly does “unbroken” mean? Not “you can draw it without lifting the pen”, which is a statement about hands and paper, not about numbers. The formal answer is continuity, and it is a duel, like the ones in lesson 1.

The Doubter speaks first: “I want the height to stay within $\varepsilon$ of $f(x_0)$. Here is my $\varepsilon$, as small as I like.”

You answer: “Then stay within $\delta$ of the position $x_0$, and I guarantee it.”

The road is unbroken at $x_0$ if you can always answer, however mean the Doubter's $\varepsilon$ is. At a cliff you cannot: once the Doubter's tolerance is small compared with the height of the cliff, every window around $x_0$, however narrow, still reaches over the edge.

2. The definition

Let $D\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be non-empty and $f:D\to\mathbb{R}$.

Continuity at a point (Definition 3.45, p. 153). $f$ is continuous at $x_0\in D$ if

$$\forall\,\varepsilon>0\ \ \exists\,\delta>0\ \ \forall x\in D:\ \ |x-x_0|<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ |f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon.$$

$f$ is continuous (stetig) if it is continuous at every point of $D$:

$$\forall x_0\in D\ \forall\,\varepsilon>0\ \exists\,\delta>0\ \forall x\in D:\ |x-x_0|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon.$$

Read the two absolute values as distances, the way you did in lesson 5: $|x-x_0|$ is the distance along the road, and $|f(x)-f(x_0)|$ is the difference in height.

The order of the quantifiers is everything. Read it as a game played in this order:

  1. A point $x_0$ is fixed.
  2. The Doubter picks $\varepsilon>0$ (how close the heights must be).
  3. Knowing $\varepsilon$, you pick $\delta>0$ (how close the positions must be).
  4. The Doubter tests any $x\in D$ within $\delta$ of $x_0$. You win if $f(x)$ is always within $\varepsilon$ of $f(x_0)$.

So $\delta$ is allowed to depend on $\varepsilon$ and on $x_0$. That's why $\delta$ is written after $\varepsilon$. In practice you always do the algebra first and read off which $\delta$ works. (If a single $\delta$ works for all $x_0$ at once, the function is called uniformly continuous, gleichmässig stetig. That's lesson 8.)

The negation, which you need for “not continuous”

Negate it with the lesson 1 rules: swap $\forall$ and $\exists$, and negate the inside. The negation of “$P\Rightarrow Q$” is “$P$ and not $Q$”.

$f$ is not continuous at $x_0$ if

$$\exists\,\varepsilon>0\ \ \forall\,\delta>0\ \ \exists x\in D:\ \ |x-x_0|<\delta\ \ \text{and}\ \ |f(x)-f(x_0)|\ge\varepsilon.$$

Read aloud: “there is one bad tolerance $\varepsilon$ such that, however small the window $\delta$, some point inside the window has a height that misses by at least $\varepsilon$.”

Recipe for a discontinuity proof: name one $\varepsilon$ (usually half the jump), let $\delta>0$ be arbitrary, and construct an explicit $x$ inside the window with $|f(x)-f(x_0)|\ge\varepsilon$. It's the same shape as the “not open” proofs in lesson 5.

3. Worked ε-δ proofs (Beispiel 3.47, p. 154)

Do these by hand a few times, then never again: section 4 gives you the rules that make ε-δ work unnecessary for anything built from the standard pieces. The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the proof.

(a) Constants are continuous

Let $f(x)=c$ for all $x$.

  1. Let $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Compute $|f(x)-f(x_0)|$. Which $\delta$ works?

    Answer

    $|f(x)-f(x_0)|=|c-c|=0<\varepsilon$ for every $x$, so any $\delta>0$ works, for example $\delta=1$. Here $\delta$ depends on nothing at all. ∎

(b) $f(x)=ax$ is continuous

  1. What if $a=0$?

    Answer

    Then $f$ is a constant, so (a) applies.

  2. Let $a\neq0$, $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Work backwards: write $|f(x)-f(x_0)|$ in terms of $|x-x_0|$.

    Answer

    $$\begin{aligned}|f(x)-f(x_0)|&=|ax-ax_0|\\&=|a|\,|x-x_0|\end{aligned}.$$

  3. Which $\delta$ makes this smaller than $\varepsilon$? Check it.

    Answer

    We want $|x-x_0|<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{|a|}$, so choose $\delta=\tfrac{\varepsilon}{|a|}>0$. Then $|x-x_0|<\delta$ gives $$\begin{aligned}|f(x)-f(x_0)|&=|a|\,|x-x_0|\\&<|a|\cdot\tfrac{\varepsilon}{|a|}\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$∎

This is the standard move: compute $|f(x)-f(x_0)|$, bound it by something times $|x-x_0|$, then divide $\varepsilon$ by that something. Here $\delta$ depends on $\varepsilon$ but not on $x_0$.

(c) $f(x)=|x|$ is continuous

  1. Let $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Which inequality from lesson 5 compares $\big||x|-|x_0|\big|$ with $|x-x_0|$, and which $\delta$ does it give?

    Answer

    The reverse triangle inequality, $\big||u|-|v|\big|\le|u-v|$. Choose $\delta=\varepsilon$. For $|x-x_0|<\delta$: $$\begin{aligned}|f(x)-f(x_0)|&=\big||x|-|x_0|\big|\\&\le |x-x_0|\\&<\delta\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$∎

(d) $f(x)=x^2$ is continuous (Übung 3.48, p. 155)

This one is instructive, because $\delta$ must depend on $x_0$ as well. First with numbers, at $x_0=3$.

  1. Factor $|x^2-9|$. Which factor does $\delta$ control, and what is the problem with the other one?

    Answer

    $|x^2-9|=|x-3|\cdot|x+3|$. The first factor is what $\delta$ controls. The second depends on $x$ and could be huge if $x$ were far away.

  2. How do you bound $|x+3|$ by a constant?

    Answer

    First promise $|x-3|<1$. Then $2<x<4$, hence $|x+3|<7$, and so $|x^2-9|<7\,|x-3|$.

  3. Which $\delta$ guarantees both $|x-3|<1$ and $7|x-3|<\varepsilon$?

    Answer

    $\delta=\min\left(1,\tfrac{\varepsilon}{7}\right)$: a number below the smaller bound is below both. (Two guards at a gate: you must satisfy the stricter one.)

  4. Why is the $1$ in the minimum necessary?

    Answer

    For $\varepsilon=100$, $\tfrac\varepsilon7\approx14.3$ alone would allow $x=17$. Then $|x+3|=20$, not $<7$, and $|x^2-9|=280>100$: the proof breaks. The $\min$ caps $\delta$ at $1$ and prevents that.

  5. Now a general $x_0$. Let $\varepsilon>0$. If $|x-x_0|<1$, bound $|x+x_0|$.

    Answer

    $|x|<|x_0|+1$, so $|x+x_0|\le|x|+|x_0|<2|x_0|+1$ (triangle inequality).

  6. Choose $\delta$ and finish.

    Answer

    $\delta=\min\left(1,\ \frac{\varepsilon}{2|x_0|+1}\right)>0$. Then $|x-x_0|<\delta$ gives $|x-x_0|<1$, so the bound holds, and $$\begin{aligned}|x^2-x_0^2|&=|x-x_0|\,|x+x_0|\\&<\frac{\varepsilon}{2|x_0|+1}\cdot(2|x_0|+1)=\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$ ∎

The $\min(1,\dots)$ trick is the same one from lesson 5. Use it whenever a factor needs bounding before you can divide by it. Note that $\delta$ shrinks as $|x_0|$ grows: far out, the parabola is steep.

Moves used: minimum of two δs

(e) The floor function is not continuous at any $n\in\mathbb{Z}$

Let $n\in\mathbb{Z}$, so $f(n)=\lfloor n\rfloor=n$.

  1. What does the negation from section 2 ask you to produce, and which $\varepsilon$ do you name?

    Answer

    One $\varepsilon>0$ such that for every $\delta>0$ there is an $x$ with $|x-n|<\delta$ and $|f(x)-f(n)|\ge\varepsilon$. The jump has height $1$, so name half of it: $\varepsilon=\tfrac12$.

  2. Let $\delta>0$ be arbitrary. Which $x$ do you take, and why the $\min$?

    Answer

    $x=n-\tfrac12\min(\delta,1)$. Then $|x-n|=\tfrac12\min(\delta,1)<\delta$, so $x$ is in the window. And $n-1<x<n$, because $0<\tfrac12\min(\delta,1)\le\tfrac12<1$, so $\lfloor x\rfloor=n-1$. The $\min$ is needed because $\delta$ might be huge, and $x$ must still land in $(n-1,n)$.

  3. Finish.

    Answer

    $$\begin{aligned}|f(x)-f(n)|&=|(n-1)-n|\\&=1\\&\ge \tfrac12\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$So the negation holds, and $f$ is not continuous at $n$. ∎

4. The rules that save you from ε-δ

Nobody proves continuity of $\dfrac{x^3-2x}{x^2+1}$ with ε and δ. You build it from pieces instead. All four statements below are proved in the script; the sum proof is Worked example 2 in section 7.

Proposition 3.50 (p. 155). If $f_1,f_2:D\to\mathbb{R}$ are continuous at $x_0$, then so are $f_1+f_2$, $f_1\cdot f_2$ and $af_1$ for $a\in\mathbb{R}$. So the continuous functions $C(D)$ form a vector subspace of all functions.

Korollar 3.51 (p. 156). Every polynomial is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. Why: $x\mapsto x$ and the constants are continuous by section 3, and induction with Proposition 3.50 builds every $\sum_{k=0}^n a_kx^k$.

Proposition 3.52 (p. 156). If $f$ is continuous at $x_0$ and $g$ is continuous at $f(x_0)$, then $g\circ f$ is continuous at $x_0$. Why: chain the two games. Given $\varepsilon$, continuity of $g$ gives an $\eta>0$; feed that $\eta$ as the “$\varepsilon$” into continuity of $f$ to get $\delta$.

Übung 3.53 (p. 157). $x\mapsto\tfrac1x$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}^\times=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$, hence $\tfrac{f}{g}$ is continuous wherever $f,g$ are continuous and $g$ has no zero.

Exam technique: the continuity checklist

Asked whether a function is continuous? Work down this list:

  1. Is it built from polynomials, $|\cdot|$, $\sqrt{\ }$, $\sin$, $\cos$, $\exp$ by $+$, $\cdot$, $\div$ and $\circ$? Then it's continuous everywhere it's defined, by the rules above. Say which rule, and you're done. (For $\sqrt{\ }$ see Übung 3.48, p. 155. The script defines $\exp$, $\sin$ and $\cos$ only later in the course and proves their continuity there; until then, only use polynomials, $|\cdot|$ and $\sqrt{\ }$ as building blocks.)
  2. Is there a division? Check the zeros of the denominator. Those points are the only candidates for trouble.
  3. Is it defined by cases (Fallunterscheidung)? Inside each piece use step 1. At the seam $b$, continuity holds exactly when the two formulas agree there: $f_1(b)=f_2(b)$ (Übung 3.54, p. 157). That's where the exam puts its points.
  4. Is it a jump or something exotic (floor, the Dirichlet function)? Then prove discontinuity with the negation recipe: name $\varepsilon$, take arbitrary $\delta$, build $x$.

5. The intermediate value theorem

We say $c$ lies between $x_1$ and $x_2$ if $x_1\le c\le x_2$ or $x_2\le c\le x_1$ (so the order of the two endpoints doesn't matter).

Satz 3.58, Zwischenwertsatz (p. 159). Let $I\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be an interval, $f:I\to\mathbb{R}$ continuous, and $a,b\in I$. Then for every $c$ between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ there is an $x$ between $a$ and $b$ with

$$f(x)=c.$$

Three warnings, all of which the exam likes:

  • It gives existence, not uniqueness. There may be many such $x$, as in the picture.
  • It needs an interval. On $D=[0,1]\cup[2,3]$ the statement fails: a function can be $0$ on the left piece and $1$ on the right one, continuously, and never take the value $\tfrac12$.
  • It needs continuity on the whole stretch, not just at $a$ and $b$.

6. The proof (worth 7–9 exam points)

This is the proof you must be able to reproduce. It is a direct payoff from lesson 6: the supremum does all the work, so the theorem is really a consequence of completeness.

You'll need these (all from lessons 6 and 7)
  • Supremum, two properties (lesson 6; Satz 2.59, p. 105–106, says a non-empty set that is bounded above has one). $s=\sup X$ means (1) upper bound: $x\le s$ for every $x\in X$, and (2) least: no number $t<s$ is an upper bound.
  • Continuity at $x_0$ (Definition 3.45, p. 153): for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a $\delta>0$ such that $|x-x_0|<\delta$ implies $|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$. As a picture: inside the $\delta$-window around $x_0$, the graph stays inside the $\varepsilon$-band around $f(x_0)$.
  • Trichotomy (from the order axioms): for two real numbers exactly one of $<$, $=$, $>$ holds. So to prove $f(x_0)=c$ it is enough to rule out $f(x_0)<c$ and $f(x_0)>c$.

Proof A: the intermediate value theorem

The idea. Walk along the graph from the left and collect every spot where you are at or below the level $c$. The right-hand edge of that collection, $x_0=\sup X$, is where the curve must cross the line: if $f(x_0)$ were below $c$, the collection would reach further right (breaking “upper bound”); if above, it would end earlier (breaking “least”).

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • An existence claim with no formula: the tool is the supremum. So define a set, check it is non-empty and bounded, and take its sup.
  • To show $f(x_0)=c$: rule out $f(x_0)<c$ and $f(x_0)>c$ (trichotomy). Each case is a contradiction with one sup property.
  • Continuity is used with a chosen $\varepsilon$: the room available is the gap $|f(x_0)-c|$.
The storyWhat you write

Arrange the road so it starts below the level and ends above it.

Without loss of generality $a<b$ and $f(a)\le f(b)$ (else use $-f$, $-c$, or swap $a,b$). If $c=f(a)$ or $c=f(b)$ we are done, so assume $f(a)<c<f(b)$.

Collect every spot at or below the level.

Let $X=\{x\in[a,b]: f(x)\le c\}$. Then $a\in X$, and $X$ is bounded above by $b$.

Its right-hand edge is the candidate.

So $x_0=\sup X$ exists (completeness: a non-empty set that is bounded above has a supremum) and $a\le x_0\le b$. We show $f(x_0)=c$.

If the road were still below the level there, it would stay below a little further right…

Case $f(x_0)<c$. Then $x_0<b$. Continuity at $x_0$ with $\varepsilon=c-f(x_0)$ gives $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ for $x\in[a,b]$, $|x-x_0|<\delta$. Take $x=\min\left(b,x_0+\frac\delta2\right)>x_0$.

…so the collection reaches past its edge.

Then $f(x)<f(x_0)+\varepsilon=c$, so $x\in X$ with $x>x_0$, contradicting that $x_0$ is an upper bound of $X$.

If the road were above the level there, it would be above it a little to the left too…

Case $f(x_0)>c$. Then $x_0>a$. Continuity with $\varepsilon=f(x_0)-c$ gives $\delta>0$; for $x\in(x_0-\delta,x_0]\cap[a,b]$ we get $f(x)>f(x_0)-\varepsilon=c$, so $x\notin X$.

…so the collection already ended earlier.

Since $X\subseteq[a,x_0]$, every element of $X$ is $\le x_0-\delta$. So $x_0-\delta$ is an upper bound smaller than $x_0$, contradicting that $x_0$ is the least upper bound.

Only “exactly at the level” is left.

By trichotomy $f(x_0)=c$, with $x_0\in[a,b]$. ∎

Moves used: reduce to one case (oBdA) the supremum trick continuity with ε = the gap contradict “least”

A run on real numbers. For $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,2]$ with $c=2$: $X=[0,\sqrt2]$, $x_0=\sqrt2$. Pretend $x_0=1.2$: the gap is $2-1.44=0.56$, and $x=1.3$ has $1.69\le2$, so $1.3\in X$ beats the “upper bound” $1.2$. Pretend $x_0=1.6$: the gap is $0.56$, every $x\in(1.45,1.6]$ has $x^2>2$, so $1.45$ is a smaller upper bound. Notice that this proof shows that $\sqrt2$ exists, using only completeness.

The proof in one piece, with every detail

Setup (oBdA). We may assume $a<b$ and $f(a)\le f(b)$. Why we may: if $f(a)>f(b)$, apply the result to $-f$ and the value $-c$; a solution of $-f(x)=-c$ is a solution of $f(x)=c$. Similarly, if $a>b$ we swap the names of $a$ and $b$, and if $a=b$ then $c=f(a)$ and $x=a$ works. This "oBdA" sentence earns a mark in the Kobel-Keller scheme, so write it.

So let $c\in[f(a),f(b)]$. If $c=f(a)$ take $x=a$; if $c=f(b)$ take $x=b$. So assume $f(a)<c<f(b)$.

The set. Define

$$X=\{\,x\in[a,b]\ :\ f(x)\le c\,\}.$$
  • $X\neq\emptyset$, because $f(a)<c$, so $a\in X$.
  • $X\subseteq[a,b]$, so $X$ is bounded above by $b$.

By completeness (lesson 6: a non-empty set that is bounded above has a supremum) the supremum $x_0=\sup X$ exists, and $a\le x_0\le b$. Claim: $f(x_0)=c$. We rule out the two other cases.

Case 1: $f(x_0)<c$ is impossible.

  • Since $f(b)>c>f(x_0)$, we have $x_0\neq b$, so $x_0<b$.
  • Apply continuity at $x_0$ with $\varepsilon=c-f(x_0)>0$: there is $\delta>0$ such that $|x-x_0|<\delta$ implies $|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x\in[a,b]$.
  • Because $x_0<b$, we can pick an $x\in(x_0,\,x_0+\delta)\cap[a,b]$ (for instance $x=\min\left(b,\ x_0+\tfrac{\delta}{2}\right)$).
  • For this $x$:
    $$\begin{aligned}f(x)&=f(x_0)+\big(f(x)-f(x_0)\big)\\&<f(x_0)+\varepsilon\\&=c\end{aligned},$$
    since $\varepsilon=c-f(x_0)$.
  • So $f(x)\le c$, which means $x\in X$. But $x>x_0=\sup X$, contradicting that $x_0$ is an upper bound of $X$.

Case 2: $f(x_0)>c$ is impossible.

  • Since $f(a)<c<f(x_0)$, we have $x_0\neq a$, so $x_0>a$.
  • Apply continuity at $x_0$ with $\varepsilon=f(x_0)-c>0$, giving some $\delta>0$ as above.
  • For every $x\in(x_0-\delta,\,x_0)\cap[a,b]$:
    $$\begin{aligned}f(x)&=f(x_0)+\big(f(x)-f(x_0)\big)\\&>f(x_0)-\varepsilon\\&=c\end{aligned}.$$
  • So no such $x$ lies in $X$, that is, $(x_0-\delta,x_0)\cap[a,b]\cap X=\emptyset$. Also $x_0\notin X$, because $f(x_0)>c$. Together with $X\subseteq[a,x_0]$ (as $x_0$ is an upper bound), every element of $X$ is $\le x_0-\delta$.
  • So $x_0-\delta$ is an upper bound of $X$ smaller than $x_0$, contradicting $x_0=\sup X$ being the least upper bound.

By trichotomy, $f(x_0)=c$, and $x_0\in[a,b]$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure

Six beats. If you can say these in order, you can rebuild the proof:

  1. oBdA $a<b$, $f(a)\le f(b)$; handle $c=f(a)$ and $c=f(b)$ separately.
  2. Define $X=\{x\in[a,b]: f(x)\le c\}$.
  3. Non-empty and bounded ⇒ $x_0=\sup X$ exists (completeness).
  4. If $f(x_0)<c$: continuity with $\varepsilon=c-f(x_0)$ pushes a point of $X$ to the right of $x_0$. Contradiction with "upper bound".
  5. If $f(x_0)>c$: continuity with $\varepsilon=f(x_0)-c$ makes $x_0-\delta$ an upper bound. Contradiction with "least".
  6. Hence $f(x_0)=c$.

The two cases are mirror images: in both you choose $\varepsilon$ as the gap $|f(x_0)-c|$, which is exactly the amount of room you have. Remember that one idea and the rest follows.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why do we need $x_0<b$ in the first case?

    Answer

    To find a point of $[a,b]$ to the right of $x_0$. It holds because $f(b)>c>f(x_0)$, so $x_0\neq b$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Which sup property does each case contradict?

    Answer

    $f(x_0)<c$: a point of $X$ right of $x_0$ breaks (1), “upper bound”. $f(x_0)>c$: the smaller upper bound $x_0-\delta$ breaks (2), “least”.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Where is it used that the domain is an interval?

    Answer

    $X\subseteq[a,b]\subseteq I$, and $f$ is defined and continuous on all of $[a,b]$, so the points $x$ near $x_0$ that we pick are in the domain. On $[0,1]\cup[2,3]$ the point $\min(b,x_0+\frac\delta2)$ could fall in the gap.

  4. Rung 4 (8 min). Write the whole proof from the six beats alone.

7. Using the theorem: worked examples

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

Worked example 1: a root of $x^5-3x+1$

  1. Why is $f(x)=x^5-3x+1$ continuous?

    Answer

    It's a polynomial, and polynomials are continuous (section 4).

  2. Find two points with opposite signs.

    Answer

    $f(0)=1>0$ and $f(1)=1-3+1=-1<0$.

  3. Conclude, and state the general pattern.

    Answer

    $0$ lies between $f(1)$ and $f(0)$, so by the IVT some $x\in(0,1)$ has $f(x)=0$. ∎ Pattern: for “$g(x)=h(x)$ has a solution”, set $f=g-h$, check continuity, find opposite signs.

Worked example 2: sums of continuous functions (Proposition 3.50, p. 155)

If $f_1,f_2$ are continuous at $x_0$, so is $f_1+f_2$.

  1. Let $\varepsilon>0$. How much room do you give each function, and what do you get?

    Answer

    $\frac\varepsilon2$ each: $\delta_1>0$ with $|f_1(x)-f_1(x_0)|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $|x-x_0|<\delta_1$, and $\delta_2$ likewise for $f_2$. Allowed, because the definition holds for every positive number.

  2. Which single $\delta$ makes both hold?

    Answer

    $\delta=\min(\delta_1,\delta_2)>0$.

  3. Finish with the triangle inequality.

    Answer

    For $|x-x_0|<\delta$: $$\begin{aligned}&|(f_1+f_2)(x)-(f_1+f_2)(x_0)|\\&\le|f_1(x)-f_1(x_0)|+|f_2(x)-f_2(x_0)|\\&<\tfrac\varepsilon2+\tfrac\varepsilon2=\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$ ∎

Moves used: ε/2 twice minimum of two δs

Worked example 3: a seam (Übung 3.54, p. 157)

For which $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}$ is $f(x)=x^2+1$ for $x<2$, $f(x)=\alpha x$ for $x\ge2$, continuous on $\mathbb{R}$?

  1. Where is continuity automatic?

    Answer

    On $(-\infty,2)$ and $(2,\infty)$: there $f$ agrees locally with a polynomial (checklist step 1).

  2. What must happen at the seam $x_0=2$?

    Answer

    The formulas must agree: $2^2+1=5$ and $2\alpha$. So $\alpha=\frac52$.

  3. Check $\alpha=\frac52$ with ε-δ.

    Answer

    Left: $|x^2+1-5|=|x-2||x+2|$, which is $<5|x-2|$ when $|x-2|<1$, so $\delta_1=\min(1,\frac\varepsilon5)$. Right: $|\frac52x-5|=\frac52|x-2|$, so $\delta_2=\frac{2\varepsilon}5$. Take $\delta=\min(\delta_1,\delta_2)$.

  4. What if $\alpha\neq\frac52$?

    Answer

    A jump at $2$: the negation recipe with $\varepsilon=\frac12|5-2\alpha|$ proves discontinuity.

Why it's cool. The intermediate value theorem is an existence machine: it proves that $x^5-3x+1$ has a root between $0$ and $1$ without ever computing it. Its proof is the first place where completeness does real work: on $\mathbb Q$ the theorem is false ($x^2-2$ changes sign but has no rational root).

8. Your turn

Exercise 1 (ε-δ by hand)

(a) Prove that $f(x)=3x-4$ is continuous at every $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$.
(b) Prove that $f(x)=x^2+x$ is continuous at $x_0=1$. Hint: factor $f(x)-f(1)$ and use $\min(1,\dots)$.
(c) Prove that $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ with $f(x)=1$ for $x\ge0$ and $f(x)=0$ for $x<0$ is not continuous at $0$.

Solution

(a) Frame: first line “Let $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\varepsilon>0$”, then “Choose $\delta=\dots$”, last line “so $|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$”. The gap: find $\delta$ by computing $|f(x)-f(x_0)|$ first. Moves: continuity with a chosen ε.

Let $x_0\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Then $$|f(x)-f(x_0)|=|3x-3x_0|=3|x-x_0|.$$Choose $\delta=\tfrac\varepsilon3>0$. If $|x-x_0|<\delta$, then $$|f(x)-f(x_0)|=3|x-x_0|<3\cdot\tfrac\varepsilon3=\varepsilon.$$∎

(b) Frame: as in (a), at $x_0=1$; the gap is a factor that must be bounded before you divide by it. Moves: minimum of two δs.

$f(1)=2$. Factor:

$$\begin{aligned}|f(x)-f(1)|&=|x^2+x-2|\\&=|x-1|\,|x+2|\end{aligned}.$$
  • Tame $|x+2|$: if $|x-1|<1$, then $0<x<2$, so $|x+2|<4$.
  • Choose $\delta=\min\left(1,\ \tfrac\varepsilon4\right)>0$.
  • Then $|x-1|<\delta$ gives $|f(x)-f(1)|=|x-1||x+2|$, and this is $<\tfrac\varepsilon4\cdot4=\varepsilon$. ∎

(c) Frame: the negation: “Let $\varepsilon=\dots$. Let $\delta>0$ be arbitrary. Take $x=\dots$”, ending with “$|x-0|<\delta$ and $|f(x)-f(0)|\ge\varepsilon$”. Moves: the negation recipe.

$f(0)=1$. Choose $\varepsilon=\tfrac12$. Let $\delta>0$ be arbitrary and set $x=-\tfrac\delta2$. Then $|x-0|=\tfrac\delta2<\delta$, and $x<0$, so $f(x)=0$ and

$$|f(x)-f(0)|=|0-1|=1\ge\tfrac12=\varepsilon.$$

So the negation holds and $f$ is not continuous at $0$. ∎ (Any $\varepsilon\le1$ works.)

Exercise 2 (the checklist, no ε-δ)

Where is each function continuous? Name the rule you use.

(a) $\dfrac{x^3-2x}{x^2+1}$   (b) $\dfrac{1}{x^2-4}$   (c) $\big|x^2-3\big|$   (d) $f(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{x^2-1}{x-1},&x\neq1\\ 2,&x=1\end{cases}$

Solution
  • (a) Continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$. Numerator and denominator are polynomials, hence continuous. The denominator $x^2+1\ge1$ is never $0$, so the quotient rule (a quotient of continuous functions is continuous where the denominator is not $0$) applies everywhere.
  • (b) Continuous on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-2,2\}$. Same reasoning, but $x^2-4=0$ exactly at $x=\pm2$. At those points the function isn't defined at all, so the question of continuity doesn't arise there; on its domain it is continuous.
  • (c) Continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. It's the composition $g\circ h$ with $h(x)=x^2-3$ (a polynomial) and $g(y)=|y|$ (continuous by section 3(c)). A composition of continuous functions is continuous (section 4).
  • (d) Continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. For $x\neq1$, $\tfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}=\tfrac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1}=x+1$, and the cancellation is legitimate because $x-1\neq0$. So $f$ agrees with the polynomial $x+1$ away from $1$, and at $x=1$ the defined value is $2=1+1$, which matches. Hence $f(x)=x+1$ for all $x$, a polynomial. If the value at $1$ had been anything other than $2$, there would be a jump there.

Exercise 3 (IVT, exam-style)

(a) Show that $x^3+x=10$ has a real solution.
(b) Übung 3.60, p. 160. Show that every real polynomial of odd degree has a real zero. Hint: for $f(x)=a_nx^n+\dots$ with $a_n>0$ and $n$ odd, argue that $f$ is positive for large $x$ and negative for large negative $x$; write $f(x)=x^n\left(a_n+\tfrac{a_{n-1}}{x}+\dots+\tfrac{a_0}{x^n}\right)$.
(c) Give a continuous $f:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}$ that takes the values $-1$ and $1$ but never $0$ — or explain why none exists.

Solution

(a) Frame: for “an equation has a solution”: move everything to one side, check continuity, find two points with opposite signs, apply the intermediate value theorem.

Let $f(x)=x^3+x-10$, continuous as a polynomial. $f(0)=-10<0$ and $f(3)=27+3-10=20>0$. Since $0$ lies between $f(0)$ and $f(3)$, the IVT gives an $x\in(0,3)$ with $f(x)=0$, that is, $x^3+x=10$. ∎

(b) Frame: same pattern as (a), but the two points must be found for a general polynomial: show $f(R)>0$ and $f(-R)<0$ for one large $R$. Moves: reduce to one case (oBdA).

Let $f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^n a_jx^j$ with $n$ odd and $a_n\neq0$. We may assume $a_n>0$ (otherwise use $-f$, which has the same zeros). For $x\neq0$ write

$$\begin{aligned}f(x)&=x^n\left(a_n+\frac{a_{n-1}}{x}+\dots+\frac{a_0}{x^n}\right)\\&=x^n\big(a_n+r(x)\big)\end{aligned}.$$
  • Each term of $r(x)$ tends to $0$ in size as $|x|$ grows. Concretely, for $|x|\ge R:=1+\tfrac{2}{a_n}\sum_{j<n}|a_j|$ we get $|r(x)|\le\tfrac{1}{|x|}\sum_{j<n}|a_j|\le\tfrac{a_n}{2}$, using $|x|\ge1$ so that $|x|^{n-j}\ge|x|$.
  • Hence $a_n+r(x)\ge\tfrac{a_n}2>0$ for $|x|\ge R$.
  • Since $n$ is odd, $x^n>0$ for $x=R$ and $x^n<0$ for $x=-R$. So $f(R)>0$ and $f(-R)<0$.
  • $f$ is continuous, and $0$ lies between $f(-R)$ and $f(R)$, so the IVT gives a zero in $(-R,R)$. ∎

Why “odd” is essential: $x^2+1$ has even degree and no real zero.

(c) No such $f$ exists. $(0,1)$ is an interval, so if $f(p)=-1$ and $f(q)=1$ for some $p,q\in(0,1)$, the IVT applies with $a=p$, $b=q$ and $c=0$ (which lies between $-1$ and $1$) and produces an $x$ between $p$ and $q$ — hence in $(0,1)$ — with $f(x)=0$. The theorem doesn't need a closed interval, only an interval.

Exercise 4 (Figalli FS26, MC3)

Open the original: FS26 exam

Let $f:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous. Which statement is always true?

(A) $f$ is bounded.   (B) $f$ attains a maximum on $(0,1)$.   (C) $f((0,1))$ is an interval.   (D) $f$ is uniformly continuous on $(0,1)$.

Solution

Answer C.

  • (A) False. $f(x)=\tfrac1x$ is continuous on $(0,1)$ (quotient rule, denominator never $0$ there) but unbounded.
  • (B) False. $f(x)=x$ on $(0,1)$ attains no maximum: its supremum is $1$, which is not in the set, exactly as in lesson 6. Maxima are guaranteed on closed, bounded intervals, which is lesson 8.
  • (C) True. This is the IVT in disguise: if $u<w$ are both in the image, say $u=f(p)$ and $w=f(q)$, then every $c$ between them is $f(x)$ for some $x$ between $p$ and $q$, so $c$ is in the image too. A subset of $\mathbb{R}$ containing everything between any two of its points is an interval (a script exercise, p. 115).
  • (D) False. $f(x)=\tfrac1x$ again: near $0$ the function changes arbitrarily fast, so no single $\delta$ works for all points at once. Uniform continuity is guaranteed on closed bounded intervals (lesson 8).

Exercise 5 (Felder FS23, fixed point)

Open the original: FS23 exam with solutions

Let $I=[a,b]$ and let $f:I\to I$ be continuous (so $f$ maps $I$ into itself). Show that $f$ has a fixed point: some $x\in I$ with $f(x)=x$. Hint: apply the IVT to $g(x)=f(x)-x$.

Then show by example that this fails for $I=\mathbb{R}$.

Solution

Frame: a fixed point is a zero of $g(x)=f(x)-x$, so the pattern of Exercise 3(a) applies: continuity of $g$, opposite signs at $a$ and $b$, then the intermediate value theorem. Moves: a helper function.

Define $g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ by $g(x)=f(x)-x$. It is continuous, as a difference of continuous functions ($f$ and $x\mapsto x$; sums and multiples of continuous functions are continuous).

  • If $f(a)=a$ or $f(b)=b$ we are done, so assume neither.
  • Since $f$ maps into $I=[a,b]$, we have $a\le f(a)\le b$. Combined with $f(a)\neq a$: $f(a)>a$, so $g(a)=f(a)-a>0$.
  • Likewise $f(b)\le b$ and $f(b)\neq b$ give $f(b)<b$, so $g(b)=f(b)-b<0$.
  • $0$ lies between $g(b)<0$ and $g(a)>0$, so by the IVT there is an $x\in[a,b]$ with $g(x)=0$, that is, $f(x)=x$. ∎

Failure on $\mathbb{R}$: $f(x)=x+1$ is continuous and maps $\mathbb{R}$ into $\mathbb{R}$, but $f(x)-x=1\neq0$ for every $x$. The proof used the two endpoints, which $\mathbb{R}$ doesn't have.

Bonus (Übung 3.59, p. 160, and Übung 3.61)

(a) You fly from Zürich to Lima. Show that you must cross the equator.
(b) Let $I$ be a non-empty interval and $f:I\to\mathbb{R}$ continuous and injective. Show that $f$ is strictly monotone. Hint: suppose not, find $x<y<z$ with $f(y)$ not between $f(x)$ and $f(z)$, and apply the IVT twice to produce two points with the same value.

Solution

(a) Let $\varphi(t)$ be your latitude at time $t$, for $t$ from departure $t_0$ to arrival $t_1$. Flight is continuous in time, so $\varphi$ is continuous. Zürich has $\varphi(t_0)\approx+47^\circ>0$ and Lima has $\varphi(t_1)\approx-12^\circ<0$. The value $0$ lies between them, so by the IVT there is a time $t$ with $\varphi(t)=0$: you are on the equator. ∎

(b) Frame: contradiction: “Suppose $f$ is not strictly monotone”, and the last line is “so $f(u)=f(v)$ with $u\neq v$, contradicting injectivity”. Moves: assume the opposite.

Suppose $f$ is continuous and injective on the interval $I$ but not strictly monotone. Then there are $x<y<z$ in $I$ with $f(y)$ not strictly between $f(x)$ and $f(z)$. (If for all such triples $f(y)$ were strictly between, $f$ would be strictly monotone. That step is a script exercise, p. 151; take it as given here.) Injectivity makes all three values distinct, so one of these holds:

  • $f(y)>f(x)$ and $f(y)>f(z)$. Pick $c$ with $\max(f(x),f(z))<c<f(y)$. The IVT on $[x,y]$ gives $u\in(x,y)$ with $f(u)=c$, and on $[y,z]$ gives $v\in(y,z)$ with $f(v)=c$. But $u<y<v$, so $u\neq v$ while $f(u)=f(v)$, contradicting injectivity.
  • $f(y)<f(x)$ and $f(y)<f(z)$. The same argument with $f(y)<c<\min(f(x),f(z))$.

So $f$ is strictly monotone. ∎

9. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

The theorem that waited 2000 years. “A continuous curve from below a line to above it must cross the line” was used as obvious for centuries; even Euclid's very first construction quietly assumes that two circles meet. Bolzano wrote the first real proof in 1817, with the explicit aim of removing geometric intuition from analysis. He could not make it fully rigorous, because nobody had yet defined the real numbers; the missing ingredient was completeness, your axiom 16. Dedekind and Cantor finally constructed $\mathbb{R}$ in 1872. So the gap between “obvious” and “proved” was filled by exactly the thing you learned last lesson.

A trick for parties. At any moment there are two points exactly opposite each other on the equator with the same temperature. Let $T(\theta)$ be the temperature at angle $\theta$ and set $g(\theta)=T(\theta)-T(\theta+180^\circ)$. Then $g(0)=-g(180^\circ)$, so $g$ takes a value and its negative; by the IVT it is $0$ somewhere. That point and its antipode match. A similar IVT argument shows that a four-legged table on an uneven (but continuous) floor can always be made to stand steady by rotating it.

Where your intuition is wrong. Continuity is much weaker than “drawable”. There is a function continuous at every irrational number and discontinuous at every rational one (Thomae's function). Weierstrass shocked his contemporaries in 1872 with a function that is continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere: an unbroken road that is jagged at every single point. You can't draw it, but it exists, and it's built as a sum of infinitely many ever smaller, ever faster waves (lesson 15, uniform convergence).
