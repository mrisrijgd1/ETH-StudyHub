Recap of lesson 8 (5 min)

  • Inverse function theorem: continuous and strictly monotone on an interval $I$ ⇒ $f(I)$ is an interval and $f^{-1}:f(I)\to I$ is continuous and strictly monotone in the same direction.
  • Boundedness theorem and extreme value theorem: $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$ ⇒ $f$ is bounded, and it attains a maximum and a minimum. Both fail without “closed” ($x$ on $[0,1)$, $\frac1x$ on $(0,1]$) or without “bounded” ($x$ on $[0,\infty)$).
  • Uniformly continuous: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is one $\delta>0$ such that $|x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x,y$. The $\delta$ is chosen before the points.
  • Heine's theorem: continuous on $[a,b]$ ⇒ uniformly continuous. Frame: “Let $\varepsilon>0$. Let $X$ be the set of $t\in[a,b]$ for which one $\delta$ works on $[a,t]$.” The goal becomes $b\in X$. (Heine comes back today, in Worked example 2.)
  • Moves: the supremum trick, continuity with a chosen ε, ε/2 twice, the negation recipe, reduce to one case (oBdA). The cards are in the Moves library.

  1. Why is $x^2$ not uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}$, in one sentence?

    Answer

    Far out the graph is steep: for $\varepsilon=1$ and any $\delta>0$, the points $x=\frac1\delta$ and $y=\frac1\delta+\frac\delta2$ are closer than $\delta$, but $y^2-x^2=1+\frac{\delta^2}4\ge1$.

  2. In the proof that the maximum is attained, which helper function did we use, and why?

    Answer

    $F(x)=\frac1{S-f(x)}$ with $S=\sup f$. If $f$ never reached $S$, $F$ would be continuous on $[a,b]$, hence bounded, but $f$ gets arbitrarily close to $S$, which makes $F$ arbitrarily large.

  3. Is $f(x)=\frac1x$ on $[1,2]$ guaranteed a maximum?

    Answer

    Yes. It's continuous and $[1,2]$ is a compact interval, so the extreme value theorem applies. (The maximum is $f(1)=1$.)

1. The story: tiles and canopies

The queen wants to tax every field by its area. But her surveyors only know how to measure one shape: a rectangle, width times height. Fields have curved, hilly edges. How can rectangles ever measure a hill?

Two guilds offer their services. The Tilers lay flat rectangular tiles under the ground, never poking out above it. The Tentmakers stretch flat canopies over the ground, never touching it from below. Every tiling is too small and every canopy is too big, so the true area, if it means anything, must lie in between.

The Royal Assessor makes a rule. A field is measurable if the two guilds can be pushed to agree: for every tolerance $\varepsilon$, however tiny, there is a tiling and a canopy whose areas differ by less than $\varepsilon$. Then exactly one number lies between all tilings and all canopies, and that number is the area.

Most fields pass. But in the far north lies the Glittering Field, where every patch of ground, however narrow, contains enchanted stones standing at height $1$ and plain earth at height $0$. Every tile must sit at height $0$ or lower, every canopy at height $1$ or higher, and the guilds never agree. That field cannot be taxed.

And then there is the Rising Slope, a field that only ever climbs from west to east. There the guilds win with one beautiful trick: the gaps between tiles and canopy slide sideways into a single thin column. Proving that trick works is the main goal of today.

In maths: the rectangles are step functions, the tiles are lower sums, the canopies are upper sums, “measurable” is Riemann integrable, the Glittering Field is the Dirichlet function, and the Rising Slope is any monotone function.

2. Step functions: the rectangles

Throughout, $a<b$ are real numbers and we work on the compact interval $[a,b]$.

Partition (Definition 4.1, p. 178). A partition of $[a,b]$ is a finite list of points

$$Z=\{a=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_{n-1}<x_n=b\}.$$

A partition $Z'$ is finer than $Z$ if it contains all the points of $Z$ (and maybe more). The common refinement of two partitions uses all the points of both.

Step function (Definition 4.2, p. 178). $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ is a step function if there is a partition $Z$ and numbers $c_1,\dots,c_n$ with $f(x)=c_k$ for all $x\in(x_{k-1},x_k)$. At the partition points themselves, $f$ may take any values.

Integral of a step function (Definitions 4.4 and 4.6, p. 179–180).

$$\int_a^b f\,dx=\sum_{k=1}^n c_k\,(x_k-x_{k-1})=\sum_{k=1}^n c_k\,\Delta x_k.$$

Why this is well-defined (Lemma 4.5, p. 179). A step function has many partitions that work: you can always add extra points. So we must check the sum doesn't depend on the partition. Adding one point $y$ inside the piece $(x_{\ell-1},x_\ell)$ splits one rectangle into two with the same height:

$$c_\ell(x_\ell-x_{\ell-1})=c_\ell(x_\ell-y)+c_\ell(y-x_{\ell-1}),$$

so the sum doesn't change. Adding points one at a time (induction) handles any finer partition, and two arbitrary partitions are compared through their common refinement. The values at partition points never enter the sum at all.

Two rules for step functions (Lemmas 4.7 and 4.8, p. 181–182). If $f,g$ are step functions and $s\in\mathbb{R}$, then $f+g$ and $sf$ are step functions, and

$$\begin{aligned}&\int_a^b(f+g)\,dx=\int_a^b f\,dx+\int_a^b g\,dx\\&\int_a^b sf\,dx=s\int_a^b f\,dx\\&f\le g\ \Rightarrow\ \int_a^b f\,dx\le \int_a^b g\,dx\end{aligned}.$$

The reason is the same for all three: take the common refinement of the two partitions. On each of its pieces both $f$ and $g$ are constant, say $c_k$ and $d_k$, and then everything is a statement about the finite sums $\sum c_k\Delta x_k$ and $\sum d_k\Delta x_k$. For example, $f\le g$ gives $c_k\le d_k$ for every $k$, so $\sum c_k\Delta x_k\le\sum d_k\Delta x_k$.

3. Lower and upper integrals (tiles and canopies): the definition of the integral

Now let $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ be any bounded function, say $|f|\le M$. The tiles are step functions $u\le f$, the canopies are step functions $o\ge f$. (Constant step functions $-M$ and $M$ show that both kinds exist.)

Definition 4.10 (p. 183). The set of lower sums and the set of upper sums are

$$\begin{aligned}&\mathcal U(f)=\Big\{\int_a^b u\,dx\ :\ u\text{ step function},\ u\le f\Big\}\\&\mathcal O(f)=\Big\{\int_a^b o\,dx\ :\ o\text{ step function},\ f\le o\Big\}\end{aligned}.$$

Definition 4.11 (p. 183). The lower integral is $\underline I(f)=\sup\mathcal U(f)$, the best tiling. The upper integral is $\overline I(f)=\inf\mathcal O(f)$, the best canopy. $f$ is Riemann integrable if $\underline I(f)=\overline I(f)$, and then this common value is $\int_a^b f\,dx$.

For this definition to make sense, we need the best tiling to be no bigger than the best canopy. That is our first proof.

Proof A: every lower step-function integral is at most every upper one (every tiling is at most every canopy)

Claim (p. 183, inequality (4.2)). For every bounded $f$: if $u\le f\le o$ are step functions, then $\int_a^b u\,dx\le\int_a^b o\,dx$. Consequently $\underline I(f)\le\overline I(f)$.

The idea. A tile lies under the ground and a canopy lies over it, so the tile lies under the canopy, and the step-function rule “$u\le o$ gives $\int u\le\int o$” does the rest. The second sentence is pure supremum logic: each canopy is a ceiling for all tilings, so it's also a ceiling for the best tiling.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $u,o$ be step functions with $u\le f\le o$.”
  • Last line: “…so $\sup\mathcal U(f)\le\inf\mathcal O(f)$.” To prove a $\sup$ is $\le$ something, show that something is an upper bound. To prove something is $\le$ an $\inf$, show it's a lower bound.
  • The gap to fill: compare one tiling with one canopy.
The storyWhat you write

Take any tiling and any canopy over the same field.

Let $u,o$ be step functions with $u\le f\le o$.

The tiles are under the ground, the ground is under the canopy, so the tiles are under the canopy, and their area is smaller.

Then $u\le o$, so by monotonicity of the integral of step functions (section 2), $\int_a^b u\,dx\le\int_a^b o\,dx$.

So any one canopy is a ceiling over all tilings, and therefore over the best tiling too.

Fix $o$. Every element of $\mathcal U(f)$ is $\le\int_a^b o\,dx$, so $\int_a^b o\,dx$ is an upper bound of $\mathcal U(f)$. As the supremum is the least upper bound, $\underline I(f)=\sup\mathcal U(f)\le\int_a^b o\,dx$.

That holds for every canopy, so the best tiling is a floor under all canopies, and therefore under the best canopy.

As $o$ was arbitrary, $\underline I(f)$ is a lower bound of $\mathcal O(f)$. As the infimum is the greatest lower bound, $\underline I(f)\le\inf\mathcal O(f)=\overline I(f)$. ∎

Moves used: sup ≤ an upper bound

The proof in one piece

Let $u,o$ be step functions with $u\le f\le o$. Then $u\le o$, so $\int_a^b u\,dx\le\int_a^b o\,dx$ by monotonicity for step functions. Hence each $\int_a^b o\,dx$ is an upper bound of $\mathcal U(f)$, so $\underline I(f)\le\int_a^b o\,dx$. Hence $\underline I(f)$ is a lower bound of $\mathcal O(f)$, so $\underline I(f)\le\overline I(f)$. ∎

4. The squeeze: the ε-criterion

Computing a supremum over all tilings is hopeless in practice. The following criterion says you only ever need one good pair for each tolerance. It's the tool behind every integrability proof in this course.

The ε-criterion (Proposition 4.12, (i)⇔(iii), p. 184). A bounded function $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ is Riemann integrable if and only if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there are step functions $u\le f\le o$ with

$$\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon.$$

For the exam, cite it as “the ε-criterion for Riemann integrability” and write out the condition once. The script also lists a third equivalent form (ii), “exactly one number lies between all lower and all upper sums”; that's the Royal Assessor's rule from the story.

Proof B: the ε-criterion

The idea. “⇒”: if the best tiling equals the best canopy, then there are tilings and canopies as close to that common value as we like, from below and from above. Take each within $\frac\varepsilon2$. “⇐”: the lower and the upper integral are trapped between $\int u$ and $\int o$, so their distance is less than $\varepsilon$, for every $\varepsilon$. A distance that's smaller than every positive number is $0$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • “⇒” starts with “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” and must end with “…so $u\le f\le o$ and $\int(o-u)<\varepsilon$.” The gap: find $u$ and $o$. They come from the definition of $\sup$ and $\inf$.
  • “⇐” must end with “…so $\underline I(f)=\overline I(f)$.” We already have “$\le$” from Proof A. The gap: show $\overline I(f)-\underline I(f)<\varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon>0$.
The storyWhat you write

(⇒) The field is measurable, with area $I$. The Assessor names a tolerance.

Let $f$ be integrable, $I=\underline I(f)=\overline I(f)$, and let $\varepsilon>0$.

$I$ is the best tiling, so a tiling just a little below $I$ isn't the best: some real tiling beats it.

Since $I-\frac\varepsilon2<I=\sup\mathcal U(f)$, the number $I-\frac\varepsilon2$ is not an upper bound of $\mathcal U(f)$. So there is a step function $u\le f$ with $\int_a^b u\,dx>I-\frac\varepsilon2$.

The same from above, with a canopy.

Likewise, $I+\frac\varepsilon2$ is not a lower bound of $\mathcal O(f)$, so there is a step function $o\ge f$ with $\int_a^b o\,dx<I+\frac\varepsilon2$.

Then the canopy and the tiling are less than $\varepsilon$ apart.

By linearity for step functions, $$\begin{aligned}\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx&=\int_a^b o\,dx-\int_a^b u\,dx\\&<\Big(I+\frac\varepsilon2\Big)-\Big(I-\frac\varepsilon2\Big)\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

(⇐) Now suppose the guilds can always be pushed within any tolerance. The best tiling and the best canopy sit between the given tiling and canopy.

Assume the condition, and let $\varepsilon>0$. Take $u\le f\le o$ with $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$. Then $\int_a^b u\,dx\le\underline I(f)$ (sup is an upper bound) and $\overline I(f)\le\int_a^b o\,dx$ (inf is a lower bound).

So they are less than $\varepsilon$ apart, for every $\varepsilon$, and they can only be equal.

With Proof A: $$0\le\overline I(f)-\underline I(f)\le\int_a^b o\,dx-\int_a^b u\,dx<\varepsilon.$$ As $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $\overline I(f)-\underline I(f)=0$, so $f$ is integrable. ∎

Moves used: approach a supremum ε/2 twice smaller than every ε

The proof in one piece

(⇒) Let $I=\underline I(f)=\overline I(f)$ and $\varepsilon>0$. As $I-\frac\varepsilon2$ is not an upper bound of $\mathcal U(f)$, there is a step function $u\le f$ with $\int_a^b u\,dx>I-\frac\varepsilon2$. Likewise there is a step function $o\ge f$ with $\int_a^b o\,dx<I+\frac\varepsilon2$. By linearity, $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$.

(⇐) Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $u\le f\le o$ with $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$. Then $\int_a^b u\,dx\le\underline I(f)\le\overline I(f)\le\int_a^b o\,dx$, so $0\le\overline I(f)-\underline I(f)<\varepsilon$. As $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $\underline I(f)=\overline I(f)$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure

(⇒)

  1. Approach from below: $I-\frac\varepsilon2$ is not an upper bound of $\mathcal U(f)$, so there's a step function $u\le f$ with $\int u>I-\frac\varepsilon2$.
  2. Approach from above: $I+\frac\varepsilon2$ is not a lower bound of $\mathcal O(f)$, so there's a step function $o\ge f$ with $\int o<I+\frac\varepsilon2$.
  3. Subtract (linearity): $\int(o-u)<\varepsilon$.

(⇐)

  1. Sandwich: $\int u\le\underline I(f)\le\overline I(f)\le\int o$ (Proof A in the middle).
  2. So $0\le\overline I(f)-\underline I(f)<\varepsilon$.
  3. ε arbitrary ⇒ $\underline I(f)=\overline I(f)$.

In short: “ε/2 below, ε/2 above, subtract” and “sandwich, smaller than every ε”.

5. The Glittering Field: a function that is not integrable

Beispiel 4.17 (p. 186). The Dirichlet function $f=\mathbb 1_{\mathbb Q}$ on $[0,1]$, with $f(x)=1$ for rational $x$ and $f(x)=0$ for irrational $x$, has $\overline I(f)=1$ and $\underline I(f)=0$. So it is bounded but not Riemann integrable.

You'll need these
  • $\mathbb Q$ is dense (lesson 6, Korollar 2.70): every interval $(x,y)$ with $x<y$ contains a rational number.
  • The irrationals are dense too: pick a rational $q$ with $x-\sqrt2<q<y-\sqrt2$. Then $q+\sqrt2\in(x,y)$, and it's irrational, because otherwise $\sqrt2=(q+\sqrt2)-q$ would be rational.
  • Telescoping sum: $\sum_{k=1}^n(x_k-x_{k-1})=x_n-x_0$. All the middle terms cancel.

Proof C: the Dirichlet function is not integrable

The idea. Every piece of every partition contains a rational point, where $f=1$, so every canopy has height at least $1$ on every piece: its area is at least $1$. Every piece also contains an irrational point, where $f=0$, so every tile has height at most $0$: its area is at most $0$. The guilds stay a full $1$ apart forever.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • The goal “$\overline I(f)=1$” is about an infimum, so it splits into two lines: “every upper sum is $\ge1$” and “some upper sum equals $1$”.
  • First line: “Let $o$ be a step function with $f\le o$, with partition $Z=\{0=x_0<\dots<x_n=1\}$ and values $c_k$.”
  • The gap to fill: show $c_k\ge1$ for every $k$.
The storyWhat you write

Take any canopy over the Glittering Field, and look at one of its flat pieces.

Let $o\ge f$ be a step function with partition $Z=\{0=x_0<\dots<x_n=1\}$ and $o=c_k$ on $(x_{k-1},x_k)$. Fix $k$.

Under that piece there's an enchanted stone at height $1$, and the canopy must clear it.

By density of $\mathbb Q$ there is a rational $x\in(x_{k-1},x_k)$. Then $1=f(x)\le o(x)=c_k$.

So every piece of the canopy is at height $1$ or more, and the canopy's area is at least the field's width, $1$.

Hence $$\int_0^1 o\,dx=\sum_{k=1}^n c_k\Delta x_k\ge\sum_{k=1}^n\Delta x_k=x_n-x_0=1.$$

A flat canopy at height exactly $1$ is allowed, so the best canopy is exactly $1$.

So $1$ is a lower bound of $\mathcal O(f)$, and the constant step function $o=1\ge f$ has $\int_0^1 o\,dx=1\in\mathcal O(f)$. Hence $\overline I(f)=\min\mathcal O(f)=1$.

From below it's the mirror image, with plain earth at height $0$ under every tile.

Likewise, by density of the irrationals, every step function $u\le f$ has value $\le0$ on every piece, so $\int_0^1 u\,dx\le0$, and $u=0$ gives $0$. Hence $\underline I(f)=0\neq1=\overline I(f)$: $f$ is not integrable. ∎

Moves used: density in every piece telescoping sum

The proof in one piece

Let $o\ge f$ be a step function, constant $=c_k$ on $(x_{k-1},x_k)$ for a partition $0=x_0<\dots<x_n=1$. Each piece contains a rational $x$, so $c_k=o(x)\ge f(x)=1$, and $\int_0^1 o\,dx=\sum c_k\Delta x_k\ge\sum\Delta x_k=1$. Since $o=1$ has integral $1$, $\overline I(f)=1$. Similarly, each piece contains an irrational $x$, so every step function $u\le f$ has $\int_0^1u\,dx\le0$, and $u=0$ gives $\underline I(f)=0$. As $0\neq1$, $f$ is not Riemann integrable. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Upper integral: take any step function $o\ge f$ with its partition. Every piece contains a rational, so every value $c_k\ge1$.
  2. Telescope: $\int o\ge\sum\Delta x_k=1$, and $o=1$ achieves it, so $\overline I(f)=1$.
  3. Lower integral: every piece contains an irrational, so every step function $u\le f$ has values $\le0$, and $u=0$ achieves it, so $\underline I(f)=0$.
  4. Conclude: $0\neq1$, so $f$ is not integrable.

In short: “a rational in every piece, an irrational in every piece”.

    Answer

    Let $u\le f$ be a step function with partition $0=x_0<\dots<x_n=1$ and $u=d_k$ on $(x_{k-1},x_k)$. By density of the irrationals each piece contains an irrational $x$, so $d_k=u(x)\le f(x)=0$. Hence $\int_0^1u\,dx=\sum d_k\Delta x_k\le0$, so $0$ is an upper bound of $\mathcal U(f)$. The step function $u=0$ satisfies $u\le f$ and has integral $0$, so $\underline I(f)=\max\mathcal U(f)=0$.

  3. Rung 3 (5 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone, then compare.

Why it's cool: the Dirichlet function only takes the two values $0$ and $1$, and it's as bounded as a function can be. Being bounded is not enough to be integrable, and neither is having finitely many values. What breaks integrability is wild jumping in every interval. Monotone functions can't do that, as we'll see in section 7.

6. The rules of the integral

These are the rules you'll use all the time when computing. For the exam you need to state them correctly. Their proofs are sup/inf bookkeeping, and no old exam asks for them, so here we prove only monotonicity, which is short. The rest are on the skip list.

Linearity (Satz 4.19, p. 188). If $f,g$ are integrable on $[a,b]$ and $s\in\mathbb{R}$, then $f+g$ and $sf$ are integrable and $\int_a^b(f+g)\,dx=\int_a^b f\,dx+\int_a^b g\,dx$, $\int_a^b sf\,dx=s\int_a^b f\,dx$.

Monotonicity (Satz 4.24 (i), (ii), p. 190). If $f,g$ are integrable, then $f\ge0\Rightarrow\int_a^b f\,dx\ge0$, and $f\le g\Rightarrow\int_a^b f\,dx\le\int_a^b g\,dx$.

Triangle inequality (Satz 4.24 (iii), p. 190). If $f$ is integrable, then so is $|f|$, and $\left|\int_a^b f\,dx\right|\le\int_a^b|f|\,dx$.

Additivity over intervals (Satz 4.26, p. 193). For $a<b<c$: $f$ is integrable on $[a,c]$ if and only if it is integrable on $[a,b]$ and on $[b,c]$, and then $\int_a^c f\,dx=\int_a^b f\,dx+\int_b^c f\,dx$. With the convention $\int_b^a f\,dx=-\int_a^b f\,dx$ and $\int_a^a f\,dx=0$, the formula holds for $a,b,c$ in any order (Wichtige Übung 4.27).

Changing finitely many values (Übung 4.22, p. 190) changes neither integrability nor the integral. (Your Exercise 6 does one point.)

Proof D: monotonicity, and the triangle inequality

The idea. For $f\ge0$, the flat tile at height $0$ is a legal tiling, so the best tiling is at least $0$. For $f\le g$, apply that to $g-f\ge0$. For the triangle inequality, use $-|f|\le f\le|f|$ and monotonicity twice. (That $|f|$ is integrable is part of the theorem, and we take it from the script here.)

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (i) must end with “$\int_a^b f\,dx\ge0$”, and $\int_a^b f\,dx=\sup\mathcal U(f)$, so it's enough to find one element of $\mathcal U(f)$ that is $\ge0$.
  • (ii) and (iii) are inequalities between integrals, so the first move is to turn them into “an integral is $\ge0$” and use (i).
The storyWhat you write

(i) If the ground is never below sea level, a tile lying flat on sea level is allowed. So the best tiling is at least $0$.

Let $f\ge0$ be integrable. The step function $u=0$ satisfies $u\le f$, so $0=\int_a^b u\,dx\in\mathcal U(f)$, and $0\le\sup\mathcal U(f)=\int_a^b f\,dx$.

(ii) If one field is everywhere at least as high as another, the difference is never below sea level.

Let $f\le g$ be integrable. By linearity $g-f$ is integrable, and $g-f\ge0$, so by (i) $$\int_a^b g\,dx-\int_a^b f\,dx=\int_a^b(g-f)\,dx\ge0.$$

(iii) The field lies between its own reflection $-|f|$ and its height $|f|$, so its area does too.

Let $f$ be integrable; then $|f|$ is integrable (part of the theorem, taken from the script). From $-|f|\le f\le|f|$, (ii) and linearity: $-\int_a^b|f|\,dx\le\int_a^b f\,dx\le\int_a^b|f|\,dx$, which says $\left|\int_a^b f\,dx\right|\le\int_a^b|f|\,dx$. ∎

Moves used: sup ≥ any element reduce to “≥ 0”

The proof in one piece

(i) If $f\ge0$, then $u=0$ is a step function with $u\le f$, so $0\in\mathcal U(f)$ and $\int_a^b f\,dx=\sup\mathcal U(f)\ge0$. (ii) If $f\le g$, then $g-f$ is integrable by linearity and $g-f\ge0$, so by (i) $\int_a^b g\,dx-\int_a^b f\,dx=\int_a^b(g-f)\,dx\ge0$. (iii) $|f|$ is integrable (taken from the script). From $-|f|\le f\le|f|$, (ii) and linearity give $-\int_a^b|f|\,dx\le\int_a^b f\,dx\le\int_a^b|f|\,dx$, that is, $\left|\int_a^b f\,dx\right|\le\int_a^b|f|\,dx$. ∎

What the integral means (Section 4.4, p. 195–198). One definition, many meanings. If $f\ge0$, $\int_a^b f\,dx$ is the area under the graph. Defined as “the area under the curve”, it would be circular, since we have no definition of area for curved shapes without it.

7. The Rising Slope: monotone functions are integrable (worth 7 exam points)

Satz 4.31 (p. 199). Every monotone function $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ is Riemann integrable.

You'll need these
  • Monotonically increasing (monoton wachsend): $x\le y\Rightarrow f(x)\le f(y)$. Not strict; flat parts are allowed.
  • Monotone ⇒ bounded on $[a,b]$: $f(a)\le f(x)\le f(b)$ for all $x$. (Exercise 3 of lesson 8.) So the definition applies.
  • The ε-criterion from section 4.
  • Archimedean principle (lesson 6): for every real $r$ there is $n\in\mathbb N$ with $n>r$.
  • Telescoping sum: $$\sum_{k=1}^n\big(f(x_k)-f(x_{k-1})\big)=f(x_n)-f(x_0).$$

Colour codetiles $\class{k-del}{u}$ green, canopy $\class{k-c}{o}$ rose, $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$ blue.

Proof E: monotone functions are integrable

The idea. Cut $[a,b]$ into $n$ pieces of equal width $\frac{b-a}n$. On each piece, an increasing $f$ is lowest at the left end and highest at the right end. So tile each piece at its left-end height and put the canopy at its right-end height. The gap on piece $k$ is a thin rectangle of height $f(x_k)-f(x_{k-1})$. Now slide all the gaps sideways into one column: their heights stack up exactly from $f(a)$ to $f(b)$, so the total gap is one rectangle of width $\frac{b-a}n$ and height $f(b)-f(a)$. Choose $n$ large and it's as thin as you like.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • The goal “$f$ is Riemann integrable” with the ε-criterion forces the first line “Let $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}>0$.” and the last lines “…so $\class{k-del}{u}\le f\le\class{k-c}{o}$ and $\int_a^b(\class{k-c}{o}-\class{k-del}{u})\,dx<\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$. By the ε-criterion, $f$ is integrable.”
  • Before that: one line for “oBdA increasing”, one line for “$f$ is bounded”.
  • The gap to fill: build $\class{k-del}{u}$ and $\class{k-c}{o}$ (equal pieces, left and right ends), check $\class{k-del}{u}\le f\le\class{k-c}{o}$, compute $\int(\class{k-c}{o}-\class{k-del}{u})$, choose $n$.
The storyWhat you write

A slope that only falls is a rising slope seen from the other side. So we only treat the rising one.

Without loss of generality $f$ is increasing; otherwise replace $f$ by $-f$, which is increasing, and use linearity ($f=(-1)\cdot(-f)$). Then $f(a)\le f(x)\le f(b)$ for all $x\in[a,b]$, so $f$ is bounded.

The Assessor names a tolerance. The guilds cut the field into $n$ equal strips; they'll choose how many at the end.

Let $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}>0$ and $n\in\mathbb N$ (chosen below). Let $x_k=a+k\frac{b-a}n$ for $k=0,\dots,n$, so $\Delta x_k=\frac{b-a}n$.

On each strip, tiles at the height of the strip's west end, the lowest point of that strip. The canopy at the height of its east end, the highest point.

Define step functions $$\class{k-del}{u}(x)=\begin{cases}f(x_{k-1})&x\in[x_{k-1},x_k),\ k=1,\dots,n\\ f(b)&x=b,\end{cases}$$ $$\class{k-c}{o}(x)=\begin{cases}f(a)&x=a\\ f(x_k)&x\in(x_{k-1},x_k],\ k=1,\dots,n.\end{cases}$$

Because the slope only rises, the west end really is lowest and the east end really is highest. So the tiles stay under the ground and the canopy over it.

$\class{k-del}{u}\le f$: if $x=b$, then $\class{k-del}{u}(x)=f(x)$. Otherwise $x\in[x_{k-1},x_k)$ for some $k$, and $x_{k-1}\le x$ gives $\class{k-del}{u}(x)=f(x_{k-1})\le f(x)$. Likewise $f\le\class{k-c}{o}$: for $x\in(x_{k-1},x_k]$, $x\le x_k$ gives $f(x)\le f(x_k)=\class{k-c}{o}(x)$. Also $\class{k-c}{o}(a)=f(a)$.

The gaps: one thin rectangle per strip. Slide them into one column; the heights telescope from $f(a)$ to $f(b)$.

On $(x_{k-1},x_k)$, $\class{k-c}{o}-\class{k-del}{u}=f(x_k)-f(x_{k-1})$. So $$\begin{aligned}\int_a^b(\class{k-c}{o}-\class{k-del}{u})\,dx&=\sum_{k=1}^n\big(f(x_k)-f(x_{k-1})\big)\frac{b-a}n\\&=\frac{b-a}n\big(f(b)-f(a)\big),\end{aligned}$$ by the telescoping sum.

Make the strips thin enough and the column is thinner than the tolerance.

By the Archimedean principle choose $n>\frac{(b-a)(f(b)-f(a))}{\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}}$. Then $\int_a^b(\class{k-c}{o}-\class{k-del}{u})\,dx<\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}$, with $\class{k-del}{u}\le f\le\class{k-c}{o}$. As $\class{k-eps}{\varepsilon}>0$ was arbitrary, $f$ is Riemann integrable by the ε-criterion. ∎

Moves used: reduce to one case (oBdA) the ε-criterion telescoping sum choose n by Archimedes

The proof in one piece (as you'd write it in the exam)

Without loss of generality $f$ is increasing (otherwise consider $-f$ and use linearity). Then $f(a)\le f\le f(b)$, so $f$ is bounded. Let $\varepsilon>0$, $n\in\mathbb N$, and $x_k=a+k\frac{b-a}n$ for $k=0,\dots,n$. Define step functions $u,o$ by $u=f(x_{k-1})$ on $[x_{k-1},x_k)$, $u(b)=f(b)$, and $o=f(x_k)$ on $(x_{k-1},x_k]$, $o(a)=f(a)$. Since $f$ is increasing, $u\le f\le o$. Moreover

$$\begin{aligned}\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx&=\sum_{k=1}^n\big(f(x_k)-f(x_{k-1})\big)\frac{b-a}n\\&=\frac{(b-a)\big(f(b)-f(a)\big)}n\end{aligned},$$

which is $<\varepsilon$ if we choose $n>\frac{(b-a)(f(b)-f(a))}{\varepsilon}$ (Archimedean principle). By the ε-criterion, $f$ is Riemann integrable. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. oBdA increasing (else $-f$). Then $f(a)\le f\le f(b)$, so $f$ is bounded.
  2. Let $\varepsilon>0$ and cut into $n$ equal pieces: $x_k=a+k\frac{b-a}n$.
  3. Define $u$ and $o$: $u=f(x_{k-1})$ on $[x_{k-1},x_k)$, $o=f(x_k)$ on $(x_{k-1},x_k]$, plus $u(b)=f(b)$, $o(a)=f(a)$. Monotone ⇒ $u\le f\le o$.

In short: “oBdA, equal pieces, $u$ at left ends and $o$ at right ends, telescope, Archimedes, ε-criterion”.

Consequences: piecewise monotone functions and polynomials

Korollar 4.34 (p. 200). A bounded function that is monotone on each piece $(x_{k-1},x_k)$ of some partition is integrable. Why: on each closed piece $[x_{k-1},x_k]$, change $f$ at the two endpoints to make it monotone there (changing finitely many values doesn't matter), apply Satz 4.31, and glue the pieces with additivity over intervals.

So $x^2$ is integrable on any $[a,b]$ (decreasing on $(a,0)$, increasing on $(0,b)$), and so is every monomial $x^d$. With linearity, every polynomial is integrable on every compact interval. (Satz 4.37, p. 202, then computes $\int_a^b x^d\,dx=\frac{b^{d+1}-a^{d+1}}{d+1}$ by hand; in lesson 19 you'll get this in one line from the fundamental theorem of calculus.)

Worked example 1: compute $\int_0^1 x\,dx$ with lower and upper sums

  1. Why is $f(x)=x$ integrable on $[0,1]$, before computing anything?

    Answer

    It's increasing, so it's integrable because monotone functions are integrable (section 7).

  2. Cut $[0,1]$ into $n$ equal pieces. Which values should the lower step function $u$ and the upper step function $o$ take on piece $k$?

    Answer

    The piece is $[\frac{k-1}n,\frac kn]$. Since $f$ is increasing, take the value at the left end for $u$, $u=\frac{k-1}n$, and at the right end for $o$, $o=\frac kn$.

  3. Compute $\int_0^1 u\,dx$ and $\int_0^1 o\,dx$. (You need $1+2+\dots+m=\frac{m(m+1)}2$.)

    Answer
    $$\begin{aligned}&\int_0^1u\,dx=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{k-1}n\cdot\frac1n=\frac{(n-1)n}{2n^2}=\frac{n-1}{2n}\\&\int_0^1o\,dx=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac kn\cdot\frac1n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2n^2}=\frac{n+1}{2n}\end{aligned}.$$

  4. Where does $I=\int_0^1x\,dx$ lie, and why?

    Answer

    $\int u\,dx\in\mathcal U(f)$, so $\int u\,dx\le\sup\mathcal U(f)=I$. Likewise $I=\inf\mathcal O(f)\le\int o\,dx$. So $\frac{n-1}{2n}\le I\le\frac{n+1}{2n}$, for every $n$.

  5. Conclude the value.

    Answer

    Both bounds are $\frac12\mp\frac1{2n}$, so $|I-\frac12|\le\frac1{2n}$ for every $n\in\mathbb N$. If $I\neq\frac12$, the Archimedean principle gives $n>\frac1{2|I-1/2|}$, so $\frac1{2n}<|I-\frac12|$: a contradiction. So $\int_0^1x\,dx=\frac12$, the area of the triangle, as it should be.

Worked example 2: continuous functions are integrable (Figalli HS23 B1, 7 points; Satz 4.42, p. 205)

Show that every continuous function $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ is Riemann integrable. This is the twin of section 7: same frame, different gap.

  1. Write the frame: the first line, and the last lines.

    Answer

    First: “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” Last: “…so $u\le f\le o$ and $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$. By the ε-criterion, $f$ is integrable.” Also needed early: “$f$ is bounded by the boundedness theorem”.

  2. In section 7, monotonicity told us where $f$ is lowest and highest on each piece. What replaces it here, and which theorem do you use?

    Answer

    On each closed piece $[x_{k-1},x_k]$, $f$ is continuous, so by the extreme value theorem it attains a minimum $m_k$ and a maximum $M_k$. Define the step functions by $u=m_k$ and $o=M_k$ on $[x_{k-1},x_k)$, and $u(b)=m_n$, $o(b)=M_n$. Then $u\le f\le o$.

  3. The gap on piece $k$ is $M_k-m_k$. We want the total $\sum(M_k-m_k)\Delta x_k$ to be $<\varepsilon$. What would be enough for each $M_k-m_k$?

    Answer

    $M_k-m_k<\frac\varepsilon{b-a}$ for every $k$, because then

    $$\begin{aligned}\sum_{k=1}^n(M_k-m_k)\Delta x_k&<\frac\varepsilon{b-a}\sum_{k=1}^n\Delta x_k\\&=\frac\varepsilon{b-a}(b-a)\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

  4. Which theorem from lesson 8 makes the gap that small on every piece at once, and how do you choose the partition?

    Answer

    Heine's theorem: $f$ is uniformly continuous, so for $\frac\varepsilon{b-a}$ there is one $\delta>0$ with $$|x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(y)|<\frac\varepsilon{b-a}.$$Take equal pieces of width $\frac{b-a}n<\delta$ (Archimedes). On piece $k$, $m_k=f(z)$ and $M_k=f(z')$ with $z,z'$ in the same piece, so $|z-z'|\le\frac{b-a}n<\delta$, and $M_k-m_k<\frac\varepsilon{b-a}$.

  5. Write the proof in one piece.

    Answer

    Let $\varepsilon>0$. By Heine's theorem $f$ is uniformly continuous, so there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(x)-f(y)|<\frac\varepsilon{b-a}$ whenever $|x-y|<\delta$. Choose $n\in\mathbb N$ with $\frac{b-a}n<\delta$ and let $x_k=a+k\frac{b-a}n$. By the extreme value theorem, $f$ attains a minimum $m_k=f(z_k)$ and a maximum $M_k=f(z_k')$ on $[x_{k-1},x_k]$. Since $|z_k-z_k'|<\delta$, $M_k-m_k<\frac\varepsilon{b-a}$. Define step functions $u=m_k$, $o=M_k$ on $[x_{k-1},x_k)$ and $u(b)=m_n$, $o(b)=M_n$. Then $u\le f\le o$ and

    $$\begin{aligned}\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx&=\sum_{k=1}^n(M_k-m_k)\frac{b-a}n\\&<\frac\varepsilon{b-a}\cdot(b-a)\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$

    By the ε-criterion, $f$ is Riemann integrable. ∎

Moves used: the ε-criterion choose n by Archimedes continuity with a chosen ε (here uniform: $\frac\varepsilon{b-a}$)

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Let $\varepsilon>0$. Heine ⇒ one $\delta$ with $$|x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(y)|<\frac\varepsilon{b-a}.$$
  2. Equal pieces of width $\frac{b-a}n<\delta$ (Archimedes).
  3. $u$ = minimum, $o$ = maximum on each piece: extreme value theorem on each piece gives $m_k$, $M_k$. Put $u=m_k$, $o=M_k$.
  4. Small gaps: $m_k$, $M_k$ are taken at points less than $\delta$ apart, so $M_k-m_k<\frac\varepsilon{b-a}$.
  5. Sum: $\int(o-u)<\frac\varepsilon{b-a}(b-a)=\varepsilon$. ε-criterion ⇒ integrable.

Exercise 3 (the twin of Worked example 1, Übung 4.15, p. 186)

Show $\int_0^1x^2\,dx=\frac13$ with $n$ equal pieces. You need $1^2+2^2+\dots+m^2=\frac{m(m+1)(2m+1)}6$.

Solution

Frame: as in Worked example 1: integrable first, then the lower and upper sums for $n$ equal pieces, then trap $I$ between them and let Archimedes finish. Moves: choose n by Archimedes.

Integrable: $x^2$ is increasing on $[0,1]$, and monotone functions are integrable. On piece $k$, $[\frac{k-1}n,\frac kn]$, $u=\frac{(k-1)^2}{n^2}$ (left end) and $o=\frac{k^2}{n^2}$ (right end). So

$$\begin{aligned}&\int_0^1u\,dx=\frac1{n^3}\sum_{k=1}^n(k-1)^2=\frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{6n^3}\\&\int_0^1o\,dx=\frac1{n^3}\sum_{k=1}^nk^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^3}\end{aligned}.$$

Bounds: expand: $$\begin{aligned}\frac{(n-1)(2n-1)}{6n^2}&=\frac{2n^2-3n+1}{6n^2}\\&=\frac13-\frac1{2n}+\frac1{6n^2}\end{aligned}$$and $\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}=\frac13+\frac1{2n}+\frac1{6n^2}$. Frame: as in section 7. Now $f$ is highest at the left end of each piece, so $u$ takes the value at the right end and $o$ the value at the left end.

Proof. $f(b)\le f\le f(a)$, so $f$ is bounded. Let $\varepsilon>0$, $n\in\mathbb N$, $x_k=a+k\frac{b-a}n$. Let $u=f(x_k)$ on $(x_{k-1},x_k]$, $u(a)=f(a)$, and $o=f(x_{k-1})$ on $[x_{k-1},x_k)$, $o(b)=f(b)$. For $x\in(x_{k-1},x_k]$, $x\le x_k$ gives $f(x)\ge f(x_k)=u(x)$; and $u(a)=f(a)$. So $u\le f$, and likewise $f\le o$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx&=\sum_{k=1}^n\big(f(x_{k-1})-f(x_k)\big)\frac{b-a}n\\&=\frac{(b-a)\big(f(a)-f(b)\big)}n\\&<\varepsilon\end{aligned}$$

for $n>\frac{(b-a)(f(a)-f(b))}\varepsilon$. By the ε-criterion, $f$ is integrable. ∎

Moves used: the ε-criterion telescoping sum choose n by Archimedes

Exercise 5 (which theorems?)

Explain in two or three sentences each why these are integrable: (a) $f(x)=x^2$ on $[-1,2]$, (b) $f(x)=\sqrt{1-x^2}$ on $[0,1]$ (Übung 4.32, p. 200), (c) $f(x)=\lfloor x\rfloor$ on $[0,3]$.

Solution

(a) $x^2$ is decreasing on $[-1,0]$ and increasing on $[0,2]$, so it is integrable on each because monotone functions are integrable, and on $[-1,2]$ by additivity over intervals. (Or: it's a polynomial, or it's continuous.)

(b) For $0\le x<y\le1$ we have $x^2<y^2$, so $1-x^2>1-y^2\ge0$, and since $\sqrt{\ }$ is increasing (lesson 8), $\sqrt{1-x^2}>\sqrt{1-y^2}$. So $f$ is decreasing, hence integrable. (Or: it's continuous, by Worked example 2.) Its integral is $\frac\pi4$, a quarter of the unit disc, which you'll be able to compute in lesson 19.

(c) $\lfloor x\rfloor$ is increasing, so integrable. In fact it's a step function: $0$ on $(0,1)$, $1$ on $(1,2)$, $2$ on $(2,3)$, so $\int_0^3\lfloor x\rfloor\,dx=0+1+2=3$. Note it is not continuous, so only the monotone theorem applies, not Worked example 2.

Exercise 6 (proof with the ε-criterion)

Let $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ with $f(\frac12)=5$ and $f(x)=0$ for all other $x$. Show directly with the ε-criterion that $f$ is integrable, and that $\int_0^1f\,dx=0$.

Solution

Frame: “Let $\varepsilon>0$. … so $u\le f\le o$ and $\int(o-u)<\varepsilon$.” The gap: an upper step function that equals $5$ only on a very short interval around $\frac12$.

Proof. Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $\eta=\min(\frac\varepsilon{20},\frac14)$. Let $u=0$, and let $o=5$ on $[\frac12-\eta,\frac12+\eta]$ and $o=0$ elsewhere. Both are step functions (partition $0<\frac12-\eta<\frac12+\eta<1$), and $u\le f\le o$. Then $$\int_0^1(o-u)\,dx=5\cdot2\eta=10\eta\le\frac\varepsilon2<\varepsilon.$$By the ε-criterion $f$ is integrable.

Value: $0=\int u\le\int_0^1f\,dx\le\int o=10\eta$, and $\eta$ can be made as small as we like, so $\int_0^1f\,dx=0$. (We took $\eta\le\frac14$ only so that $[\frac12-\eta,\frac12+\eta]\subseteq[0,1]$.) ∎

Moves used: the ε-criterion smaller than every ε

Bonus

Let $g(x)=1$ for rational $x$ and $g(x)=-1$ for irrational $x$, on $[0,1]$. Show that $|g|$ is integrable but $g$ is not. So the converse of “$f$ integrable ⇒ $|f|$ integrable” is false.

Solution

Frame: two claims. “$|g|$ integrable” is one line. “$g$ not integrable” means $\underline I(g)\neq\overline I(g)$, so bound every upper and every lower step function, as for the Dirichlet function in Proof C.

$|g|=1$ is constant, hence a step function, hence integrable. For $g$: every piece of every partition contains a rational and an irrational point, so, exactly as in section 5, every step function $o\ge g$ has values $\ge1$ and every step function $u\le g$ has values $\le-1$ on every piece. So $\overline I(g)=1$ and $\underline I(g)=-1$, which differ. ∎

10. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Archimedes did this 2,250 years ago. Around 250 BC, Archimedes computed the area under a parabola by trapping it between inscribed and circumscribed shapes and showing the gap can be made smaller than any given amount. That is precisely the ε-criterion, without the word “function”. Your Exercise 3 is his result, and it's also where the course began (section 1.1 of the script, the “Quadratur der Parabel” in the first lecture).

The popcorn function (Übung 4.25, p. 192). Let $g(x)=\frac1q$ if $x=\frac pq$ in lowest terms, and $g(x)=0$ if $x$ is irrational, on $[0,1]$. It is discontinuous at every rational number, so its jumps are dense, just like the Dirichlet function's. Yet it is integrable, with integral $0$! The reason: for any $\varepsilon$, only finitely many points have $g(x)\ge\varepsilon$ (those with $q\le\frac1\varepsilon$), and finitely many spikes can be covered by narrow tents, as in Exercise 6. Its graph looks like popcorn bursting from the axis.

Where this leads. Lebesgue proved in 1902 that a bounded function is Riemann integrable exactly when its set of discontinuities is “negligible” (has measure zero). The rationals are negligible, which explains the popcorn function. Lebesgue then built a better integral, which even integrates the Dirichlet function (its integral is $0$). That's the integral of measure theory, later in the Bachelor (the course “Mass und Integral”).
