Why it matters for the exam

Ramps, ropes, springs and friction are the raw material of most exam problems: a crate pulled up a ramp (FS25), a skier on a drag lift (FS17), a spring on an incline (FS20). The work is always the same: a clean free-body diagram, the weight split along and across the slope, and Newton's second law in two components. The multiple-choice part likes the tricky cases: a tilted scale, a lift, a cyclist at top speed.

Recap of lesson 2 (3 min)

  1. State Newton's second law in its general form, and when it becomes $\vec F=m\vec a$.

    Answer

    $\vec F=\dot{\vec p}$, where $\vec F$ is the net force on the body. For constant mass, $\dot{\vec p}=m\vec a$.

  2. A book lies on a table. Name the third-law partner of the book's weight.

    Answer

    The book pulling the earth upwards. (The table's push on the book is not the partner: it acts on the same body.)

  3. What is the impulse of a force, and what does it equal?

    Answer

    $\int_{t_A}^{t_E}\vec F\,dt$, and it equals the change of momentum $\vec p_E-\vec p_A$.

1. The story: hauling the winter supplies

Before the snow closes the pass, the fortress hauls its supplies up the steep ramp to the gate. The haulers know three things by heart. A crate on the ramp is pulled down the slope by only part of its weight, the rest presses it into the planks. A crate stuck on the ramp holds itself with friction, but only up to a limit, and once it starts sliding it slides more easily than it stuck. And the steeper the ramp, the less the planks can hold.

Inside the fortress, sacks go up the well-shaft in a hoist, and the quartermaster weighs them on a scale standing in the hoist. The quartermaster has learnt not to trust it: when the hoist starts upwards the sacks weigh more, when it slows down at the top they weigh less, and once, when the rope broke, the scale showed nothing at all while everything fell.

In physics: the part of the weight along the slope is the downhill force $mg\sin\alpha$, the part pressing into the planks is balanced by the normal force $mg\cos\alpha$, the holding and sliding are static and sliding friction with $\mu_H>\mu_G$, and the scale in the hoist shows the apparent weight $m(g+a)$.

2. Free-body diagrams and normal forces

The script's rules for any setup (section 3.9.1, page 55):

  1. Cut a complicated system into parts that can each be treated as a point mass.
  2. Draw each body as a point.
  3. Draw only the forces that act on that body, each with a direction and a size.

Every interaction between bodies appears as a force. Where a body touches a surface, the surface pushes back perpendicular to itself: the normal force $\vec F_N$. It is as large as needed to stop the body from sinking into the surface, no more.

Worked example 1: two stacked blocks, and what a scale shows

Block $A$ (mass $M_A$) lies on block $B$ (mass $M_B$), which lies on the floor. Everything is at rest. Find the normal force of $B$ on $A$ and of the floor on $B$.

  1. Draw the forces on $A$, then on $B$.

    Answer

    On $A$: its weight $M_Ag$ (down) and the normal force $N_A$ from $B$ (up). On $B$: its weight $M_Bg$ (down), the normal force $N_B$ from the floor (up), and the push $F_{AB}$ of $A$ on $B$ (down). Five forces in all.

  2. Use equilibrium for each block and the third law.

    Answer

    $A$: $N_A=M_Ag$. Third law: $A$ pushes on $B$ with $F_{AB}=N_A=M_Ag$. $B$: $N_B=M_Bg+F_{AB}=(M_A+M_B)g$. The floor carries both.

  3. If $B$ is a bathroom scale, what does it show?

    Answer

    A scale measures the normal force it has to push with, here $N_A=M_Ag$. That's why the script says “weight” is felt through the force that holds you up (section 3.9.1). It matters in a lift (section 7) and on a tilted scale (Exercise 1).

3. Ropes

A rope can only pull, along its own direction. For a massless rope the pull, the rope tension, is the same all along it, and a massless, frictionless pulley only changes its direction. Where ropes meet in a knot, treat the knot as a body (section 3.9.2, page 58).

Worked example 2: a weight hanging from two ropes

A mass $M$ hangs from a knot. Two ropes hold the knot: rope $A$ at $30^\circ$ above the horizontal to the left, rope $B$ at $45^\circ$ to the right. Find the tensions $F_A$ and $F_B$.

  1. Which forces act on the knot? Write the equilibrium in components ($x$ to the right, $y$ up).

    Answer

    Rope $A$, rope $B$, and the third rope carrying the weight, $Mg$ downwards.

    $$x:\ -F_A\cos30^\circ+F_B\cos45^\circ=0,\qquad y:\ F_A\sin30^\circ+F_B\sin45^\circ-Mg=0.$$

  2. Solve.

    Answer

    From $x$: $F_B=F_A\frac{\cos30^\circ}{\cos45^\circ}=F_A\sqrt{\tfrac32}$. Into $y$: $\frac{F_A}2+\frac{\sqrt3}2F_A=Mg$, so

    $$F_A=\frac{2Mg}{1+\sqrt3}\approx0.73\,Mg,\qquad F_B=\sqrt{\tfrac32}\,F_A\approx0.90\,Mg.$$

    The steeper rope carries more. Together they carry more than $Mg$ (part of each pull goes sideways and cancels).

4. Springs

Stretch a spring from its rest length $x_0$ to $x$ and it pulls back with the restoring force (Hooke's law, section 3.9.3, page 59):

$$F=-D\,(x-x_0).$$

The script calls the spring constant $k$, Rupp and the formula sheet call it $D$ (Federhärte). For a mass on a spring, the second law gives $m\ddot x=-D(x-x_0)$. Released at rest from $x_1$, the solution is

$$x(t)=x_0+(x_1-x_0)\cos\omega t,\qquad\omega=\sqrt{\tfrac Dm}:$$

an oscillation. Check by differentiating twice: $\ddot x=-\omega^2(x-x_0)$. ✓ Oscillations get their own lessons (12–13); for now, a spring is one more force in the diagram.

5. Friction

Friction acts along the contact surface, against the (attempted) motion. It comes from the electromagnetic forces between the surface atoms (section 3.9.4, page 61).

Static and sliding friction
  • Static friction (Haftreibung): while the body sticks, friction is as large as needed to hold it, up to a maximum: $F_R\le\mu_H\,F_N$.
  • Sliding friction (Gleitreibung): once it slides, $F_R=\mu_G\,F_N$, against the velocity.
  • Usually $\mu_H>\mu_G$, and to a first approximation both are independent of the contact area and of the speed.

The script writes $\mu_0$ for static and $\mu$ for sliding; Rupp and the formula sheet write $\mu_H$ and $\mu_G$.

The inclined plane. On a slope of angle $\alpha$, split the weight along and across the slope. Along: the downhill force $mg\sin\alpha$. Across: $mg\cos\alpha$, balanced by the normal force, so $F_N=mg\cos\alpha$.

  • It sticks if the friction needed, $mg\sin\alpha$, is at most $\mu_HF_N=\mu_Hmg\cos\alpha$, that is, if $\tan\alpha\le\mu_H$. The mass cancels: a heavier crate is pressed harder into the slope (script, equation (3.92)).
  • If it slides downwards: $ma=mg\sin\alpha-\mu_Gmg\cos\alpha$, so $a=g(\sin\alpha-\mu_G\cos\alpha)$.

Try it: the inclined plane

Tilt the ramp and change the friction coefficients. The arrows are the downhill force (orange), the normal force $F_N$ (blue) and the friction $F_R$ (dark).

Notice the gap: a crate that is already sliding keeps sliding at angles where it would have stuck had it been at rest (whenever $\mu_G<\tan\alpha\le\mu_H$). Haulers know it: once it slips, it's hard to stop.

Worked example 3: pulling a crate up a ramp

A crate of mass $m$ rests on a ramp of angle $\alpha$ with $\tan\alpha<\mu_H$. A rope parallel to the ramp pulls it upwards with force $F$. How large must $F$ be to start it moving, and to keep it moving at constant speed?

  1. In which direction does friction act, and what is its largest value?

    Answer

    Friction opposes the attempted motion, so it points down the slope. At most $\mu_HF_N=\mu_Hmg\cos\alpha$ while it sticks.

  2. The force to start it moving?

    Answer

    Along the slope, $F$ must beat the downhill force and the largest static friction: $F_{\min}=mg\sin\alpha+\mu_Hmg\cos\alpha$.

  3. The force for constant speed?

    Answer

    Constant speed means net force zero (first law), now with sliding friction: $F=mg\sin\alpha+\mu_Gmg\cos\alpha$. Less than $F_{\min}$, since $\mu_G<\mu_H$.

6. Drag

A body moving through a fluid (a liquid or a gas) feels a force against its velocity that grows with the speed (section 3.9.5, page 64):

  • at small speeds, proportional to $v$: $F_R=\kappa v$, for a sphere the Stokes law $F_R=6\pi\eta r\,v$ ($\eta$ the viscosity);
  • at large speeds (a ball, a car, a skydiver), proportional to $v^2$: $F_R=\frac12c_W\rho A\,v^2$, with the drag coefficient $c_W$, the fluid's density $\rho$ and the cross-section $A$.

A falling body speeds up until the drag equals its weight. Then the net force is zero and it falls at a constant terminal velocity. With $v^2$-drag, $mg=\frac12c_W\rho Av_\infty^2$ gives $v_\infty=\sqrt{\frac{2mg}{c_W\rho A}}$.

Try it: falling with air

A body falls from rest with drag $\frac12c_W\rho Av^2$ ($\rho=1.25\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$). Change its mass and its effective area $c_WA$.

The dashed line is free fall without air. A skydiver spread out ($c_WA\approx0.6\,\mathrm{m^2}$) levels off near $45\,\mathrm{m/s}$; with an open parachute ($c_WA\approx15$–$20\,\mathrm{m^2}$) the terminal speed drops to a few metres per second.

Worked example 4: braking on a wet road

A car on a flat road feels only a friction force proportional to its speed, $F_R=-\kappa\dot x$ (section 3.9.6). It starts at $x=0$ with speed $v_0$. Find $v(t)$, $x(t)$ and the total braking distance.

  1. Write the equation of motion for $v=\dot x$.

    Answer

    $m\dot v=-\kappa v$, so $\dot v=-\alpha v$ with $\alpha=\frac\kappa m$.

  2. Solve it by separating the variables (Analysis lesson 22).

    Answer

    $\frac{dv}v=-\alpha\,dt$; integrate from $v_0$ to $v$ and from $0$ to $t$: $\ln\frac v{v_0}=-\alpha t$, so $v(t)=v_0e^{-\alpha t}$.

  3. Integrate again. How far does the car get in total?

    Answer

    $x(t)=\int_0^tv_0e^{-\alpha\tilde t}\,d\tilde t=\frac{v_0}\alpha\left(1-e^{-\alpha t}\right)$. As $t\to\infty$, $x\to\frac{v_0}\alpha=\frac{mv_0}\kappa$: the car never quite stops in this model, but it never passes $\frac{mv_0}\kappa$.

7. Force fields, gravity and apparent weight

Instead of tracking every body that pulls on a test particle, we describe their combined effect by a force field: the force on the test particle at each point, $\vec F(\vec r,t)=\gamma\,\vec f(\vec r,t)$ (section 3.10.1, page 66). The coupling constant $\gamma$ belongs to the test particle (its charge, its mass), the field $\vec f$ to everything else. Two examples:

  • Electric (Coulomb) force: $\vec F=q\vec E$ with $\vec E=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac Q{r^2}\hat r$.
  • Gravity (section 3.10.3): $\vec F=-G\frac{mM}{r^2}\hat r=m\,\vec g(\vec r)$ with $\vec g=-G\frac M{r^2}\hat r$.

In the second law, the gravitational mass in $\vec F$ and the inertial mass in $m\vec a$ cancel (they are equal, lesson 2): $\vec a=\vec g$, the same for every body. At the earth's surface $g=G\frac M{R_E^2}\approx9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, and at a small height $h$, $g(h)\approx g\left(1-\frac{2h}{R_E}\right)$ (a first-order Taylor expansion).

Apparent weight (section 3.10.3.2, page 71). You feel your weight only through the force that holds you up. In a lift accelerating with $a$ (upwards positive), you move with the lift, so

$$F_N-mg=ma\iff F_N=m(g+a).$$

Starting upwards ($a>0$) you feel heavier, at constant speed normal, braking on the way up ($a<0$) lighter, and in free fall ($a=-g$) the scale shows zero: weightlessness. Gravity hasn't vanished; nothing has to hold you up any more.

Try it: the scale in the lift

A person of $60\,\mathrm{kg}$ stands on a scale in a lift. Choose the lift's acceleration (upwards positive).

Why it's cool. The rule $\tan\alpha\le\mu_H$ is a measuring instrument: tilt a board until a coin starts to slide, and the angle tells you the coefficient of static friction, whatever the coin weighs. And the lift shows why astronauts in orbit float: the station and everyone in it are in free fall around the earth together.

8. Exercises

Do them on paper. Numbers only at the end, $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, no calculator.

Exercise 1 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.1(c), 1 point, original)

You set up a scale at $20^\circ$ to the horizontal and put an object of mass $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ on it. The object doesn't slide. What does the scale show? (A) Less than 1 kg. (B) Exactly 1 kg. (C) More than 1 kg. (D) It depends on the coefficient of static friction.

Solution

A. A scale measures the force perpendicular to its plate, the normal force $F_N=mg\cos20^\circ<mg$. The component $mg\sin20^\circ$ along the plate is held by friction and doesn't press on the scale. It doesn't depend on $\mu_H$: friction only decides whether it sticks, which is given.

Exercise 2 (FS17, problem 1(a) “Schlepplift”, 1.5 points, original)

A skier stands on a slope of angle $\alpha$, waiting for the drag lift. The coefficient of friction (static and sliding) between skis and snow is $\mu$. Find the steepest slope $\alpha_{\text{krit}}$ on which the skier doesn't slide down.

Solution

Forces: weight, normal force $F_N=mg\cos\alpha$, static friction up the slope. Sticking needs $mg\sin\alpha\le\mu\,mg\cos\alpha$, so $\tan\alpha\le\mu$ and $\alpha_{\text{krit}}=\arctan\mu$.

Exercise 3 (FS25, problem 3 “Kiste”, parts (b), (c), (e), (f), 6 points, original)

A crate of mass $m$ is pulled up a ramp of angle $\alpha$ (or held on it) by a rope parallel to the ramp with force $F_S$. The coefficients of static and sliding friction are $\mu_h>\mu_g$. (Parts (a) and (d) ask when the crate tips over: that is statics, lesson 10.)

(b) Rope slack: how steep may the ramp be at most so that the crate doesn't start to slide? [1 point]

(c) Now the rope pulls. How large must $F_S$ be at least to set the crate moving? [2 points]

(e) How large must $F_S$ be to keep it moving in a straight line at constant speed? [1 point]

(f) The crate moves up with speed $v_0$ when the rope breaks. For how long does it still slide up the ramp before it stops? [1 point]

Solution

Frame: axis along the ramp, upwards positive; $F_N=mg\cos\alpha$ throughout.

(b) As in the inclined plane: $\tan\alpha\le\mu_h$, so $\alpha_{\max}=\arctan\mu_h$.

(c) As in Worked example 3: $F_S\ge mg(\sin\alpha+\mu_h\cos\alpha)$.

(e) Sliding friction, net force zero: $F_S=mg(\sin\alpha+\mu_g\cos\alpha)$.

(f) After the rope breaks, the crate still moves up, so sliding friction points down, like the downhill force: $ma=-mg\sin\alpha-\mu_gmg\cos\alpha$. Constant deceleration, so $v(t)=v_0-g(\sin\alpha+\mu_g\cos\alpha)\,t$, which is zero at

$$t=\frac{v_0}{g(\sin\alpha+\mu_g\cos\alpha)}.$$

(Then it stops for good, since the crate sticks on this ramp by (b).)

Exercise 4 (HS12, problem 1 “Fallschirmspringer”, 8 points, original)

A skydiver of mass $80\,\mathrm{kg}$ forgets his parachute and jumps at $2000\,\mathrm m$. His fall is slowed by air drag; air has density $1.25\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and his effective cross-section is $c_WA=0.6\,\mathrm{m^2}$.

(a) Set up his equation of motion and solve it for the limiting case of constant speed. How large is that speed in km/h? [3 points]

(b) He's lucky and lands in soft, deep powder snow, making a hole $1.2\,\mathrm m$ deep. How large is the impulse on his body during the impact? [2 points]

(c) Assume the snow brakes him uniformly. Does he survive? A human survives a pressure of $3.5\,\mathrm{atm}$. Explain. [3 points]

Solution

(a) Axis downwards: $m\dot v=mg-\frac12c_W\rho Av^2$. Constant speed means $\dot v=0$:

$$v_\infty=\sqrt{\frac{2mg}{c_W\rho A}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\cdot80\cdot10}{1.25\cdot0.6}}\,\mathrm{m/s}=\sqrt{\frac{1600}{0.75}}\,\mathrm{m/s}\approx46\,\mathrm{m/s}\approx170\,\mathrm{km/h}.$$

(He reaches it long before the ground: 2000 m is plenty.)

(b) From $46\,\mathrm{m/s}$ to rest: the impulse equals the change of momentum, $|\Delta p|=mv_\infty\approx80\cdot46\,\mathrm{N\,s}\approx3.7\cdot10^3\,\mathrm{N\,s}$, upwards.

(c) Uniform braking over $s=1.2\,\mathrm m$: $v_\infty^2=2as$, so $a=\frac{v_\infty^2}{2s}=\frac{2133}{2.4}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\approx890\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. The snow must also carry his weight: $F=m(a+g)\approx80\cdot900\,\mathrm N=7.2\cdot10^4\,\mathrm N$. Spread over his cross-section of about $0.6\,\mathrm{m^2}$ (he lands flat), the pressure is $\frac{7.2\cdot10^4}{0.6}\,\mathrm{Pa}\approx1.2\cdot10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\approx1.2\,\mathrm{atm}<3.5\,\mathrm{atm}$: he survives (if he lands flat; on his feet the area would be far smaller). Any clearly argued area assumption earns the points.

Exercise 5 (the lift, a quick one)

A $60\,\mathrm{kg}$ person stands on a scale in a lift. What does the scale show (in newtons, and in “kg” as the scale displays it) when the lift (a) starts upwards with $2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, (b) moves at constant speed, (c) brakes on the way down with $2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, (d) falls freely?

Solution

$F_N=m(g+a)$ with $a$ upwards positive. (a) $a=2$: $720\,\mathrm N$, “72 kg”. (b) $a=0$: $600\,\mathrm N$, “60 kg”. (c) Braking on the way down means the acceleration points up: $a=+2$, again $720\,\mathrm N$. (d) $a=-10$: $0$.

Scoring yourself: Exercise 1 (1), Exercise 2 (1.5), Exercise 3 (6) and Exercise 4 (8) make 16.5 points. With 11 you're done. Exercise 5 is a warm-up.

9. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

Why friction ignores the contact area. Leonardo da Vinci found it around 1500, by dragging blocks lying on different faces; Guillaume Amontons rediscovered it in 1699. It sounds wrong: surely a bigger surface rubs more? The answer came only in the 20th century. Seen under a microscope, two “flat” surfaces touch only at the tips of tiny bumps, and the true contact area is often less than a thousandth of the visible one. Press harder and the bumps flatten, so the true contact area grows in proportion to the normal force, whatever the size of the block. Friction follows the true area, so it follows $F_N$: that is $F_R=\mu F_N$.

The coin and the angle. The script's inclined plane hides a measuring trick that is centuries old: tilt the plane until the block just starts to slide, and $\mu_H=\tan\alpha$, without knowing any mass or force.
