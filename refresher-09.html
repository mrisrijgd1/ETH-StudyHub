1. Warm-up (5 min)

Three quick questions. Answer each in your head, then open it.

  1. Differentiate $x\mapsto\int_0^{x^2}e^{t^2}\,dt$.

    Answer

    With $G(y)=\int_0^ye^{t^2}dt$ we have $G'(y)=e^{y^2}$ (fundamental theorem), so by the chain rule the derivative of $G(x^2)$ is $e^{(x^2)^2}\cdot2x=2xe^{x^4}$.

  2. Compute $\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx$.

    Answer

    $\bigl[-\cos x\bigr]_0^\pi=-\cos\pi+\cos0=1+1=2$.

  3. Which method for $\int x\cos x\,dx$, and what is it?

    Answer

    By parts with $u=x$ (it becomes $1$) and $v'=\cos x$: $x\sin x-\int\sin x\,dx=x\sin x+\cos x+C$. Check: $(x\sin x+\cos x)'=\sin x+x\cos x-\sin x=x\cos x$ ✓.

2. Recap card: lesson 19 (15 min)

Read this once, slowly. Everything in bold must come out exactly in the exam.

Lesson 19: the fundamental theorem and how to integrate

  • Fundamental theorem: if $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$ and continuous at $x_0$, then $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$ is differentiable at $x_0$ with $F'(x_0)=f(x_0)$.
  • Computing integrals: for $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$ and any antiderivative $F$: $\int_a^bf=F(b)-F(a)$. Two antiderivatives on an interval differ by a constant.
  • Power series can be differentiated and integrated term by term inside the radius of convergence; the radius stays the same.
  • By parts: $\int_a^buv'=\bigl[uv\bigr]_a^b-\int_a^bu'v$ for $u,v$ continuously differentiable. $u$ = the factor that gets simpler when differentiated ($x^n$, $\log x$, $\arctan x$); a lone $\log x$ or $\arctan x$: take $v'=1$.
  • Substitution: $\int_a^bg(\varphi(x))\varphi'(x)\,dx=\int_{\varphi(a)}^{\varphi(b)}g(u)\,du$. In practice: $u=\varphi(x)$, $du=\varphi'(x)\,dx$, new limits. Special case: $\int\frac{\varphi'}{\varphi}=\log|\varphi|+C$.
  • Partial fractions for $\frac{p}{q}$: divide first if $\deg p\ge\deg q$; factor $q$; one term $\frac A{x-a}$ per simple zero, $\frac{A_1}{x-a}+\dots+\frac{A_k}{(x-a)^k}$ per $k$-fold zero, $\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+bx+c}$ per quadratic factor; plug in the zeros to find the constants.
  • Roots: $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$: $x=a\sin\theta$. $\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$: $x=a\tan\theta$. $\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$: $x=a\cosh u$. Rational in $\sin,\cos$: $u=\tan\frac x2$. All on the formula sheet.
  • The decision list, in this order: table (after simplifying) → inner function with its derivative as a factor (substitution) → product of two kinds (by parts) → rational (partial fractions) → square roots (trig/hyperbolic) → a root or $e^x$ to get rid of (substitute it).
  • Always check by differentiating. Twenty seconds, and it catches almost every sign error.

4. Write it on paper: three exam proofs (25 min)

For each proof: read the claim and the frame, close your eyes for ten seconds, then write the whole proof on paper without looking at the lesson. Only then open the fold and mark yourself with the grader's list. Proof 1 is the one that comes as a 5-point question; Proofs 2 and 3 are short, and they are why the methods in section 5 are allowed.

Proof 1: the fundamental theorem (Theorem 9.2, 2018 winter exam)

Claim. Let $a<b$ and $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ Riemann integrable. If $f$ is continuous at $x_0\in[a,b]$, then $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$ is differentiable at $x_0$ with $F'(x_0)=f(x_0)$. (Lesson 19, Proof A, script p. 417–418. The 2018 winter exam, question 12, asked exactly this for 5 points: it is Exercise 10 of the lesson.)

What the definitions force

First line: “Let $\varepsilon>0$”, then the $\delta$ from continuity at $x_0$. Last line: $\left|\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}-f(x_0)\right|\le\varepsilon$ for $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$. The gap: write the difference quotient as one integral, and write $f(x_0)$ as an integral over the same strip, so they can be subtracted. Two cases, $x>x_0$ and $x<x_0$.

The proof and the grader's list

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $f$ is continuous at $x_0$, there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(t)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ for all $t\in[a,b]$ with $|t-x_0|<\delta$.

Let $x\in[a,b]$ with $0<x-x_0<\delta$. By interval additivity, $F(x)-F(x_0)=\int_{x_0}^xf(t)\,dt$, and $f(x_0)=\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^xf(x_0)\,dt$. Every $t\in[x_0,x]$ has $|t-x_0|<\delta$, so $|f(t)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ there. Subtracting under one integral, $$\begin{aligned}&\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}-f(x_0)\\&\quad=\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^x\bigl(f(t)-f(x_0)\bigr)dt,\end{aligned}$$ so by the triangle inequality for integrals $$\begin{aligned}&\left|\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}-f(x_0)\right|\\&\quad\le\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^x|f(t)-f(x_0)|\,dt\\&\quad\le\frac{\varepsilon\,(x-x_0)}{x-x_0}=\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$

For $0<x_0-x<\delta$: $\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}=\frac1{x_0-x}\int_x^{x_0}f(t)\,dt$, and the same estimate on $[x,x_0]$ gives $\le\varepsilon$. Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $\frac{F(x)-F(x_0)}{x-x_0}\to f(x_0)$ as $x\to x_0$, so $F'(x_0)=f(x_0)$. (At an endpoint only one case occurs.) ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ from continuity at $x_0$, written first;
  • interval additivity: $F(x)-F(x_0)=\int_{x_0}^xf$;
  • the average trick: $f(x_0)$ written as $\frac1{x-x_0}\int_{x_0}^xf(x_0)\,dt$, so both sit under one integral;
  • the estimate with the triangle inequality, ending in $\le\varepsilon$;
  • the case $x<x_0$, with the one-line reason why it is the same.

In short: “the quotient is an average; continuity makes every value close; so the average is close”.

Proof 2: $\int_a^bf=F(b)-F(a)$ (Korollar 9.3–9.5)

Claim. Let $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ be continuous and let $F$ be any antiderivative of $f$. Then $\int_a^bf(t)\,dt=F(b)-F(a)$. (Lesson 19, Proof B, script p. 418–419. This is the line that makes every computation in section 5 legal.)

What the definitions force

You know one antiderivative already: $F_0(x)=\int_a^xf$, by Proof 1. The gap: show $F$ and $F_0$ differ by a constant, name it $C$, then plug in $x=a$ and $x=b$.

The proof and the grader's list

$f$ is continuous, hence integrable, and continuous at every point; by Proof 1, $F_0(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$ satisfies $F_0'=f$ on $[a,b]$. Then $(F-F_0)'=f-f=0$ on the interval $[a,b]$, so $F-F_0=C$ is constant (lesson 17: $f'=0$ on an interval gives constant). At $x=a$: $F(a)=F_0(a)+C=0+C$, so $C=F(a)$. At $x=b$: $\int_a^bf=F_0(b)=F(b)-C=F(b)-F(a)$. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • “continuous ⇒ integrable” and Proof 1 for $F_0'=f$;
  • $(F-F_0)'=0$ on an interval, so constant, with the reason named;
  • the constant found at $x=a$, and the value read off at $x=b$.

In short: “one antiderivative from Proof 1; any other differs by a constant; plug in $a$, then $b$”.

Proof 3: integration by parts and substitution (Sections 9.2.1, 9.2.2)

Claim. (a) For $u,v$ continuously differentiable on $[a,b]$: $\int_a^buv'=\bigl[uv\bigr]_a^b-\int_a^bu'v$. (b) For $\varphi:[a,b]\to I$ continuously differentiable and $g$ continuous on the interval $I$: $\int_a^bg(\varphi(x))\varphi'(x)\,dx=\int_{\varphi(a)}^{\varphi(b)}g(u)\,du$. (Lesson 19, Proofs D and E, script p. 425–426 and 434–435.)

What the definitions force

Both are equations between numbers, so Proof 2 turns each side into “antiderivative at the top minus at the bottom”. The gap: (a) the product rule, read backwards; (b) the chain rule, read backwards.

The proof and the grader's list

(a) $u'v$ is continuous, so it has an antiderivative $W$ (Proof 2). By the product rule, $(uv-W)'=u'v+uv'-u'v=uv'$, so $uv-W$ is an antiderivative of $uv'$. By Proof 2, $$\begin{aligned}\int_a^buv'&=\bigl[uv-W\bigr]_a^b\\&=\bigl[uv\bigr]_a^b-\int_a^bu'v.\end{aligned}$$

(b) $g$ is continuous on $I$, so it has an antiderivative $G$. By the chain rule, $(G\circ\varphi)'=(g\circ\varphi)\cdot\varphi'$, which is continuous. By Proof 2 twice, $$\begin{aligned}&\int_a^bg(\varphi(x))\varphi'(x)\,dx\\&\quad=G(\varphi(b))-G(\varphi(a))\\&\quad=\int_{\varphi(a)}^{\varphi(b)}g(u)\,du.\end{aligned}$$∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • the existence of the antiderivative $W$ (or $G$), with “continuous” as the reason;
  • the product rule (or chain rule) written out and simplified;
  • Proof 2 used to go from antiderivatives to the definite integrals.

In short: “every integration rule is a differentiation rule read backwards, plus Proof 2”.

5. Drill: ten integrals (20 min)

For each integral, first name the method from the decision list (ten seconds, out loud), then compute it on paper and check by differentiating. Open the fold only when your answer is done. In the exam, the method choice is most of the work.

  1. $\int xe^{2x}\,dx$

    Method and answer

    By parts, $u=x$, $v'=e^{2x}$, $v=\frac12e^{2x}$: $\frac x2e^{2x}-\int\frac12e^{2x}dx=\left(\frac x2-\frac14\right)e^{2x}+C$.

  2. $\int\frac{x}{x^2+1}\,dx$

    Method and answer

    Substitution: the derivative of $x^2+1$ is $2x$, which is (up to $\frac12$) a factor. $\frac12\int\frac{2x}{x^2+1}dx=\frac12\log(x^2+1)+C$. No absolute value needed: $x^2+1>0$.

  3. $\int\log x\,dx$ on $(0,\infty)$

    Method and answer

    By parts with $v'=1$, $u=\log x$: $x\log x-\int x\cdot\frac1x\,dx=x\log x-x+C$.

  4. $\int\sin x\cos^2x\,dx$

    Method and answer

    Substitution $u=\cos x$, $du=-\sin x\,dx$: $-\int u^2\,du=-\frac{\cos^3x}3+C$.

  5. $\int\frac{dx}{x^2-1}$ on $(1,\infty)$

    Method and answer

    Partial fractions: $x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)$ and $\frac1{x^2-1}=\frac{1/2}{x-1}-\frac{1/2}{x+1}$ (plug in $x=1$ and $x=-1$ after multiplying by $x^2-1$). So $\frac12\log|x-1|-\frac12\log|x+1|+C=\frac12\log\frac{x-1}{x+1}+C$.

  6. $\int\frac{2x+3}{x^2+3x+2}\,dx$ on $(0,\infty)$

    Method and answer

    A trap: it looks like partial fractions, but the numerator is exactly the derivative of the denominator. So it's $\frac{\varphi'}\varphi$: $\log(x^2+3x+2)+C$. (Partial fractions also work, $\frac1{x+1}+\frac1{x+2}$, and give the same answer, just slower. Step 2 of the list comes before step 4 for a reason.)

  7. $\int\frac{dx}{x^2+2x+5}$

    Method and answer

    The denominator has no real zeros, so complete the square: $x^2+2x+5=(x+1)^2+2^2$. With the table, $\int\frac{dx}{(x+1)^2+4}=\frac12\arctan\frac{x+1}2+C$.

  8. $\int e^{\sqrt x}\,dx$ on $(0,\infty)$

    Method and answer

    A root to get rid of: $u=\sqrt x$, $x=u^2$, $dx=2u\,du$. Then by parts: $\int2ue^u\,du=2ue^u-2e^u$. Back: $2(\sqrt x-1)e^{\sqrt x}+C$.

  9. $\int_0^1\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx$

    Method and answer

    $x=\sin\theta$, $dx=\cos\theta\,d\theta$, limits $\theta=0$ to $\frac\pi2$, and $\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta}=\cos\theta$ there: $$\begin{aligned}&\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta\\&\quad=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{1+\cos2\theta}2\,d\theta\\&\quad=\frac\pi4.\end{aligned}$$ Sanity check: a quarter of the unit disc has area $\frac\pi4$ ✓.

  10. $\int_0^1\arctan x\,dx$

    Method and answer

    By parts with $v'=1$, $u=\arctan x$, then substitution for the rest: $$\begin{aligned}&\bigl[x\arctan x\bigr]_0^1-\int_0^1\frac{x}{1+x^2}dx\\&\quad=\frac\pi4-\frac12\log2.\end{aligned}$$

Got eight or more right, with the method named first? Then integrals are safe points for you. Fewer? Redo the ones you missed tomorrow, without looking.

6. Spot the mistake (5 min)

A student computes an integral and hands in this. The answer is wrong (a positive function can't have a negative integral). Where exactly does it break?

“Solution.” $$\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^1\frac{dx}{x^2}&=\left[-\frac1x\right]_{-1}^1\\&=-1-1=-2.\end{aligned}$$

The mistake

Proof 2 needs $f$ continuous on the whole interval $[a,b]$. Here $\frac1{x^2}$ is not even defined at $0\in[-1,1]$, and it is unbounded near $0$, so it isn't Riemann integrable on $[-1,1]$ at all. Also, $-\frac1x$ is an antiderivative only on $(-\infty,0)$ and on $(0,\infty)$ separately, not on an interval containing $0$.

The correct reading: this is an improper integral (lesson 20, unit 10), and $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^2}=\infty$, so it diverges.

Exam lesson: before writing $\bigl[F\bigr]_a^b$, check that the integrand is continuous on all of $[a,b]$. One glance at the zeros of the denominator is enough.
