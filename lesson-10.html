Why it matters for the exam Convergence of sequences is used in almost every later exam question: series, power series, uniform convergence and limits of functions are all built on it. The proofs here (uniqueness of limits, convergent ⇒ bounded, sum rule, sequences and continuity) are the standard moves inside bigger exam proofs. Directly tested: open and closed sets in Figalli HS23 MC1 and FS26 MC1 (your Exercise 2), limits of sequences in nearly every “compute” part, and the sequence test for discontinuity in many multiple-choice blocks.

Recap of lesson 9 (5 min)

  • Step function: constant on the open pieces of a partition. Its integral is $\sum c_k\Delta x_k$.
  • Lower and upper integral: $\underline I(f)=\sup$ of all $\int u$ with step functions $u\le f$, $\overline I(f)=\inf$ of all $\int o$ with step functions $o\ge f$. Always $\underline I(f)\le\overline I(f)$. Riemann integrable means they are equal.
  • The ε-criterion: $f$ is integrable ⇔ for every $\varepsilon>0$ there are step functions $u\le f\le o$ with $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$.
  • Monotone ⇒ integrable (7 points): oBdA increasing, equal pieces, $u$ at left ends and $o$ at right ends, telescope to $\frac{(b-a)(f(b)-f(a))}n$, Archimedes, ε-criterion.
  • Continuous ⇒ integrable: the same frame, with Heine making $M_k-m_k<\frac\varepsilon{b-a}$ on every piece.
  • Not integrable: the Dirichlet function, because every interval contains rationals and irrationals.

  1. Why does every lower sum lie below every upper sum?

    Answer

    If $u\le f\le o$, then $u\le o$, and the integral of step functions is monotone, so $\int u\le\int o$.

  2. Is a bounded function with finitely many values always integrable?

    Answer

    No. The Dirichlet function takes only the values $0$ and $1$ and is not integrable.

  3. In the monotone proof, where does the Archimedean principle come in?

    Answer

    To choose $n>\frac{(b-a)(f(b)-f(a))}{\varepsilon}$, so that $\int(o-u)=\frac{(b-a)(f(b)-f(a))}{n}<\varepsilon$.

1. The story: the measure of distance

In the kingdom, three guilds argue about how far apart two towns are. The Ravens fly in a straight line. The Couriers of the grid city can only walk along streets running north–south or east–west, so they add up the two distances. And the Royal Railway runs every line through the capital: unless two towns lie on the same line out of the capital, the journey is “to the capital, then out again”.

The queen's mathematician says all three are right. A way of measuring distance is acceptable if it obeys three laws. A town is at distance $0$ only from itself. The way there is as long as the way back. A detour is never shorter than going directly. Any rule that obeys the three laws is a metric, and the land together with it is a metric space. There is even a lazy wizard's metric: every other town is at distance $1$, and your own town is at distance $0$.

Once you can measure distance, you can talk about aiming. An archer shoots arrow after arrow at a target. We say the arrows converge to the target if, however small a ring you paint around the target, from some arrow onwards every arrow lands inside the ring. Early wild shots don't matter. Only “eventually, all of them” counts.

And a continuous road map is one that respects aiming: if arrows home in on a town, then their images on the map home in on the image of the town. This is the second way to describe continuity, and often the easier one to use.

In maths: towns are points, the guilds are the Euclidean, Manhattan and French railway metrics, the wizard's rule is the discrete metric, rings are open balls, and the arrows are a sequence.

2. Norms: length in a vector space

Definition 5.1 (p. 225). A norm on a real (or complex) vector space $V$ is a map $\|\cdot\|:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}$ such that for all $v,w\in V$ and scalars $\alpha$:

  • (definite) $\|v\|=0\iff v=0$,
  • (homogeneous) $\|\alpha v\|=|\alpha|\,\|v\|$,
  • (triangle inequality) $\|v+w\|\le\|v\|+\|w\|$.

The standard examples on $\mathbb{R}^d$ (Beispiel 5.2 and Section 5.1.1). For $v=(v_1,\dots,v_d)$:

$$\begin{aligned}&\|v\|_\infty=\max_{j}|v_j|\\&\|v\|_1=\sum_{j=1}^d|v_j|\\&\|v\|_2=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^d|v_j|^2}\end{aligned}.$$

For example, $v=(3,-4)$ has $\|v\|_\infty=4$, $\|v\|_1=7$ and $\|v\|_2=5$. The Euclidean norm $\|\cdot\|_2$ is the ordinary length. On $\mathbb{R}$ (with $d=1$) all three are just $|v|$.

Cauchy–Schwarz (Proposition 5.3, p. 227). For the inner product $\langle v,w\rangle=\sum_k v_kw_k$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$: $|\langle v,w\rangle|\le\|v\|_2\,\|w\|_2$, with equality exactly when $v,w$ are linearly dependent. It's what proves the triangle inequality for $\|\cdot\|_2$ (Korollar 5.5). You'll prove it in Linear Algebra, so here we only use it.

A norm on functions (Beispiel 5.7, p. 229). On the space of continuous functions on $[a,b]$, $\|f\|_\infty=\max_{x\in[a,b]}|f(x)|$ is a norm. The maximum exists by the extreme value theorem. This norm will be the key to uniform convergence (lesson 15).

3. Metrics: distance between anything

Definition 5.10 (p. 230). A metric on a set $X$ is a map $d:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}$ such that for all $x_1,x_2,x_3\in X$:

  • (definite) $d(x_1,x_2)=0\iff x_1=x_2$,
  • (symmetric) $d(x_1,x_2)=d(x_2,x_1)$,
  • (triangle inequality) $d(x_1,x_3)\le d(x_1,x_2)+d(x_2,x_3)$.

$(X,d)$ is then a metric space.

  • Every norm gives a metric (Lemma 5.11, p. 231): $d(v,w)=\|v-w\|$. Each axiom of the metric comes from the matching axiom of the norm; for symmetry use homogeneity with $\alpha=-1$. On $\mathbb{R}$ this is the familiar $d(x,y)=|x-y|$.
  • The discrete metric (Beispiel 5.12 (i)): $d(x,y)=1$ if $x\neq y$ and $0$ if $x=y$, on any set. It satisfies the triangle inequality because if $d(x_1,x_3)=1$, then $x_2$ differs from at least one of $x_1,x_3$, so the right side is at least $1$.
  • Manhattan and the French railway (Beispiel 5.12 (ii), (iii)): $d_1((x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2))=|x_1-x_2|+|y_1-y_2|$, and on $\mathbb{C}$: $d(z_1,z_2)=|z_1-z_2|$ if $z_1,z_2$ lie on one line through $0$, and $|z_1|+|z_2|$ otherwise.
  • Subsets: any subset $Y\subseteq X$ is a metric space with the same $d$ (the induced metric). So $[0,1]$, $\mathbb{Q}$ and $(0,1)$ are all metric spaces.

Reverse triangle inequality (Wichtige Übung 5.14, p. 233). $|d(x_1,y)-d(x_2,y)|\le d(x_1,x_2)$. Why: the triangle inequality gives $d(x_1,y)\le d(x_1,x_2)+d(x_2,y)$, so $d(x_1,y)-d(x_2,y)\le d(x_1,x_2)$, and swapping $x_1,x_2$ gives the same for the negative. On $\mathbb{R}$ with $y=0$: $\big||a|-|b|\big|\le|a-b|$.

Open balls and open sets

Definition 5.16 (p. 234). The open ball of radius $r>0$ around $x_0$ is $B_r(x_0)=\{x\in X: d(x,x_0)<r\}$. A set $O\subseteq X$ is open if for every $x_0\in O$ there is $r>0$ with $B_r(x_0)\subseteq O$. A neighbourhood of $x_0$ (Definition 5.24, p. 238) is any set containing some $B_\varepsilon(x_0)$.

In $\mathbb{R}$, $B_r(x_0)=(x_0-r,x_0+r)$, so this is exactly lesson 5's definition of open sets. In $\mathbb{R}^2$ the ball depends on the metric: a round disc for $d_2$, a square for $d_\infty$, a diamond for $d_1$.

Proof A: two open balls intersect in an open set (Lemma 5.17, p. 234)

Claim. In any metric space, $B_{r_1}(x_1)\cap B_{r_2}(x_2)$ is open.

The idea. Stand at a point $x$ in both balls. You are $d(x,x_1)$ away from the first centre, so you still have room $r_1-d(x,x_1)$ before the first ball's edge. Likewise for the second. A small ball around $x$ with the smaller of the two rooms stays inside both. The triangle inequality makes “room” precise.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • “Open” forces the first line “Let $x\in B_{r_1}(x_1)\cap B_{r_2}(x_2)$.” and the last line “…so $B_r(x)\subseteq B_{r_1}(x_1)\cap B_{r_2}(x_2)$.”
  • Showing a set inclusion forces “Let $y\in B_r(x)$. … so $y\in B_{r_1}(x_1)$ and $y\in B_{r_2}(x_2)$.”
  • The gap to fill: choose $r$.
The storyWhat you write

Stand somewhere inside both rings.

Let $x\in B_{r_1}(x_1)\cap B_{r_2}(x_2)$, so $d(x,x_1)<r_1$ and $d(x,x_2)<r_2$.

Measure how much room you have before each edge, and keep the smaller room.

Let $r=\min\{r_1-d(x,x_1),\ r_2-d(x,x_2)\}$. Both numbers are positive, so $r>0$.

Any point within that room of you is still inside the first ring: walking to it and then to the centre is at most the room plus your distance.

Let $y\in B_r(x)$. By the triangle inequality, $$d(y,x_1)\le d(y,x)+d(x,x_1)<r+d(x,x_1)\le r_1.$$

The same for the second ring.

Likewise $d(y,x_2)<r_2$. So $y\in B_{r_1}(x_1)\cap B_{r_2}(x_2)$, hence $B_r(x)\subseteq B_{r_1}(x_1)\cap B_{r_2}(x_2)$. As $x$ was arbitrary, the intersection is open. ∎

Moves used: minimum of two δs insert a middle point

The proof in one piece

Let $x\in B_{r_1}(x_1)\cap B_{r_2}(x_2)$ and $$r=\min\{r_1-d(x,x_1),r_2-d(x,x_2)\}>0.$$For $y\in B_r(x)$, the triangle inequality gives $$\begin{aligned}d(y,x_1)&\le d(y,x)+d(x,x_1)\\&<r+d(x,x_1)\\&\le r_1\end{aligned}$$, and likewise $d(y,x_2)<r_2$. So $B_r(x)\subseteq B_{r_1}(x_1)\cap B_{r_2}(x_2)$, and the intersection is open. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why is $r>0$? One sentence.

    Answer

    “Since $d(x,x_1)<r_1$ and $d(x,x_2)<r_2$, both $r_1-d(x,x_1)$ and $r_2-d(x,x_2)$ are positive, so their minimum is positive.”

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the chain of inequalities that shows $d(y,x_1)<r_1$, with the reason for each step.

    Answer

    $d(y,x_1)\le d(y,x)+d(x,x_1)$ (triangle inequality) $<r+d(x,x_1)$ (as $y\in B_r(x)$) $\le r_1$ (as $r\le r_1-d(x,x_1)$).

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone, then compare.

4. Sequences and convergence

Definition 5.20 (p. 236). A sequence in a set $X$ is a map $\mathbb{N}\to X$, $n\mapsto a_n$. We write $(a_n)_n$ or $(a_1,a_2,\dots)$.

Definition 5.21 (p. 236). A sequence $(a_n)_n$ in a metric space $(X,d)$ converges to $A\in X$ if

$$\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\mathbb{N}\ \forall n\ge N:\ d(a_n,A)<\varepsilon.$$

Then $A$ is a limit of the sequence, and we write $\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=A$ or $a_n\to A$. A sequence with a limit is convergent, otherwise divergent. In $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ the condition reads $|a_n-A|<\varepsilon$.

Read it like the duel from lesson 1: the Doubter names a tolerance $\varepsilon$, the Champion answers with a starting index $N$, and from $N$ on, every term must lie within $\varepsilon$ of $A$. The $N$ may depend on $\varepsilon$. In the language of balls: every ball $B_\varepsilon(A)$ contains all terms from some point on. Changing finitely many terms, or shifting the index (Lemma 5.25), changes nothing.

Proof B: a limit is unique (Lemma 5.22, p. 237)

The idea. If the arrows eventually land within $\varepsilon$ of two different targets $A_1$ and $A_2$, then by the triangle inequality the targets are within $2\varepsilon$ of each other. Choose $\varepsilon$ as half their distance and you get a contradiction.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • “Unique” is easiest by contradiction: first line “Suppose $a_n\to A_1$ and $a_n\to A_2$ with $A_1\neq A_2$.”, last line “…contradiction.”
  • Using convergence twice gives two indices $N_1,N_2$, and you need one $n$ that is past both.
  • The gap to fill: choose $\varepsilon$ so that the triangle inequality contradicts itself.
The storyWhat you write

Suppose the arrows home in on two different targets at once.

Suppose $a_n\to A_1$ and $a_n\to A_2$ with $A_1\neq A_2$. Let $\varepsilon=\frac{d(A_1,A_2)}2>0$.

Eventually all arrows are near the first target, and eventually all are near the second. Wait until both have kicked in.

There are $N_1,N_2$ with $d(a_n,A_1)<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N_1$ and $d(a_n,A_2)<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N_2$. Let $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$.

Arrow number $N$ is close to both targets, so the targets are close to each other, closer than they actually are.

By the triangle inequality, $$\begin{aligned}d(A_1,A_2)&\le d(A_1,a_N)+d(a_N,A_2)\\&<2\varepsilon\\&=d(A_1,A_2)\end{aligned},$$ a contradiction. So the limit is unique. ∎

Moves used: take the larger N insert a middle point ε/2 twice

The proof in one piece

Suppose $a_n\to A_1$ and $a_n\to A_2$ with $A_1\neq A_2$, and let $\varepsilon=\frac12d(A_1,A_2)>0$. Choose $N_1,N_2$ with $d(a_n,A_1)<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N_1$ and $d(a_n,A_2)<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N_2$, and let $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$. Then $$\begin{aligned}d(A_1,A_2)&\le d(A_1,a_N)+d(a_N,A_2)\\&<2\varepsilon\\&=d(A_1,A_2)\end{aligned}$$, a contradiction. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Assume two limits $A_1\neq A_2$; set $\varepsilon=\frac12d(A_1,A_2)$.
  2. Two indices $N_1,N_2$; take $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$.
  3. Triangle inequality through $a_N$: $d(A_1,A_2)<2\varepsilon=d(A_1,A_2)$. Contradiction.

In short: “half the distance, max of the Ns, triangle through $a_N$”.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why do we take $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$ rather than just $N_1$?

    Answer

    We need one index $n$ for which both $d(a_n,A_1)<\varepsilon$ and $d(a_n,A_2)<\varepsilon$ hold. That's guaranteed for $n\ge N_1$ and for $n\ge N_2$, so for $n\ge\max\{N_1,N_2\}$.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the last two sentences (from “Let $N=\dots$”).

    Answer

    See the one-piece proof, from “and let $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$”.

  3. Rung 3 (3 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Proof C: a convergent sequence is bounded (Lemma 5.27, p. 238)

A sequence in $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ or a normed space is bounded if there is $M$ with $\|a_n\|\le M$ (in $\mathbb{R}$: $|a_n|\le M$) for all $n$.

The idea. From some point on, all terms are within $1$ of the limit $A$, so their size is at most $1+|A|$. Before that point there are only finitely many terms, and finitely many numbers always have a maximum.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Last line: “…so $|a_n|\le M$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$.” We must name $M$.
  • The given: convergence holds for every $\varepsilon$, so we may use one $\varepsilon$ of our choice: $\varepsilon=1$.
  • The gap to fill: handle the tail ($n\ge N$) and the finitely many first terms separately.
The storyWhat you write

Paint a ring of radius $1$ around the target. Eventually every arrow lands in it.

Let $A=\lim a_n$. With $\varepsilon=1$ there is $N$ with $|a_n-A|<1$ for all $n\ge N$.

Those arrows are never further from the centre than the target plus one.

For $n\ge N$: $$|a_n|=|a_n-A+A|\le|a_n-A|+|A|<1+|A|$$.

The early wild arrows are only finitely many, so one of them is the furthest out.

Let $M=\max\{|a_1|,\dots,|a_{N-1}|,\ 1+|A|\}$. Then $|a_n|\le M$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$. ∎

Moves used: use convergence with a chosen ε insert a middle point

The proof in one piece

Let $A=\lim a_n$. There is $N$ with $|a_n-A|<1$ for $n\ge N$, so $|a_n|\le|a_n-A|+|A|<1+|A|$ for $n\ge N$. Hence $$|a_n|\le M=\max\{|a_1|,\dots,|a_{N-1}|,1+|A|\}$$for all $n$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Convergence with $\varepsilon=1$ gives $N$.
  2. Tail: $|a_n|\le|a_n-A|+|A|<1+|A|$ for $n\ge N$.
  3. Head: $M=\max\{|a_1|,\dots,|a_{N-1}|,1+|A|\}$.

In short: “ε = 1, tail by the triangle inequality, head by a finite max”.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Why can't we take $M=1+|A|$ alone?

    Answer

    The terms $a_1,\dots,a_{N-1}$ before $N$ can be far from $A$, for example $a_1=1000$. Only the tail is controlled by convergence.

  2. Rung 2 (2 min). Write the line for the tail, with the triangle inequality.

    Answer

    “For $n\ge N$: $$\begin{aligned}|a_n|&=|(a_n-A)+A|\\&\le |a_n-A|+|A|\\&<1+|A|\end{aligned}$$.”

  3. Rung 3 (2 min). Write the whole proof from the frame alone.

Contrapositive, very useful in multiple choice: an unbounded sequence diverges. For example $a_n=n$ and $a_n=(-2)^n$ diverge. The converse is false: $(-1)^n$ is bounded but divergent (Worked example 1).

5. The limit rules

Proposition 5.30 (p. 239–241). Let $a_n\to A$ and $b_n\to B$ in $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. Then

  • $a_n+b_n\to A+B$ and $\alpha a_n\to\alpha A$ for every constant $\alpha$,
  • $a_nb_n\to AB$,
  • if $a_n\neq0$ for all $n$ and $A\neq0$, then $\frac1{a_n}\to\frac1A$ (so also $\frac{b_n}{a_n}\to\frac BA$).

Proof D: the sum rule

The idea. To get $a_n+b_n$ within $\varepsilon$ of $A+B$, get each of $a_n$, $b_n$ within $\frac\varepsilon2$ of its limit, and wait until both have happened. The triangle inequality adds the two errors.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Let $\varepsilon>0$.” Last line: “…so $|(a_n+b_n)-(A+B)|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.”
  • The gap to fill: choose $N$ from the two given convergences.
The storyWhat you write

The Doubter names a tolerance. Split it in half, one half for each sequence.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. There are $N_1,N_2$ with $|a_n-A|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $n\ge N_1$ and $|b_n-B|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $n\ge N_2$.

Wait until both have kicked in, then add the two errors.

Let $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$. For $n\ge N$: $$\begin{aligned}|(a_n+b_n)-(A+B)|&\le |a_n-A|+|b_n-B|\\&<\tfrac\varepsilon2+\tfrac\varepsilon2\\&=\varepsilon\end{aligned}.$$ So $a_n+b_n\to A+B$. ∎

Moves used: ε/2 twice take the larger N

The proof in one piece

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N_1,N_2$ with $|a_n-A|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $n\ge N_1$ and $|b_n-B|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $n\ge N_2$, and let $N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}$. For $n\ge N$, $$\begin{aligned}|(a_n+b_n)-(A+B)|&\le |a_n-A|+|b_n-B|\\&<\varepsilon\end{aligned}$$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder

  1. Rung 1 (1 min). Fill the blank: “$|(a_n+b_n)-(A+B)|=$ ______ $\le|a_n-A|+|b_n-B|$.”

6. Subsequences, accumulation points, and coordinates

Definition 5.38 (p. 242). If $n_1<n_2<n_3<\dots$ is a strictly increasing sequence of indices, then $(a_{n_k})_k$ is a subsequence of $(a_n)_n$. You keep infinitely many terms, in order, and drop the rest.

Lemma 5.39 (p. 243). Every subsequence of a convergent sequence converges, to the same limit.

Proposition 5.42 (p. 243). $A$ is an accumulation point of $(a_n)_n$ if some subsequence converges to $A$. Equivalently: for every $\varepsilon>0$ and every $N$ there is an $n\ge N$ with $d(a_n,A)<\varepsilon$, so the sequence comes within $\varepsilon$ of $A$ again and again.

Lemma 5.39 is a quick way to prove divergence: find two subsequences with different limits. For $a_n=(-1)^n$, the even terms are constantly $1$ and the odd terms constantly $-1$, so $(a_n)_n$ can't converge. Its accumulation points are $1$ and $-1$. (Why Lemma 5.39 holds: since $n_k\ge k$, once $k\ge N$ we also have $n_k\ge N$, so $d(a_{n_k},A)<\varepsilon$.)

Coordinates (Proposition 5.44 and Korollar 5.45, p. 244–246). A sequence in $\mathbb{R}^d$ converges if and only if each coordinate converges, and it doesn't matter whether you use $\|\cdot\|_1$, $\|\cdot\|_2$ or $\|\cdot\|_\infty$. In particular, $z_n\to z$ in $\mathbb{C}$ if and only if $\operatorname{Re}z_n\to\operatorname{Re}z$ and $\operatorname{Im}z_n\to\operatorname{Im}z$. The reason is the inequalities $\|w\|_\infty\le\|w\|_2\le\|w\|_1\le d\,\|w\|_\infty$ from the figure: if one norm of $v_n-v$ is small, all are.

7. Continuity, now with sequences

Definition 5.48 (p. 247). Let $X,Y$ be metric spaces and $f:X\to Y$. Then $f$ is ε-δ continuous at $x_0$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ with $f(B_\delta(x_0))\subseteq B_\varepsilon(f(x_0))$. And $f$ is sequentially continuous at $x_0$ if for every sequence $x_n\to x_0$ in $X$ we have $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$.

Lemma 5.49 (p. 247). The two are equivalent.

On $\mathbb{R}$, ε-δ continuity is exactly lesson 7's definition, with balls in place of $|x-x_0|<\delta$. The new thing is the sequence version, and the theorem that it's the same.

Proof E: ε-δ continuity ⇔ sequential continuity

You'll need these
  • ε-δ continuity at $x_0$: $$\begin{aligned}&\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x:\\&\quad d(x,x_0)<\delta\Rightarrow d(f(x),f(x_0))<\varepsilon\end{aligned}$$.
  • Its negation (the negation recipe): $\exists\varepsilon>0\ \forall\delta>0\ \exists x:\ d(x,x_0)<\delta$ and $d(f(x),f(x_0))\ge\varepsilon$.
  • $\frac1n\to0$ (Worked example 1, by Archimedes).

The idea. (⇒) If $f$ keeps a whole ball around $x_0$ close to $f(x_0)$, then any sequence that eventually enters that ball has images that eventually stay close. (⇐) Go by contrapositive. If $f$ is not ε-δ continuous, then for one bad $\varepsilon$, every ball around $x_0$, however small, contains a bad point. Pick one bad point in the ball of radius $1$, one in radius $\frac12$, one in radius $\frac13$, and so on. These bad points converge to $x_0$, but their images stay $\varepsilon$ away from $f(x_0)$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (⇒) First lines: “Let $x_n\to x_0$ and $\varepsilon>0$.” Last line: “…so $d(f(x_n),f(x_0))<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.” The gap: find $N$.
  • (⇐) by contrapositive. First line: “Suppose $f$ is not ε-δ continuous at $x_0$. Then there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that for every $\delta>0$ …” Last line: “…so $f(x_n)\not\to f(x_0)$, and $f$ is not sequentially continuous.” The gap: build the sequence $x_n$.
The storyWhat you write

(⇒) Arrows are homing in on $x_0$, and someone names a tolerance around $f(x_0)$ on the map.

Assume $f$ is ε-δ continuous at $x_0$. Let $x_n\to x_0$ and $\varepsilon>0$.

Continuity gives a ring around $x_0$ that the map sends inside the tolerance.

Choose $\delta>0$ with $d(x,x_0)<\delta\Rightarrow d(f(x),f(x_0))<\varepsilon$.

The arrows eventually all land in that ring, so their images all land in the tolerance.

Since $x_n\to x_0$, there is $N$ with $d(x_n,x_0)<\delta$ for $n\ge N$. Then $d(f(x_n),f(x_0))<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. So $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$.

(⇐) Now suppose the map is not continuous at $x_0$: some tolerance can't be met by any ring.

Suppose $f$ is not ε-δ continuous at $x_0$. Then there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that for every $\delta>0$ some $x$ has $d(x,x_0)<\delta$ but $d(f(x),f(x_0))\ge\varepsilon$.

In the ring of radius $\frac1n$ there is a bad point. Collect one for every $n$.

For each $n\in\mathbb N$ apply this with $\delta=\frac1n$: choose $x_n$ with $d(x_n,x_0)<\frac1n$ and $d(f(x_n),f(x_0))\ge\varepsilon$.

The bad points close in on $x_0$, but their images never come within $\varepsilon$ of $f(x_0)$. So sequences don't work either.

Then $x_n\to x_0$ (since $d(x_n,x_0)<\frac1n\to0$), but $d(f(x_n),f(x_0))\ge\varepsilon$ for all $n$, so $f(x_n)\not\to f(x_0)$. Hence $f$ is not sequentially continuous at $x_0$. By contraposition, sequential continuity implies ε-δ continuity. ∎

The proof in one piece

(⇒) Let $x_n\to x_0$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $\delta>0$ with $d(f(x),f(x_0))<\varepsilon$ whenever $d(x,x_0)<\delta$, and $N$ with $d(x_n,x_0)<\delta$ for $n\ge N$. Then $d(f(x_n),f(x_0))<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$, so $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$.

(⇐) Suppose $f$ is not ε-δ continuous at $x_0$. Then there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that for every $n\in\mathbb N$ there is $x_n$ with $d(x_n,x_0)<\frac1n$ and $d(f(x_n),f(x_0))\ge\varepsilon$. Then $x_n\to x_0$ but $f(x_n)\not\to f(x_0)$, so $f$ is not sequentially continuous at $x_0$. ∎

How to reproduce this under exam pressure

(⇒)

  1. Let $x_n\to x_0$, $\varepsilon>0$. Continuity gives $\delta$.
  2. Convergence of $x_n$ with tolerance $\delta$ gives $N$.
  3. Chain: $$\begin{aligned}n&\ge N\Rightarrow d(x_n,x_0)\\&<\delta\Rightarrow d(f(x_n),f(x_0))\\&<\varepsilon\end{aligned}$$.

(⇐) by contrapositive

  1. Negate ε-δ: one bad $\varepsilon$; for every $\delta$ a bad $x$.
  2. $\delta=\frac1n$ gives bad points $x_n$ with $d(x_n,x_0)<\frac1n$.
  3. Conclude: $x_n\to x_0$ but $d(f(x_n),f(x_0))\ge\varepsilon$ for all $n$.

In short: “δ from continuity, N from convergence” and “negate, take δ = 1/n”.

Why this is useful: the sequence test for discontinuity. To show $f$ is not continuous at $x_0$, you only need one sequence $x_n\to x_0$ with $f(x_n)\not\to f(x_0)$. For example, the sign function with $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$ and $\operatorname{sgn}(x)=1$ for $x>0$: $x_n=\frac1n\to0$ but $\operatorname{sgn}(x_n)=1\to1\neq0$. Building continuous functions (Proposition 5.53, p. 249). Compositions of continuous functions are continuous. A function into $\mathbb{R}^d$ is continuous exactly when each coordinate is. Addition, multiplication and $z\mapsto\frac1z$ (on $z\neq0$) are continuous. With sequences, compositions take one line: if $x_n\to x_0$, then $f(x_n)\to f(x_0)$, so $g(f(x_n))\to g(f(x_0))$.

Uniformly continuous and Lipschitz (Definition 5.52, p. 248) mean the same as in lesson 8, with $d$ in place of $|\cdot|$.

Proof F: closed sets contain their limits

Recall from lesson 5: $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ is closed if $\mathbb{R}\setminus A$ is open. This test turns “closed” into a statement about sequences, which is how multiple-choice questions like Figalli FS26 MC1 are solved. (It holds the same way in every metric space.)

Claim. $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ is closed if and only if for every sequence $(a_n)_n$ in $A$ that converges in $\mathbb{R}$, the limit lies in $A$.

The idea. (⇒) A limit outside $A$ would have a whole ball outside $A$ around it, since $\mathbb{R}\setminus A$ is open. But the sequence eventually enters every ball around its limit, and its terms are in $A$. (⇐) If the complement were not open, some point $x\notin A$ would have points of $A$ in every ball around it. Pick one in each ball of radius $\frac1n$: a sequence in $A$ converging to $x\notin A$. This is the same $\delta=\frac1n$ trick as in Proof E.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • (⇒) First lines: “Let $a_n\in A$ with $a_n\to x$. Suppose $x\notin A$.” Last line: “contradiction, so $x\in A$.”
  • (⇐) Contrapositive. First line: “Suppose $\mathbb{R}\setminus A$ is not open.” Last line: “…so $(a_n)_n$ is a sequence in $A$ whose limit is not in $A$.”
The storyWhat you write

(⇒) Arrows all landing inside the walled land home in on a target outside it. Around that target there's a whole ring outside the walls.

Let $A$ be closed, $a_n\in A$ for all $n$ and $a_n\to x$. Suppose $x\notin A$. Since $\mathbb{R}\setminus A$ is open, there is $r>0$ with $(x-r,x+r)\subseteq\mathbb{R}\setminus A$.

But eventually the arrows land in that ring, outside the walls. Impossible.

By convergence there is $N$ with $|a_N-x|<r$, so $a_N\in(x-r,x+r)\subseteq\mathbb{R}\setminus A$, contradicting $a_N\in A$. So $x\in A$.

(⇐) If the outside isn't open, there's a point outside with land of $A$ in every ring around it.

Suppose $\mathbb{R}\setminus A$ is not open. Then there is $x\notin A$ such that for every $r>0$, $(x-r,x+r)\not\subseteq\mathbb{R}\setminus A$, that is, $(x-r,x+r)$ contains a point of $A$.

Take one such point in the ring of radius $\frac1n$, for every $n$. They close in on a point outside $A$.

For each $n$ choose $a_n\in A$ with $|a_n-x|<\frac1n$. Then $a_n\to x$ and $x\notin A$. By contraposition, if all limits of convergent sequences in $A$ lie in $A$, then $\mathbb{R}\setminus A$ is open, so $A$ is closed. ∎

The proof in one piece

(⇒) Let $A$ be closed, $a_n\in A$, $a_n\to x$, and suppose $x\notin A$. As $\mathbb{R}\setminus A$ is open, $(x-r,x+r)\subseteq\mathbb{R}\setminus A$ for some $r>0$. Some $a_N$ satisfies $|a_N-x|<r$, so $a_N\notin A$, a contradiction.

The worked examples are questions: try each one in your head, then open the answer. Your answers, in order, are the solution.

Worked example 1: $\frac1n\to0$, and $(-1)^n$ diverges (Beispiel 5.28, p. 239)

  1. Write what “$\frac1n\to0$” means, with quantifiers.

    Answer

    $$\begin{aligned}&\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\mathbb N\ \forall n\ge N:\\&\quad \left|\frac1n-0\right|<\varepsilon\end{aligned}$$.

  2. Given $\varepsilon>0$, which theorem gives you $N$, and how?

    Answer

    The Archimedean principle gives $N\in\mathbb N$ with $N>\frac1\varepsilon$, that is, $\frac1N<\varepsilon$.

  3. Finish the proof.

    Answer

    For $n\ge N$: $\left|\frac1n-0\right|=\frac1n\le\frac1N<\varepsilon$. So $\frac1n\to0$.

  4. Now $a_n=(-1)^n$. Which two subsequences show it diverges, and which lemma do you cite?

    Answer

    $a_{2k}=1\to1$ and $a_{2k+1}=-1\to-1$. If $(a_n)_n$ converged to some $A$, both subsequences would converge to $A$ by Lemma 5.39 (subsequences of a convergent sequence have the same limit), so $1=A=-1$, a contradiction.

Worked example 2: geometric sequences (Beispiel 5.34, p. 241–242)

Show that $q^n\to0$ if $|q|<1$, and that $(q^n)_n$ diverges if $|q|>1$.

  1. Start with $|q|>1$. Write $|q|=1+x$ with $x>0$. Which inequality from lesson 4 bounds $(1+x)^n$ from below?

    Answer

    Bernoulli's inequality: $(1+x)^n\ge1+nx$ for $x\ge-1$.

  2. Why does that make $(q^n)_n$ unbounded, and why does unbounded give divergence?

    Answer

    Given $M>0$, Archimedes gives $n$ with $1+nx>M$, so $|q^n|=|q|^n\ge1+nx>M$. No $M$ bounds the sequence. A convergent sequence is bounded (Proof C), so $(q^n)_n$ diverges.

  3. Now $0<|q|<1$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Apply the first part to $\frac1{|q|}>1$. What do you get?

    Answer

    $\left(\frac1{|q|}\right)^n$ is unbounded, so there is $N$ with $\frac1{|q|^N}>\frac1\varepsilon$, that is, $|q|^N<\varepsilon$.

  4. Finish: why does this hold for all $n\ge N$, not just $n=N$?

    Answer

    Since $|q|<1$, the powers decrease: $|q^n-0|=|q|^n\le|q|^N<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. So $q^n\to0$. (For $q=0$ the sequence is constant $0$.)

Worked example 3: compute a limit with the rules

Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{3n^2+n}{n^2-5}$.

  1. The rules need convergent pieces, but $3n^2+n$ and $n^2-5$ are unbounded. What do you do first?

    Answer

    Divide numerator and denominator by the highest power, $n^2$: $\dfrac{3n^2+n}{n^2-5}=\dfrac{3+\frac1n}{1-\frac5{n^2}}$ for $n\ge3$ (where $n^2-5\neq0$).

  2. What are the limits of the numerator and denominator, and which rules did you use?

    Answer

    $\frac1n\to0$ (Worked example 1), so $\frac1{n^2}=\frac1n\cdot\frac1n\to0$ (product rule) and $\frac5{n^2}\to0$ (constant multiple). By the sum rule, $3+\frac1n\to3$ and $1-\frac5{n^2}\to1$.

  3. Conclude.

    Answer

    The denominator tends to $1\neq0$ and is nonzero for $n\ge3$, so by the quotient rule the limit is $\frac31=3$. (Starting at $n=3$ is allowed, because shifting the index doesn't change convergence.)

Why it's cool. One definition of distance, three axioms, covers points on a line, vectors in $\mathbb R^n$ and even whole functions. So one proof of “limits are unique” or “convergent ⇒ bounded” works in all of them at once: that is the payoff of abstraction.

Exercise 1 (Übung 5.32 (i), p. 241)

Compute, or show the limit doesn't exist: (a) $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{7n^4+15}{3n^4+n^3+n-1}$, (b) $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^2+5}{n^3+n+1}$, (c) $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^5-10}{n^2+1}$.

Solution

(b) Divide by $n^3$: $\dfrac{\frac1n+\frac5{n^3}}{1+\frac1{n^2}+\frac1{n^3}}\to\frac{0}{1}=0$.

(c) Diverges. For $n\ge2$: $n^5-10\ge\frac{n^5}2$ (since $\frac{n^5}2\ge16>10$) and $n^2+1\le2n^2$, so $\dfrac{n^5-10}{n^2+1}\ge\dfrac{n^5/2}{2n^2}=\dfrac{n^3}4$. That's unbounded, and an unbounded sequence diverges (Proof C).

General rule (Übung 5.32 (ii)): for polynomials $p,q$ of degrees $k,\ell$, $\frac{p(n)}{q(n)}$ tends to the ratio of leading coefficients if $k=\ell$, to $0$ if $k<\ell$, and diverges if $k>\ell$.

Exercise 3 (discrete metric)

Let $X$ be any set with the discrete metric. (a) Describe $B_r(x)$ for $r\le1$ and for $r>1$. (b) Which sequences converge in $X$?

Solution

(a) $d(y,x)<r$ with $r\le1$ means $d(y,x)=0$, so $B_r(x)=\{x\}$. For $r>1$, every point has distance $0$ or $1<r$, so $B_r(x)=X$. In particular, every subset of $X$ is open (each point has the ball $\{x\}=B_{1/2}(x)$ inside the set).

(b) Exactly the eventually constant sequences. If $a_n\to A$, take $\varepsilon=\frac12$: there is $N$ with $d(a_n,A)<\frac12$, so $a_n=A$, for all $n\ge N$. Conversely, an eventually constant sequence converges to the value it eventually takes.

Exercise 6 (true or false, from the script, p. 251)

Let $a_n\to a$ and $b_n\to b$ in $\mathbb{R}$. (i) If $a_n\le b_n$ for all but finitely many $n$, then $a\le b$. (ii) If $a_n\le b_n$ for infinitely many $n$, then $a\le b$. (iii) If $a_n<b_n$ for all but finitely many $n$, then $a<b$. (iv) If $a_n<b_n$ for infinitely many $n$, then $a<b$.

Solution
  • (i) True. Frame: contradiction, “Suppose $a>b$”, then continuity of the limit with $\varepsilon$ = half the gap. Moves: assume the opposite. Suppose $a>b$ and let $\varepsilon=\frac{a-b}2$. For large $n$, $a_n>a-\varepsilon=\frac{a+b}2$ and $b_n<b+\varepsilon=\frac{a+b}2$, so $a_n>b_n$ for all large $n$, contradicting $a_n\le b_n$ for all but finitely many $n$.
  • (ii) True. The indices $n$ with $a_n\le b_n$ are infinitely many, so they form subsequences $a_{n_k}\le b_{n_k}$ for all $k$. Subsequences of convergent sequences have the same limits, so $a_{n_k}\to a$ and $b_{n_k}\to b$, and by (i) $a\le b$.
  • (iii) False. $a_n=0<\frac1n=b_n$ for all $n$, but $a=b=0$.
  • (iv) False, by the same example.

So (i) and (ii) are true, (iii) and (iv) false. Strict inequalities can become equalities in the limit.

Bonus

Prove $\|w\|_\infty\le\|w\|_1\le d\,\|w\|_\infty$ for $w\in\mathbb{R}^d$, and deduce: if $\|v_n-v\|_\infty\to0$ then $\|v_n-v\|_1\to0$.

Solution

Frame: two inequalities between finite sums, each one line; then the usual “Let $\varepsilon>0$ … for $n\ge N$” for the limit, with $\frac\varepsilon d$ as the tolerance.

Let $w=(w_1,\dots,w_d)$ and $|w_m|=\max_j|w_j|=\|w\|_\infty$. Then $\|w\|_\infty=|w_m|\le\sum_j|w_j|=\|w\|_1$, and $$\|w\|_1=\sum_j|w_j|\le\sum_j|w_m|=d\,\|w\|_\infty.$$

If $\|v_n-v\|_\infty\to0$, let $\varepsilon>0$ and $N$ with $\|v_n-v\|_\infty<\frac\varepsilon d$ for $n\ge N$. Then $\|v_n-v\|_1\le d\,\|v_n-v\|_\infty<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. ∎

Scoring yourself: Exercise 1 (2), Exercise 2 (2), Exercise 3 (1) and Exercise 6 (2) make 7. If you have 5 of 7, you're done. Exercises 4 and 5 and the Bonus are proof practice and don't count against you.

10. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

The same sequence, different verdicts. Whether a sequence converges depends on the metric, not only on the points. Take $a_n=\left(\frac1n,0\right)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. With the Euclidean or Manhattan metric, $a_n\to(0,0)$. With the discrete metric, $d(a_n,(0,0))=1$ for every $n$, so it does not converge at all: in the discrete metric only sequences that are eventually constant converge. In the French railway metric (on $\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R}^2$), $a_n$ lies on a single ray through the origin, so it converges to the origin there too. Now try $b_n=\left(1,\frac1n\right)$: in the Euclidean metric $b_n\to(1,0)$, but in the French railway metric $b_n$ and $(1,0)$ never lie on a common ray through the origin, so $d(b_n,(1,0))=\|b_n\|+1\ge1$ for all $n$, and $b_n$ does not converge to $(1,0)$.

A world where every triangle is isosceles. Fix a prime, say $p=3$. For rationals $x\neq y$, write $x-y=3^k\cdot\frac ab$ with $a,b$ not divisible by $3$, and set $d_3(x,y)=3^{-k}$ (and $d_3(x,x)=0$). This is a metric, the 3-adic metric, and it satisfies a much stronger triangle inequality: $d_3(x,z)\le\max\{d_3(x,y),d_3(y,z)\}$. In this world $1,3,9,27,\dots$ get closer to $0$, so $3^n\to0$, and every point of a ball is its centre. Completing $\mathbb{Q}$ in this metric gives the $p$-adic numbers, a central tool of number theory (Andrew Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem uses them).

Averages are calmer than sequences. The sequence $(-1)^n$ diverges, but its running averages $\frac{a_1+\dots+a_n}{n}$ are $-1,0,-\frac13,0,\dots$, which tend to $0$. Cesàro proved that if $a_n\to A$, then the averages also tend to $A$ (Übung 5.36), and the example shows the converse fails. Averaging is how physicists and engineers tame oscillating signals.
