Recap of lesson 1 (5 minutes)

A group is a set with an associative operation, a neutral element and inverses; the neutral element and each inverse are unique. $\mathbb Z/n$ is an abelian group because addition of classes is well defined. A ring has two operations, an abelian group $+$ and an associative $\cdot$ that distributes over $+$; a field asks moreover that every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. Check yourself: why is $\mathbb Z/6$ not a field?

Answer

$\bar2\cdot\bar3=\bar0$ with $\bar2\ne\bar0\ne\bar3$: zero divisors. If $\bar2$ had an inverse, $\bar3=\bar2^{-1}\cdot\bar2\cdot\bar3=\bar2^{-1}\cdot\bar0=\bar0$, which is false.

1. The story: the scribes of the Free City

The guilds of the Free City have ranks now, and ranks bring disputes. Who is really a group? Who only claims to be? The city court settles every dispute with a written charter, and the court is strict about how charters are written. A scribe may use only four seals: NOT, AND, OR, and IF–THEN. And two stamps: the stamp “there is a member such that …” and the stamp “for every member …”. Every charter, however long, must be built from these.

The court also keeps an oracle. Hand it a charter made only of seals, and it tries out every possible way the small clauses could turn out, true or false, and reports when the whole charter holds. Two charters that the oracle always answers the same way count as the same charter, however differently they look.

The most feared scribe in the city has one skill that wins nearly every case: given any charter, she writes its exact rebuttal without thinking. “Every member has a partner who undoes them” becomes, under her quill, “there is a member whom no partner undoes”. You are her apprentice. By the end of today you will rebut like her.

In mathematics: a charter is a statement, the seals are the logical operators $\neg,\wedge,\vee,\to$, the stamps are the quantifiers $\exists$ and $\forall$. The oracle is the truth table, “the same charter” is logical equivalence, and the rebuttal is the negation. Why Linear Algebra needs this: every definition from now on (subgroup, vector space, linearly independent, basis, linear map) is one sentence with several quantifiers. To prove that something is not a basis, or to start a proof by contradiction, you must write the negation of that sentence correctly, and that is a mechanical skill you can learn in one hour.

2. Two levels: objects and the language about them

Halbeisen opens §1 with a distinction. On one side are the mathematical objects: numbers, functions, sets, points, lines (“we act as if these objects existed”). On the other side is the language of logic in which we describe them. The first is the semantic level, the second the syntactic level. To really understand what mathematics does, one would have to keep them apart cleanly. The lecture says openly that it won't (that happens in the course on foundations), but the handout Syntax & Semantik gives a picture worth keeping:

The score and the performance

The syntax is like a musical score: black on white, it fixes which notes are to be played. The semantics is the performance: it must follow the score but brings it to life. The group axioms are a score; a concrete group such as $(\mathbb Z,+)$ is a performance of it. A formula on the page is neither true nor false. It becomes true or false only once it is interpreted, that is, once we say what its symbols mean.

The handout Logik I builds the language from an alphabet, in three layers. You need the words, not the formal details:

  • Symbols. Variables $x,y,v_0,\dots$ (placeholders for objects), the operators $\neg,\wedge,\vee,\to,\leftrightarrow$, the quantifiers $\exists,\forall$, and “$=$”. Each theory adds its own symbols: group theory adds a constant $e$ and an operation symbol $\circ$.
  • Terms are the names of objects: $x$, $e$, $x\circ(y\circ e)$. A term is like a noun. It says nothing, so it is never true or false.
  • Formulas are the sentences: $x\circ e=x$, $\neg(x=y)$, $\forall x\,(x\circ e=x)$. Interpreted, a formula becomes a statement that is true or false.

This explains a type of exam question. “$x^2+1$” is a term, so “Is $x^2+1$ true?” is nonsense. “$x^2>x$” is a formula only if the language has a symbol $>$, and a general field has none. Worked example 8 below is exactly that question.

3. The four logical operators

Definition (logical operators). Logical operators are 1- or 2-place operators on statements $A,B,C,\dots$:

  • $\neg A$ is read “NOT $A$” (negation);
  • $A\wedge B$ is read “$A$ AND $B$”;
  • $A\vee B$ is read “$A$ OR $B$”, the inclusive or: it allows both;
  • $A\to B$ is read “IF $A$ THEN $B$”, also “$A$ IMPLIES $B$”. $A$ is the premise, $B$ the conclusion.

Abbreviations. $A\leftrightarrow B$ stands for $(A\to B)\wedge(B\to A)$, read “$A$ if and only if $B$”. The exclusive or $A\veebar B$ stands for $(A\vee B)\wedge\neg(A\wedge B)$, “exactly one of $A$, $B$” (rarely used). And $x\ne y$ stands for $\neg(x=y)$; likewise $x\notin M$ for $\neg(x\in M)$.

Two remarks that save points. First, mathematical “or” is always inclusive. “$x=0$ or $y=0$” is true when both are $0$. Second, “$\to$” is a symbol inside a statement, while the double arrow “$\iff$” of the next section is a claim about two statements. The old exams write $\Rightarrow$ and $\Leftrightarrow$ for $\to$ and $\leftrightarrow$; inside a formula they mean the same thing.

The column to remember is $A\to B$: it is false in exactly one row, “$A$ true, $B$ false”. A promise “if it rains, I bring an umbrella” is broken only if it rains and I don't bring one. On a dry day the promise is kept, whatever I carry.

A false premise implies anything (lecture example)

Let $A$ be the statement $0=1$ and $B$ the statement $1=1$, interpreted in $\mathbb N$. Then $A$ is false. Both rows with $A=0$ give $1$ in the column $A\to B$, so both

$$0=1\ \to\ 1=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0=1\ \to\ 1\ne1$$

are true statements. This is not a trick. It is why “for all $x\in\emptyset$, …” is always true (section 5), and why a proof by contradiction works (lesson 3).

A tautology is a statement that is true under every interpretation, i.e. whose column in the truth table is all $1$. The simplest is $A\vee\neg A$: whatever $A$ is, one of the two holds. Halbeisen's logical axioms (handout Logik II) start with exactly this one.

Worked example 1: $A$ is true and $B$ false. Which expression is true? (FS19, question I)

(a) $(\neg B\wedge\neg A)\vee(B\wedge A)$ (b) $(A\wedge(B\vee\neg A))\vee(B\vee\neg A)$ (c) $(\neg B\wedge A)\wedge((B\vee A)\wedge A)$ (d) $B\wedge(A\vee(B\wedge A)\vee\neg A)$. Try it in your head first.

  1. What are $\neg A$ and $\neg B$?
    Answer

    $\neg A=0$ and $\neg B=1$.

  2. Which options die at once because of an AND with a false part?
    Answer

    (d) starts with $B\wedge\dots$ and $B=0$, so (d) is $0$. In (a), both brackets contain a false part ($\neg A$, resp. $B$), so (a) is $0\vee0=0$.

  3. Evaluate (b) and (c).
    Answer

    (b): $B\vee\neg A=0\vee0=0$, so (b) is $(1\wedge0)\vee0=0$. (c): $\neg B\wedge A=1\wedge1=1$ and $(B\vee A)\wedge A=1\wedge1=1$, so (c) is $1$. Answer (c).

Habit for the exam: write the values of the small pieces above the formula, then evaluate from the inside out. Look for an AND with a $0$ or an OR with a $1$ first; they decide the whole bracket.

4. Logical equivalence

Definition (logically equivalent). Two statements $A$ and $B$ are logically equivalent, written $A\iff B$, if both $A\to B$ and $B\to A$ can be proved from the logical axioms. For example $A\iff A$ and $A\iff A\wedge A$ for every $A$.

That is the lecture's definition, and it is a statement about proofs (the syntactic level). In practice you check it on the semantic level: for statements built from operators only, $A\iff B$ holds exactly when $A$ and $B$ have the same column in the truth table. That the two agree is a theorem of logic (propositional logic is complete), which we take as given. So there are two ways to show an equivalence, and you should master both:

  • The oracle: a truth table with a row for every combination of truth values (4 rows for two letters, 8 for three).
  • The scribe: a chain of known equivalences, one per step, each named.

To show that two expressions are not equivalent, one row is enough: name values of $A,B,\dots$ for which one is true and the other false.

Proof A: De Morgan's law $\neg(A\wedge B)\iff\neg A\vee\neg B$, by truth table

Claim. For all statements $A,B$: $\neg(A\wedge B)$ and $\neg A\vee\neg B$ are logically equivalent.

The idea. Both sides only depend on the truth values of $A$ and $B$. There are four combinations. Compute both sides in each of them; if the two columns agree, the statements are equivalent.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “We compare the truth tables of both sides.”
  • Last line: “The columns agree in all four rows, so $\neg(A\wedge B)\iff\neg A\vee\neg B$.”
  • The gap: four rows, each side evaluated with the table of section 3.

Moves used: check with a truth table

The proof in one piece

Both sides depend only on the truth values of $A$ and $B$. If $A$ and $B$ are both true, $A\wedge B$ is true, so $\neg(A\wedge B)$ is false, and $\neg A\vee\neg B$ is false since both $\neg A$, $\neg B$ are false. In the other three cases at least one of $A,B$ is false: then $A\wedge B$ is false, so $\neg(A\wedge B)$ is true, and at least one of $\neg A,\neg B$ is true, so $\neg A\vee\neg B$ is true. The two sides have the same truth value in all cases. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Which single row of the table is the interesting one, and why?
    Answer

    The row $A=B=1$: it is the only row where $A\wedge B$ is true, so the only row where both sides are false.

  2. Rung 2. Prove the other De Morgan law, $\neg(A\vee B)\iff\neg A\wedge\neg B$, in the same way.
    Answer

    Columns: $\neg(A\vee B)$ is $1,0,0,0$; $\neg A\wedge\neg B$ is $(1\wedge1),(1\wedge0),$ $(0\wedge1),(0\wedge0)$ $=1,0,0,0$. They agree. In words: both are true only in the row $A=B=0$.

  3. Rung 3. Cover the table and write Proof A from the frame alone, either as a table or as the case distinction of the one-piece version.
Try it: the oracle (a truth-table checker)

Type one expression, or two to compare them. Use the letters A–E, the buttons, and brackets. The oracle lists every row; where two expressions differ, the row is marked. Typing works too: ~ or ! for not, & for and, | for or, -> and <->.

The oracle reads $\neg$ before $\wedge$ before $\vee$ before $\to$ before $\leftrightarrow$, and $A\to B\to C$ as $A\to(B\to C)$. On paper, don't rely on such conventions: always write the brackets, as Halbeisen does.

Proof B: the contrapositive, $A\to B\iff\neg B\to\neg A$

Claim (lecture p. 5, marked “we need this later”). For all statements $A,B$: $A\to B$ and $\neg B\to\neg A$ are logically equivalent.

The idea. An implication can be rewritten as an OR. Rewrite both sides as ORs; they turn out to be the same OR, up to order and a double negation. This time we go by the scribe's way, a chain of named equivalences, because it is the way that also works once quantifiers appear.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Start: $A\to B$. End: $\neg B\to\neg A$.
  • Allowed steps: the equivalences of the box above, one per step, each named.
  • The gap: get rid of “$\to$” first, since only one rule touches it, then work back.
The storyWhat you write

The apprentice rewrites the charter “if the guild meets, the bell rings” as “the guild doesn't meet, or the bell rings”.

$A\to B\iff\neg A\vee B\quad$ (implication as OR)

OR doesn't care about the order of its clauses.

$\phantom{A\to B}\iff B\vee\neg A\quad$ (commutativity of $\vee$)

A double NOT is no NOT, so “the bell rings” may be written as “not: the bell doesn't ring”.

$\phantom{A\to B}\iff\neg\neg B\vee\neg A\quad$ (double negation)

Now read the OR back as an IF, with “the bell doesn't ring” as the premise: if the bell doesn't ring, the guild didn't meet.

$\phantom{A\to B}\iff\neg B\to\neg A\quad$ (implication as OR, with premise $\neg B$ and conclusion $\neg A$). ∎

$$\begin{aligned}A\to B&\iff\neg A\vee B\iff B\vee\neg A\\&\iff\neg\neg B\vee\neg A\iff\neg B\to\neg A,\end{aligned}$$

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Is $A\to B$ equivalent to its converse $B\to A$?
    Answer

    No. One row is enough: $A=0$, $B=1$ makes $A\to B$ true and $B\to A$ false. (Try “Converse” in the oracle.) Mixing up contrapositive and converse is the most common logic mistake in proofs.

  2. Rung 2. Show $A\to B\iff\neg(A\wedge\neg B)$ by a chain.
    Answer

    $\neg(A\wedge\neg B)$ $\iff\neg A\vee\neg\neg B$ $\iff\neg A\vee B$ $\iff A\to B$ (De Morgan, double negation, implication as OR).

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof B from the frame alone, naming the rule after every $\iff$.
How to reproduce this under exam pressure
  1. Replace the implication: $A\to B\iff\neg A\vee B$.
  2. Swap the two clauses of the OR.
  3. Write $B$ as $\neg\neg B$.
  4. Read the OR back as an implication with premise $\neg B$.

In short: to OR, swap, double-NOT, back to IF.

The script uses this right away: “free of zero divisors” says $ab=0\to(a=0\vee b=0)$, and Note 1.3.6 (p. 17) advises remembering the contrapositive, $(a\ne0\wedge b\ne0)\to ab\ne0$. Notice how $\neg(a=0\vee b=0)$ became $a\ne0\wedge b\ne0$: that was De Morgan.

Proof C: negating an implication, $\neg(A\to B)\iff A\wedge\neg B$

Claim. For all statements $A,B$: $\neg(A\to B)\iff A\wedge\neg B$.

The idea. An implication fails in exactly one row of its truth table: premise true, conclusion false. So “the implication fails” must say exactly that. As a chain: turn the implication into an OR and apply De Morgan.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Start: $\neg(A\to B)$. End: $A\wedge\neg B$.
  • The gap: the NOT stands in front of an arrow, and no rule moves a NOT through an arrow. So remove the arrow first.

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Negate “if $n$ is even, then $n^2$ is even” in words.
    Answer

    “$n$ is even and $n^2$ is odd.” A negated implication is not another implication.

  2. Rung 2. Negate $A\leftrightarrow B$ and simplify to an expression without arrows.
    Answer

    $\neg\big((A\to B)\wedge(B\to A)\big)$ $\iff\neg(A\to B)\vee\neg(B\to A)$ $\iff(A\wedge\neg B)\vee(B\wedge\neg A)$, which is the exclusive or $A\veebar B$: exactly one of them holds.

  3. Rung 3. Write Proof C from the frame alone.

Only two operators are needed

With double negation, implication as OR and De Morgan, every expression can be written with only $\neg$ and $\wedge$ (or only $\neg$ and $\vee$):

$$\begin{aligned}A\vee B&\iff\neg\neg(A\vee B)\iff\neg(\neg A\wedge\neg B),\\A\to B&\iff\neg(A\wedge\neg B),\\A\wedge B&\iff\neg(\neg A\vee\neg B).\end{aligned}$$

This is the first half of Quiz 1 and Quiz 5. The second half is the same game with quantifiers (section 5).

Worked example 2: Which expression is equivalent to $C\Leftrightarrow D$? (FS19, question II)

(a) $(C\wedge D)\wedge(\neg C\wedge\neg D)$ (b) $(C\vee\neg C)\vee(D\vee\neg D)$ (c) $(C\vee D)\wedge(\neg C\vee\neg D)$ (d) $(\neg C\vee D)\wedge(\neg D\vee C)$

  1. Unfold the abbreviation $\Leftrightarrow$ and remove both arrows.
    Answer

    $C\Leftrightarrow D$ is $(C\to D)\wedge(D\to C)$ $\iff(\neg C\vee D)\wedge(\neg D\vee C)$. That is literally (d).

  2. To be sure, what are (a), (b), (c)?
    Answer

    (a) contains $C\wedge\neg C$, so it is always false. (b) contains $C\vee\neg C$, so it is always true. (c) says “at least one, and not both”: the exclusive or, the negation of $C\Leftrightarrow D$. Answer (d).

Worked example 3: Which expression is not equivalent to $\neg\big(\neg(A\vee B)\vee(B\wedge A)\big)\wedge B$? (HS18, question V)

(a) $B\wedge A$ (b) $\neg\big(\neg(A\vee B)\vee(B\wedge A)\vee\neg B\big)$ (c) $(A\vee B)\wedge\neg(B\wedge A)\wedge B$ (d) $B\wedge\neg A$

    Answer

    $\neg\big(\neg(A\vee B)\vee(B\wedge A)\big)$ $\iff\neg\neg(A\vee B)\wedge\neg(B\wedge A)$ $\iff(A\vee B)\wedge\neg(B\wedge A)$. With the $\wedge B$ at the end, that is (c).

  2. Simplify (c), knowing that $B$ must be true for it to hold.
    Answer

    If $B$ is true, then $A\vee B$ is true and $\neg(B\wedge A)$ is $\neg A$. So (c) is true exactly when $B$ is true and $A$ is false: it is $B\wedge\neg A$, i.e. (d).

  3. Check (b), and find the odd one out with a single row.
    Answer

    (b) is De Morgan applied to the whole: $\neg(X\vee\neg B)\iff\neg X\wedge B$ with $X=\neg(A\vee B)\vee(B\wedge A)$, the original. (a) is the odd one: with $A=B=1$, (a) is true and the original is false. Answer (a). (Check it in the oracle with the example “HS18 question V”.)

Worked example 4: Which expression is equivalent to $(A\Rightarrow B)\vee(B\Rightarrow A)$ for all statements $A$, $B$? (HS21, question II)

(a) $A\Leftrightarrow B$ (b) $A\vee\neg A$ (c) $A\vee B$ (d) $(\neg A\vee B)\wedge(\neg B\vee A)$

  1. Remove both arrows. What do you get?
    Answer

    $(\neg A\vee B)\vee(\neg B\vee A)$. In a pure OR the brackets and the order don't matter, so this is $(A\vee\neg A)\vee(B\vee\neg B)$.

  2. So what is it, and which option matches?
    Answer

    It contains $A\vee\neg A$, so it is a tautology: true in every row. Of the options only (b) $A\vee\neg A$ is a tautology. (a) and (d) are the same thing, $A\Leftrightarrow B$, false when $A=1$, $B=0$; (c) is false when $A=B=0$. Answer (b). In words: of two statements, one always implies the other, because if $B$ is true then $A\to B$ holds, and if $B$ is false then $B\to A$ holds.

5. Quantifiers

Definition (quantifiers). The existential quantifier $\exists$ and the universal quantifier $\forall$ say something about the elements of a domain (a set) in which they are interpreted.

  • $\exists x\,A(x)$ is read “there exists an $x$ such that $A$ holds for $x$”;
  • $\forall x\,A(x)$ is read “for all $x$, $A$ holds for $x$”.

Bounded quantifiers. Usually we quantify over a set. The lecture's examples:

$$\begin{aligned}\forall x\in\mathbb Z\,(x+0=x)\quad&\text{abbreviates}\quad\forall x\,(x\in\mathbb Z\to x+0=x),\\\exists x\in\mathbb Z\,(x+x=0)\quad&\text{abbreviates}\quad\exists x\,(x\in\mathbb Z\wedge x+x=0).\end{aligned}$$

Notice the pattern: $\forall$ goes with $\to$, $\exists$ goes with $\wedge$. (“$\exists x\,(x\in\mathbb Z\to x+x=0)$” would be true for silly reasons: any $x$ outside $\mathbb Z$ makes the implication true.)

Several variables. $\forall a,b\in R$ is short for $\forall a\in R\ \forall b\in R$. The script writes a colon after the quantifiers, as in $\forall a,b\in R: ab=0\Rightarrow(a=0)\vee(b=0)$ (p. 17); Halbeisen uses brackets. Both mean the same.

Free and bound variables

In $\forall x\,(x\circ e=x)$ the variable $x$ is bound by the quantifier: it is a placeholder inside the quantifier's range, and the statement is about no particular $x$. In $x\circ e=x$ alone, $x$ is free: the formula says something about a specific, not yet chosen $x$, and is true or false only once $x$ is fixed. A formula without free variables is a sentence; only sentences have a truth value on their own. The handout Logik I gives a mean example: in $\exists y\,(x=y)\vee\forall x\,(x=y)$ both $x$ and $y$ occur free (in one half) and bound (in the other).

Two consequences. (1) A bound variable can be renamed: $\exists x\,A(x)$ and $\exists y\,A(y)$ say the same, as long as $y$ does not already occur (handout Tautologien, rule T). The lecture does this in its proof that there is no largest prime, renaming $p_0$ to $q$. (2) An exam answer must not leave a variable free that should be bound: “$x=y^2$ for all $y$” without saying where $x$ comes from is not a statement.

Quantifier duality and negation

Duality (lecture p. 6). For every formula $B(x)$:

$$\forall x\,B(x)\iff\neg\exists x\,\neg B(x),\qquad\exists x\,B(x)\iff\neg\forall x\,\neg B(x).$$

Negating both sides (and cancelling double negations) gives the negation rules:

$$\neg\forall x\,B(x)\iff\exists x\,\neg B(x),\qquad\neg\exists x\,B(x)\iff\forall x\,\neg B(x).$$

In words: “not every member pays” means “some member doesn't pay”; “no member is a traitor” means “every member is not a traitor”. A NOT that walks through a quantifier flips it. With duality, everything can be written with only $\exists$ or only $\forall$; together with section 4, with only $\neg$, $\wedge$ and one quantifier. That is exactly what Quiz 1 and Quiz 5 ask.

Proof D: negating a bounded quantifier keeps the bound

Claim. For every set $M$ and formula $B(x)$: $\neg\forall x\in M\,B(x)\iff\exists x\in M\,\neg B(x)$.

The idea. A bounded quantifier is an abbreviation. Unfold it, flip the quantifier with the negation rule, and use Proof C on the implication that appears. The $\to$ of the $\forall$ turns into the $\wedge$ of the $\exists$, which is exactly the bounded $\exists$ again.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • Unfold: $\forall x\in M\,B(x)$ means $\forall x\,(x\in M\to B(x))$, and $\exists x\in M\,C(x)$ means $\exists x\,(x\in M\wedge C(x))$.
  • The gap: after the quantifier flips, an implication is negated. Proof C turns it into an AND.
The storyWhat you write

The charter says: “every guild member pays the tax”. Before rebutting it, the scribe writes it out in plain stamps: for everyone in the city, if they are a member, they pay.

$\neg\forall x\in M\,B(x)$ $\iff\neg\forall x\,(x\in M\to B(x))\quad$ (definition of the bounded $\forall$)

The NOT jumps over the stamp and flips it: there is someone in the city for whom the clause fails.

$\iff\exists x\,\neg(x\in M\to B(x))\quad$ (negation rule for $\forall$)

“If member, then pays” fails only for a member who doesn't pay. So: some member doesn't pay.

$\iff\exists x\,(x\in M\wedge\neg B(x))\quad$ (Proof C)
$\iff\exists x\in M\,\neg B(x)\quad$ (definition of the bounded $\exists$). ∎

Moves used: flip every quantifier, negate the core a chain of named equivalences

The proof in one piece
$$\begin{aligned}\neg\forall x\in M\,B(x)&\iff\neg\forall x\,(x\in M\to B(x))\\&\iff\exists x\,\neg(x\in M\to B(x))\\&\iff\exists x\,(x\in M\wedge\neg B(x))\\&\iff\exists x\in M\,\neg B(x),\end{aligned}$$

using in turn: the definition of the bounded $\forall$, the negation rule for $\forall$, $\neg(A\to B)\iff A\wedge\neg B$, the definition of the bounded $\exists$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. Fill in: $\neg(x\in M\to B(x))\iff\underline{\qquad}$.
    Answer

    $x\in M\wedge\neg B(x)$ (Proof C with $A=x\in M$).

  2. Rung 2. Prove the mirror version $\neg\exists x\in M\,B(x)\iff\forall x\in M\,\neg B(x)$.
    Answer
    $$\begin{aligned}\neg\exists x\,(x\in M\wedge B(x))&\iff\forall x\,\neg(x\in M\wedge B(x))\\&\iff\forall x\,(x\notin M\vee\neg B(x))\\&\iff\forall x\,(x\in M\to\neg B(x)),\end{aligned}$$

    using the negation rule for $\exists$, De Morgan, and implication as OR. The last line is $\forall x\in M\,\neg B(x)$.

How to negate under exam pressure
  1. Write the statement with all its quantifiers in front, none hidden in words (“$\dots$ for all $y$” at the end becomes $\forall y$ at the front).
  2. Walk the NOT from left to right: every $\forall$ becomes $\exists$, every $\exists$ becomes $\forall$. The bounds ($\in M$, $>0$) stay as they are.
  3. Negate the core: $\neg(A\to B)$ becomes $A\wedge\neg B$; $\neg(A\wedge B)$ becomes $\neg A\vee\neg B$; $\neg(A\vee B)$ becomes $\neg A\wedge\neg B$; $\neg(x=y)$ becomes $x\ne y$; in ordered sets $\neg(x<y)$ becomes $x\ge y$.
  4. Cancel double negations, then read the result aloud in words as a check.

In short: flip every quantifier, keep the bounds, negate the core.

Worked example 5: negate “$R$ is free of zero divisors” (script p. 17)

A ring $R$ is free of zero divisors if $\forall a,b\in R:\ ab=0\to(a=0\vee b=0)$. Try the negation in your head first.

  1. Flip the quantifiers, keep the bound.
    Answer

    $\exists a,b\in R:$ $\neg\big(ab=0\to(a=0\vee b=0)\big)$.

  2. Negate the core with Proof C, then De Morgan.
    Answer

    $\exists a,b\in R:$ $ab=0\wedge\neg(a=0\vee b=0)$, i.e. $\exists a,b\in R:$ $ab=0\wedge a\ne0\wedge b\ne0$.

  3. Say it in words, and give an instance from lesson 1.
    Answer

    “There are two nonzero elements whose product is zero”: $R$ has zero divisors. In $\mathbb Z/6$: $a=\bar2$, $b=\bar3$. To prove that a ring is not free of zero divisors, you name such a pair, nothing more.

The empty domain

The lecture asks: is $\forall x\in\emptyset\,(x\ne x)$ true or false? Use duality:

$$\begin{aligned}\forall x\in\emptyset\,(x\ne x)&\iff\neg\exists x\in\emptyset\,\neg(x\ne x)\\&\iff\neg\exists x\in\emptyset\,(x=x).\end{aligned}$$

And “there is no $x\in\emptyset$ with $x=x$” is true, since there is no $x\in\emptyset$ at all. So every “for all” over the empty set is true, however absurd its core (it is a false premise, $x\in\emptyset$, implying anything). Every “there exists” over the empty set is false. This matters in lesson 6: the empty set of vectors is linearly independent, because the definition is a “for all” statement.

The order of quantifiers

Quantifiers of the same kind can be swapped: $\forall x\forall y$ means the same as $\forall y\forall x$, and likewise for $\exists$. Mixed quantifiers cannot be swapped. Compare, in $\mathbb Z$:

$$\begin{aligned}&\forall x\in\mathbb Z\ \exists y\in\mathbb Z\ (x+y=0) &&\text{true: take }y=-x,\\&\exists y\in\mathbb Z\ \forall x\in\mathbb Z\ (x+y=0) &&\text{false.}\end{aligned}$$

In the first, $y$ is chosen after $x$ and may depend on it: every number has its own negative. In the second, one $y$ must work for all $x$ at once. The script's field axiom “$\forall x\in K\ \exists x'\in K: x+x'=0$” (p. 18) is of the first kind: each element has its own inverse.

Proof E: $\exists\forall$ implies $\forall\exists$, but not conversely

Claim. For every formula $A(x,y)$: $\exists y\,\forall x\,A(x,y)\ \to$ $\forall x\,\exists y\,A(x,y)$ is true. The converse is false in general.

The idea. If one $y$ works for every $x$, then for each given $x$ that same $y$ is a witness. The other direction fails as soon as the witness has to change with $x$, as for $x+y=0$ in $\mathbb Z$.

What the definitions force (before any idea)
  • First line: “Assume $\exists y\,\forall x\,A(x,y)$; choose such a $y_0$.”
  • To show: $\forall x\,\exists y\,A(x,y)$. So: “Let $x$ be arbitrary.” Then name a $y$ with $A(x,y)$.
  • The gap: which $y$? The only candidate we have is $y_0$. For the converse: one counterexample.
The storyWhat you write

The charter says: “there is one locksmith who can open every door”. The scribe writes down his name.

Assume $\exists y\,\forall x\,A(x,y)$. Choose $y_0$ with $\forall x\,A(x,y_0)$.

Someone points at any door.

Let $x$ be arbitrary.

That door has a locksmith who opens it: the same man as always.

By the choice of $y_0$, $A(x,y_0)$ holds. So $\exists y\,A(x,y)$, with witness $y=y_0$. Since $x$ was arbitrary, $\forall x\,\exists y\,A(x,y)$. ∎

But “every door has some locksmith who opens it” does not give one master locksmith: each door may need its own.

Converse fails: in $\mathbb Z$ with $A(x,y)$: $x+y=0$, the statement $\forall x\,\exists y\,A$ is true ($y=-x$). But $\exists y\,\forall x\,A$ is false: for any $y$, take $x=1-y$; then $x+y=1\ne0$.

Moves used: name a witness take an arbitrary element one counterexample is enough

The proof in one piece

Assume $\exists y\,\forall x\,A(x,y)$ and choose $y_0$ with $\forall x\,A(x,y_0)$. Let $x$ be arbitrary. Then $A(x,y_0)$, so $\exists y\,A(x,y)$. As $x$ was arbitrary, $\forall x\,\exists y\,A(x,y)$. The converse fails: in $\mathbb Z$, $\forall x\,\exists y\,(x+y=0)$ holds with $y=-x$, but $\exists y\,\forall x\,(x+y=0)$ is false, since for every $y$ the element $x=1-y$ gives $x+y=1\ne0$. ∎

Your turn: the proof-writing ladder
  1. Rung 1. In the counterexample, why does “$x=1-y$” come after $y$?
    Answer

    We are disproving $\exists y\,\forall x$, i.e. proving its negation $\forall y\,\exists x\,(x+y\ne0)$. In that order, $y$ is given first and we may choose $x$ depending on it.

  2. Rung 2. Give another counterexample to the converse, in $\mathbb N$ with $A(x,y)$: $y>x$.
    Answer

    $\forall x\,\exists y\,(y>x)$ is true ($y=x+1$). $\exists y\,\forall x\,(y>x)$ is false: for any $y$, $x=y$ gives $y>y$, which is false. “Every number has a bigger one” does not give “there is a biggest”.

“There exists exactly one”: $\exists!$

The script defines a map by $\forall x\in X\ \exists!\,y\in Y: f(x)=y$ (p. 13): each argument has exactly one value. $\exists!$ is an abbreviation:

$$\exists!\,y\,A(y)\quad\text{means}\quad\exists y\,\big(A(y)\wedge\forall z\,(A(z)\to z=y)\big).$$

“There is one, and every other one is the same.” So proving $\exists!$ always has two parts: existence (name a $y$) and uniqueness (take two, show they are equal). Proofs A and B of lesson 1 were uniqueness parts.

Worked example 7: “All people make the same mistakes” (HS18, question VI)

Which formula is equivalent? (a) $\forall x,y\in\{\text{people}\}$ $\exists f\in\{\text{mistakes}\}:$ $(x\text{ makes }f)\wedge(y\text{ makes }f)$ (b) $\forall x\in\{\text{people}\}$ $\exists f,g\in\{\text{mistakes}\}:$ $(x\text{ makes }f)\Leftrightarrow(x\text{ makes }g)$ (c) $\forall f\in\{\text{mistakes}\}$ $\exists x,y\in\{\text{people}\}:$ $(x\text{ makes }f)\Rightarrow(y\text{ makes }f)$ (d) $\forall x,y\in\{\text{people}\}$ $\forall f\in\{\text{mistakes}\}:$ $(x\text{ makes }f)\Rightarrow(y\text{ makes }f)$

  1. Say precisely what “the same mistakes” means for two people $x$ and $y$.
    Answer

    Every mistake that $x$ makes, $y$ makes too (and the other way round). In formulas: $\forall f:$ $(x\text{ makes }f)\Rightarrow(y\text{ makes }f)$, for both orders of $x,y$.

  2. Now quantify over all pairs and compare with the options.
    Answer

    $\forall x,y$ $\forall f:$ $(x\text{ makes }f)\Rightarrow(y\text{ makes }f)$. Since it holds for all pairs, it holds for $(y,x)$ too, which gives the other direction. That is (d). 6. Equality

The lecture lists three provable properties of “$=$”:

$$\begin{aligned}&\forall x\,(x=x) &&\text{reflexive}\\&\forall x\forall y\,(x=y\to y=x) &&\text{symmetric}\\&\forall x\forall y\forall z\,\big((x=y\wedge y=z)\to x=z\big) &&\text{transitive}\end{aligned}$$

So “$=$” is an equivalence relation, as in Analysis (lesson 3 treats equivalence relations in general). Where do these come from? The handout Logik II fixes equality by three logical axioms. In words:

  • every object equals itself: $t=t$;
  • equals may replace equals in a relation: if $t_1=t_1'$, …, $t_n=t_n'$ and $R(t_1,\dots,t_n)$ holds, then $R(t_1',\dots,t_n')$ holds;
  • equal inputs give equal outputs: if $t_1=t_1'$, …, $t_n=t_n'$, then $F(t_1,\dots,t_n)=F(t_1',\dots,t_n')$ for every operation $F$.

Why this explains “well defined”

The last axiom is what you use every time you “apply the same operation to both sides of an equation”. It is also why, in lesson 1, the addition $\bar a+\bar b:=\overline{a+b}$ had to be proved well defined. Writing the formula is easy; but for $+$ to be an operation on $\mathbb Z/n$ at all, equal inputs must give equal outputs: if $\bar a=\overline{a'}$ and $\bar b=\overline{b'}$, then $\overline{a+b}=\overline{a'+b'}$. That is exactly the axiom “equal inputs give equal outputs” for the new symbol $+$, and Proof C of lesson 1 checked it. The script says the same thing in its definition of a map (p. 13): $\forall x\in X\ \exists!\,y\in Y: f(x)=y$, “and we can show that the assignment is unique”.

Why it's cool

Every definition in Linear Algebra is a sentence with quantifiers, and the negation rules turn any of them into the description of a counterexample before you have found one. “$M$ is not linearly independent” becomes “some vector of $M$ is a combination of the others”: now you know exactly what to look for. Logic also cuts the other way: a proof of a “for all” statement always starts with “let $x$ be arbitrary”, and a proof of an “exists” statement always ends with naming a witness. Half of the first line of every proof is decided by the quantifiers alone.

For wonder: one operator is enough

Halbeisen's notes mention the Sheffer stroke $A\mid B:=\neg(A\wedge B)$, “not both”, in electronics called NAND. Section 4 showed that $\neg$ and $\wedge$ suffice for everything. But $\neg A\iff A\mid A$ and $A\wedge B$ $\iff\neg(A\mid B)$ $\iff(A\mid B)\mid(A\mid B)$, so the stroke alone suffices. That is not a curiosity: the processor of your phone is built, at the bottom, from billions of copies of one NAND circuit. Exercise 4 asks you for $A\vee B$.

Worked example 11: Is $\forall x\,(A(x)\vee B(x))$ equivalent to $\forall x\,A(x)\vee\forall x\,B(x)$?

  1. Test it in $\mathbb Z$ with $A(x)$: “$x$ is even” and $B(x)$: “$x$ is odd”.
    Answer

    Left: every integer is even or odd, true. Right: “all integers are even, or all integers are odd”, false. So not equivalent.

  2. Which of the four combinations do work?
    Answer

    $\forall$ distributes over $\wedge$: $\forall x\,(A\wedge B)\iff\forall x\,A\wedge\forall x\,B$. $\exists$ distributes over $\vee$: $\exists x\,(A\vee B)\iff\exists x\,A\vee\exists x\,B$. The other two only hold in one direction: $\forall x\,A\vee\forall x\,B\to\forall x\,(A\vee B)$ and $\exists x\,(A\wedge B)\to\exists x\,A\wedge\exists x\,B$. A trap that appears in true/false questions.

Exercise 1 (Quiz 1)

Write the following formula using only the quantifier $\exists$, the relation symbol $=$ and the operators $\neg$, $\wedge$, with no double negations $\neg\neg$:

$$\forall x\forall y\forall z\,\Big(\big(x=y\vee\neg(y=z)\big)\to\forall u\,(u\ne y)\Big).$$
Hint

Name the parts: $A:=x=y\vee\neg(y=z)$ and $B:=\forall u\,\neg(u=y)$. Then handle the prefix $\forall x\forall y\forall z$, the arrow, $A$ and $B$ one at a time.

Solution

Frame: eliminate $\forall$, $\to$, $\vee$ with $\forall v\,C\iff\neg\exists v\,\neg C$, $\ C\to D\iff\neg(C\wedge\neg D)$, $\ C\vee D\iff\neg(\neg C\wedge\neg D)$; cancel every $\neg\neg$. Moves: a chain of named equivalences; flip every quantifier, negate the core.

The formula is $\forall x\forall y\forall z\,(A\to B)$ with $A$, $B$ as in the hint.

Prefix. Applying $\forall v\,C\iff\neg\exists v\,\neg C$ three times and cancelling the two double negations in between: $\forall x\forall y\forall z\,\Theta\iff\neg\exists x\exists y\exists z\,\neg\Theta$.

The arrow. $\neg(A\to B)\iff A\wedge\neg B$ (Proof C). So we need $\neg\exists x\exists y\exists z\,(A\wedge\neg B)$.

The premise. $A=(x=y)\vee\neg(y=z)$ $\iff\neg\big(\neg(x=y)\wedge\neg\neg(y=z)\big)$ $\iff\neg\big(\neg(x=y)\wedge y=z\big)$.

The conclusion. $\neg B=\neg\forall u\,\neg(u=y)$ $\iff\exists u\,\neg\neg(u=y)$ $\iff\exists u\,(u=y)$.

Result.

$$\neg\exists x\exists y\exists z\Big(\neg\big(\neg(x=y)\wedge y=z\big)\wedge\exists u\,(u=y)\Big).$$

Only $\exists$, $=$, $\neg$, $\wedge$ occur, and every $\neg$ is followed by a quantifier, a bracket or an equation, never by another $\neg$.

Exercise 2 (Quiz 5)

Write the following formula using only the quantifier $\forall$, the relation symbol $=$ and the operators $\neg$, $\wedge$, with no double negations:

$$\exists x\exists y\exists z\,\Big(\big(x=y\wedge y\ne z\big)\to\exists u\,(u\ne y)\Big).$$
Hint

Work from the inside out: first $\exists u\,(u\ne y)$, then the arrow, then the three $\exists$ starting with the innermost.

Solution

Frame: rules (R1) $C\to D\iff\neg(C\wedge\neg D)$, (R2) $\exists v\,C\iff\neg\forall v\,\neg C$, (R3) $\neg\neg C\iff C$, and $s\ne t$ is $\neg(s=t)$. Moves: a chain of named equivalences.

Inside. $\exists u\,(u\ne y)$ $\iff\neg\forall u\,\neg\neg(u=y)$ $\iff\neg\forall u\,(u=y)$ (R2, R3).

The arrow. With $C:=x=y\wedge\neg(y=z)$ and $D:=\neg\forall u\,(u=y)$, (R1) gives

$$\begin{aligned}C\to D&\iff\neg\big(C\wedge\neg\neg\forall u\,(u=y)\big)\\&\iff\neg\big(C\wedge\forall u\,(u=y)\big)=:\neg E.\end{aligned}$$

The three $\exists$. $\exists z\,\neg E$ $\iff\neg\forall z\,\neg\neg E$ $\iff\neg\forall z\,E$. Then $\exists y\,\neg\forall z\,E$ $\iff\neg\forall y\,\neg\neg\forall z\,E$ $\iff\neg\forall y\forall z\,E$, and in the same way $\exists x\,\neg\forall y\forall z\,E\iff\neg\forall x\forall y\forall z\,E$. Each time the negation from (R2) cancels the one already there.

Result.

$$\neg\forall x\forall y\forall z\Big(\big(x=y\wedge\neg(y=z)\big)\wedge\forall u\,(u=y)\Big).$$

Only $\forall$, $=$, $\neg$, $\wedge$, and no $\neg\neg$.

Exercise 3 (handout Tautologien: the chain rule)

Show that $\big((A\to B)\wedge(B\to C)\big)\to(A\to C)$ is a tautology. Do it without an 8-row table: find the only rows that could make it false.

Hint

An implication is false only if its premise is true and its conclusion false. When is $A\to C$ false?

Solution

Frame: assume the whole implication is false and derive a contradiction in the truth values. Moves: check with a truth table (only the rows that matter).

The whole statement is false only if its premise $(A\to B)\wedge(B\to C)$ is true and its conclusion $A\to C$ is false. $A\to C$ false means $A=1$, $C=0$. Then $A\to B$ true forces $B=1$ (since $A=1$), and $B\to C$ true forces $C=1$ (since $B=1$), contradicting $C=0$. So no row makes it false: it is a tautology. (Check: the oracle's example “Chain rule”.) This is the rule behind every chain of implications in a proof.

Exercise 4 (the Sheffer stroke)

With $A\mid B:=\neg(A\wedge B)$, write $\neg A$, $A\wedge B$ and $A\vee B$ using only $\mid$ (and brackets).

Solution

Frame: express via $\neg$, $\wedge$ first, then replace. Moves: a chain of named equivalences.

$\neg A\iff\neg(A\wedge A)=A\mid A$. Then $A\wedge B$ $\iff\neg\neg(A\wedge B)=\neg(A\mid B)$ $\iff(A\mid B)\mid(A\mid B)$. Finally, by De Morgan, $A\vee B$ $\iff\neg(\neg A\wedge\neg B)=\neg A\mid\neg B$ $\iff(A\mid A)\mid(B\mid B)$. Check the last one in the oracle by typing it as ¬(¬(A∧A) ∧ ¬(B∧B)) against A ∨ B.

Exercise 5 (the order of quantifiers)

True or false? Give a witness or a counterexample. (a) $\forall x\in\mathbb R\ \exists y\in\mathbb R:\ y>x$. (b) $\exists y\in\mathbb R\ \forall x\in\mathbb R:\ y>x$. (c) $\forall x\in\mathbb Q\ \exists y\in\mathbb Q:\ xy=1$. (d) $\exists y\in\mathbb Q\ \forall x\in\mathbb Q:\ xy=0$. (e) $\forall x\in\emptyset\ \exists y\in\emptyset:\ x=y$. (f) $\exists x\in\mathbb Z/6\ \forall y\in\mathbb Z/6:\ xy=\bar0$.

Solution

Frame: a true $\forall\exists$ needs a witness depending on $x$; a false statement needs the negation proved. Moves: name a witness; take an arbitrary element; one counterexample is enough.

(a) True: given $x$, take $y=x+1$. (b) False: its negation $\forall y\ \exists x:\ y\le x$ holds with $x=y$. (c) False: $x=0$ has no $y$ with $0\cdot y=1$. (It becomes true over $\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}$: the field axiom.) (d) True: $y=0$. (e) True: a “for all” over $\emptyset$. (f) True: $x=\bar0$. (A nonzero $x$ does not work: $\bar2\cdot\bar1=\bar2$.)

Exercise 6 (negating definitions from lesson 1)

Negate, and say the result in words: (a) “$\varphi:G\to H$ is a homomorphism”, i.e. $\forall a,b\in G:\ \varphi(a\circ b)=\varphi(a)\star\varphi(b)$. (b) “$(G,\circ)$ is abelian”. (c) “every $x\in G$ has an inverse”: $\forall x\in G\ \exists x'\in G:$ $(x\circ x'=e\wedge x'\circ x=e)$. (d) “$K$ is a field in which every element is a square”: $\forall x\in K\ \exists y\in K:\ x=y^2$.

Solution

Frame: flip every quantifier, keep the bounds, negate the core. Moves: flip every quantifier, negate the core.

(a) $\exists a,b\in G:\ \varphi(a\circ b)\ne\varphi(a)\star\varphi(b)$: “some pair is not respected”. To show a map is not a homomorphism, name one such pair.

(b) $\exists a,b\in G:\ a\circ b\ne b\circ a$: “two elements that don't commute”.

(c) $\exists x\in G$ $\forall x'\in G:$ $(x\circ x'\ne e\vee x'\circ x\ne e)$: “some member is undone by no partner”, the scribe's rebuttal from the story. Note the $\vee$: a candidate fails if it fails on either side.

(d) $\exists x\in K\ \forall y\in K:\ x\ne y^2$: “some element is not a square”. In $\mathbb Q$, $x=2$.

Exercise 7 (free and bound variables)

For each formula, say which variables are free, and whether it is a sentence. (a) $\forall x\,\exists y\,(x\circ y=e)$. (b) $\exists y\,(x\circ y=e)$. (c) $\exists y\,(x=y)\vee\forall x\,(x=y)$. (d) $\forall x\,\forall y\,(x=y\to y=x)$.

Solution

Frame: a variable is bound where it lies in the range of a quantifier on that variable; otherwise free. Moves: unfold the definition.

(a) No free variables: a sentence (“every element has a right inverse”). (b) $x$ is free, $y$ bound: not a sentence; it says “this $x$ has a right inverse”. (c) In the left half, $x$ is free and $y$ bound; in the right half, $x$ is bound and $y$ free. So both occur free: not a sentence. Renaming bound variables makes it readable: $\exists v\,(x=v)\vee\forall w\,(w=y)$. (d) A sentence (symmetry of $=$, a true one).

Exercise 8 (true or false, old exam style)

(a) $A\to B\iff B\to A$. (b) $\neg(A\vee B)\iff\neg A\vee\neg B$. (c) $\forall x\,(A(x)\wedge B(x))$ $\iff\forall x\,A(x)\wedge\forall x\,B(x)$. (d) $\exists x\,(A(x)\wedge B(x))$ $\iff\exists x\,A(x)\wedge\exists x\,B(x)$. (e) “$0=1\to1\ne1$” is true in $\mathbb N$. (f) The negation of “$\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0:\ P(\varepsilon,\delta)$” is “$\exists\varepsilon\le0\ \forall\delta\le0:\ \neg P(\varepsilon,\delta)$”.

Solution

(a) False: that is the converse; $A=0$, $B=1$ separates them. (b) False: De Morgan turns $\vee$ into $\wedge$: $\neg A\wedge\neg B$; $A=1$, $B=0$ separates them. (c) True (Worked example 11). (d) False: in $\mathbb Z$, “some $x$ is even and odd” is false, “some $x$ is even and some $x$ is odd” is true. (e) True: false premise. (f) False: bounds stay; correct is $\exists\varepsilon>0\ \forall\delta>0:\ \neg P(\varepsilon,\delta)$.
