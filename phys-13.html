Why it matters for the exam

The “Schwingungen” multiple-choice block almost always has one question on the phase at resonance, one on resonance and damping, and sometimes one on beats or coupled pendulums (FS23, FS24, HS23, FS26). HS25 had a whole 12-point problem on a pump on springs. The questions are conceptual: know the three limits by heart and what the phase does.

Recap of lesson 12 (3 min)

  1. What is the solution of $\ddot x+2\rho\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0$ for weak damping?

    Answer

    $x=Ae^{-\rho t}\sin(\omega t+\delta)$ with $\omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\rho^2}$: an oscillation whose amplitude decays with $e^{-\rho t}$.

  2. What do $\tau$ and $Q$ measure?

    Answer

    $\tau=\frac1{2\rho}$ is the time in which the energy drops to $\frac1e$; $Q=2\pi\frac\tau T$ is $2\pi$ times the stored energy over the energy lost per period. Weak damping, large $Q$.

  3. How do you solve an inhomogeneous linear ODE (Analysis)?

    Answer

    General solution of the homogeneous equation plus one particular solution of the inhomogeneous one. That is exactly today's structure.

1. The story: the singing bridge

The castle's rope bridge sways gently in the wind. When the guards march across in step, the captain shouts “Break step!”, and they walk out of rhythm. A child on the bridge discovers why: bouncing slowly does nothing, bouncing fast does nothing, but bouncing at just the right pace makes the whole bridge swing higher and higher.

In the tower the bell ringers tune two bells. Struck together, the bells don't sound steady: the sound swells and fades, “wah-wah-wah”, slower the closer the bells are to each other. And in the hall two pendulums hang side by side, joined by a thin string. The ringer starts one; slowly it calms down while the other one starts to swing, until the first stands still. Then it all flows back.

In physics: a periodic force drives an oscillator at its own rhythm $\Omega$. The response is small unless $\Omega$ is near the natural frequency $\omega_0$: that is resonance, limited only by the damping. Two oscillations of slightly different frequency add up to a beat. Two coupled oscillators have two normal modes; starting one pendulum excites both modes, and their beat moves the energy back and forth.

2. Forced oscillations

Drive the damped oscillator with $F(t)=F_0\cos\Omega t$ (section 9.4.1, page 245):

$$\ddot x+2\rho\dot x+\omega_0^2x=\frac{F_0}m\cos\Omega t.$$

As in Analysis, the general solution is the homogeneous one plus a particular one. The homogeneous part is the damped oscillation from lesson 12; it dies out like $e^{-\rho t}$. That is the transient. After a time $t\gg\frac1\rho$ only the particular solution is left, the steady state, and it oscillates with the frequency of the driver, not with its own:

$$x(t)=|x_0|\cos(\Omega t+\delta_0),\qquad|x_0|=\frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\Omega^2)^2+4\rho^2\Omega^2}},\qquad\tan\delta_0=\frac{-2\rho\Omega}{\omega_0^2-\Omega^2}.$$

The script finds it with the complex ansatz $x_p=x_0e^{i\Omega t}$ (page 246): inserting gives $x_0(\omega_0^2-\Omega^2+2i\rho\Omega)=\frac{F_0}m$, a single complex division. The formula sheet writes the amplitude as $C_2$ and the phase as $\phi$; same formulas. The phase $\delta_0$ runs from $0$ to $-\pi$: the oscillator lags behind the force.

Three limits (page 249, and on the formula sheet), worth knowing by heart:

DrivingAmplitudePhaseWhat sets it
slow, $\Omega\ll\omega_0$$\frac{F_0}{m\omega_0^2}=\frac{F_0}k$$0$ (in phase)the spring: the mass just follows the force
at resonance, $\Omega\approx\omega_0$$\frac{F_0}{2m\rho\omega_0}$$-\frac\pi2$the damping alone
fast, $\Omega\gg\omega_0$$\frac{F_0}{m\Omega^2}\to0$$-\pi$ (opposite)the inertia: the mass can't keep up

At resonance the velocity $\dot x$ is in phase with the force (a quarter period ahead of $x$), so the force pushes the mass forward all the time: that is why energy flows in best there.

Amplitude resonance (page 247). Setting the derivative of $|x_0|$ with respect to $\Omega$ to zero gives the maximum at

$$\Omega_{\text{res}}=\omega_0\sqrt{1-2\xi^2},\qquad\xi=\frac\rho{\omega_0},$$

a little below $\omega_0$, and only if $\xi<\frac1{\sqrt2}$. For stronger damping (critical damping included) there is no peak: the amplitude is largest at $\Omega\to0$ and falls from there. At $\Omega_{\text{res}}$ the phase lag is still less than $90^\circ$, because $\omega_0^2-\Omega_{\text{res}}^2>0$.

Power resonance (section 9.4.4, pages 249–250). In the steady state the energy of the oscillator doesn't change, so the power the driver puts in equals the power lost to friction, $F\dot x=2m\rho\dot x^2$ on average. The mean power absorbed is largest exactly at $\Omega=\omega_0$, not at $\Omega_{\text{res}}$. For weak damping the two are practically the same.

Try it: the resonance curve

Choose the driving frequency and the damping. The upper curve is the amplitude (in units of the static deflection $\frac{F_0}k$), the lower one the phase. Press Go to see the force (arrow) and the mass in the steady state.

With small $\xi$ the peak is tall and narrow and the phase jumps quickly from $0$ to $-180^\circ$. Push $\xi$ past $0.71$: the peak disappears. At $\Omega=\omega_0$ the peak height is $\frac1{2\xi}$, which for weak damping is the quality factor $Q$.

Worked example 1: a block on a spring, driven

A block of mass $m=2\,\mathrm{kg}$ on a spring with $k=200\,\mathrm{N/m}$ is damped with $\rho=0.5\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and driven with amplitude $F_0=1\,\mathrm N$. Find the steady-state amplitude for $\Omega=2$, $10$ and $50\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

  1. What is $\omega_0$, and which limit is each $\Omega$?

    Answer

    $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}=10\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. So $\Omega=2$ is slow, $10$ is resonance, $50$ is fast. The damping $\rho=0.5$ is weak ($\xi=0.05$).

  2. Use the three limits.

    Answer

    Slow: $\frac{F_0}k=5\,\mathrm{mm}$ (the exact formula gives $5.2\,\mathrm{mm}$). Resonance: $\frac{F_0}{2m\rho\omega_0}=\frac1{20}\,\mathrm m=5\,\mathrm{cm}$. Fast: $\frac{F_0}{m\Omega^2}=\frac1{5000}\,\mathrm m=0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$.

Result: the resonance amplitude is ten times the static one, and ten is $\frac1{2\xi}=Q$. Driving five times faster than $\omega_0$ shrinks it 25-fold below the static value: that is how machines are isolated from the floor (Exercise 7).

3. Superposition and beats

Oscillations add (the equations are linear). Two of the same frequency give again one of that frequency, with an amplitude between $|A_1-A_2|$ and $A_1+A_2$ depending on the phase difference (pages 251–255; formula sheet: $A=\sqrt{A_1^2+A_2^2+2A_1A_2\cos(\delta_1-\delta_2)}$). In phase they add, in antiphase they cancel.

Two of different frequency, same amplitude (section 9.5.3, page 255): the sum-to-product identity gives

$$A\cos\omega_1t+A\cos\omega_2t=\underbrace{2A\cos\left(\frac{\omega_1-\omega_2}2t\right)}_{\text{slow amplitude}}\cos\left(\frac{\omega_1+\omega_2}2t\right).$$

If $\omega_1\approx\omega_2$, this is a fast oscillation at the mean frequency whose amplitude swells and fades slowly: a beat. The script and the formula sheet call $\omega_s=\frac{|\omega_1-\omega_2|}2$ the beat (angular) frequency. Careful: the loudness has a maximum at every maximum and every minimum of the cosine, so you hear $|f_1-f_2|$ swells per second, twice $f_s$. The time between two silences is $\frac{2\pi}{|\omega_1-\omega_2|}$ (script, page 256).

Try it: beats

One oscillation has $f_1=10\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Choose the second.

Musicians tune by this: they adjust a string until the beats against a tuning fork slow down and vanish.

4. Coupled oscillators

Two identical pendulums (mass $m$, $\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}$) are joined by a weak spring $k$ (section 9.7.1, pages 270–274). For small swings, with $x_1$, $x_2$ the horizontal displacements, the spring pulls each with $k(x_1-x_2)$:

$$\ddot x_1+\omega_0^2x_1+\frac km(x_1-x_2)=0,\qquad\ddot x_2+\omega_0^2x_2-\frac km(x_1-x_2)=0.$$

Each equation contains both unknowns: the oscillators are coupled. The trick is to add and subtract the equations. With the normal coordinates $z_2=x_1+x_2$ and $z_1=x_1-x_2$:

$$\ddot z_2+\omega_0^2z_2=0,\qquad\ddot z_1+\left(\omega_0^2+\frac{2k}m\right)z_1=0.$$

Two independent harmonic oscillators, the normal modes:

  • In phase ($x_1=x_2$, only $z_2$): the spring is never stretched, frequency $\omega_2=\omega_0$. $z_2$ is twice the centre of mass, so the centre of mass always swings with $\omega_0$.
  • In antiphase ($x_1=-x_2$, only $z_1$): the spring works on both, frequency $\omega_1=\sqrt{\omega_0^2+\frac{2k}m}$, a little higher.

Start pendulum 1 at $A$ and pendulum 2 at rest: then $z_1=z_2=A$ at $t=0$, both modes are excited equally, and going back to $x_1=\frac12(z_1+z_2)$, $x_2=\frac12(z_2-z_1)$ gives (page 272)

$$x_1=A\cos\left(\frac{\omega_1-\omega_2}2t\right)\cos\left(\frac{\omega_1+\omega_2}2t\right),\qquad x_2=A\sin\left(\frac{\omega_1-\omega_2}2t\right)\sin\left(\frac{\omega_1+\omega_2}2t\right).$$

A beat in each pendulum, a quarter of the slow period apart: when one is still, the other has all the energy. For weak coupling the full exchange takes $\Delta t=\frac\pi{\omega_1-\omega_2}\approx\frac{\pi m\omega_0}k$.

Why it's cool

A crystal is a huge set of coupled oscillators, its atoms joined by springs (the chemical bonds). Its normal modes are sound waves, and in quantum physics their energy comes in packets called phonons, which carry heat through solids. Two coupled pendulums are the smallest crystal there is.

Try it: two coupled pendulums

Pendulum 1 starts deflected, pendulum 2 at rest. Choose the coupling and press Go (the time runs fast).

Weaker coupling, slower exchange, but the exchange is always complete because the pendulums are identical.

5. Fourier: every periodic motion is a sum of harmonics

Section 9.6 (pages 259–269) shows that every periodic function with period $T$ can be written as a sum of sines and cosines of the frequencies $\frac{2\pi}T,\frac{4\pi}T,\frac{6\pi}T,\dots$ (a Fourier series). Together with the linearity of the oscillator equation, this means: the response to any periodic force is a sum of the responses to cosine forces from section 2. That's why the script only treats $F_0\cos\Omega t$. The series themselves are Physics II and Analysis II material; no exam question so far has asked for one.

6. Exercises

Do them on paper. Exam style: no calculator, the formula sheet allowed. Exercises 1–6 are multiple choice, 7 is a long problem, 8 and 9 are practice problems in exam style (not from an exam).

Exercise 1 (FS24, Konzeptfrage 1.4(c), 1 point, original)

A weakly damped oscillator is driven. What is the phase difference $\varphi$ between the driving force and the oscillator at resonance? (A) $\varphi\approx0$ (B) $\varphi\approx-\pi/2$ (C) $\varphi\approx-\pi$ (D) $\varphi\approx2\pi$

Solution

B. At $\Omega=\omega_0$ the denominator $\omega_0^2-\Omega^2$ of $\tan\delta_0$ vanishes: $\delta_0=-\frac\pi2$. The oscillator lags a quarter period behind the force.

Exercise 2 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.4(b), 1 point, original)

A weakly damped pendulum with natural frequency $\omega_r$ is driven by $F(t)=F_0\cos\Omega t$. When is the power it dissipates per period largest? (A) When its amplitude is smallest. (B) When it swings in antiphase with the force. (C) When it swings in phase with the force. (D) For $\Omega\approx\omega_r$.

Solution

D. Power resonance is exactly at $\Omega=\omega_r$. There the displacement lags by $90^\circ$ and the velocity is in phase with the force; (B) and (C) are the two limits where little power flows.

Exercise 3 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.5(a), 1 point, original)

For a weakly damped driven system: what is the phase angle $\delta$ between force and displacement at exactly the frequency where the amplitude $|x_0|$ is largest (amplitude resonance)? (A) Less than $90^\circ$ (B) Exactly $90^\circ$ (C) More than $90^\circ$ (D) Exactly $180^\circ$

Solution

A. $\Omega_{\text{res}}=\omega_0\sqrt{1-2\xi^2}<\omega_0$, so $\omega_0^2-\Omega_{\text{res}}^2>0$ and $|\delta|=\arctan\frac{2\rho\Omega}{\omega_0^2-\Omega^2}<90^\circ$. Exactly $90^\circ$ belongs to $\Omega=\omega_0$, the power resonance.

Exercise 4 (FS26, Konzeptfrage 1.5(c), 1 point, original)

A critically damped system ($\rho=\omega_0$) is driven continuously with a harmonic force of frequency $\Omega$. Which is correct for the steady state? (A) The system won't oscillate at all, it just creeps to a constant displacement. (B) The phase shift stays below $90^\circ$ for every $\Omega$. (C) The smaller $\Omega$, the larger the amplitude. (D) The largest amplitude is reached at a finite $\Omega_{\text{res}}>0$.

Solution

C. With $\xi=1>\frac1{\sqrt2}$ the root in $\Omega_{\text{res}}=\omega_0\sqrt{1-2\xi^2}$ is imaginary: no peak, the amplitude only falls as $\Omega$ grows (D wrong). The steady state oscillates with $\Omega$, whatever the damping (A wrong). The phase still runs to $-180^\circ$ as $\Omega\to\infty$ (B wrong).

Exercise 5 (FS23, Konzeptfrage 1.5(c), 2 points, original)

$x(t)=A\cos\omega_1t+A\cos\omega_2t$ with $\omega_1=20\pi\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\omega_2=21\pi\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The original shows four plots, (a)–(d), with different time axes (open it). At which times is $x(t)$ silent, how many fast oscillations lie between two silences, and so which plot is right?

Solution

(b). $x=2A\cos\left(\frac\pi2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\,t\right)\cos\left(20.5\pi\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\,t\right)$. The slow factor vanishes at $t=1,3,5,\dots\,\mathrm s$: silences every $2\,\mathrm s$, with about $20$ fast oscillations ($10.25\,\mathrm{Hz}$) in between. Only plot (b) has its silences at $1\,\mathrm s$ and $3\,\mathrm s$ with about $20$ oscillations between them; (a) is silent at $2$ and $6\,\mathrm s$, while (c) and (d) are silent far too often.

Exercise 6 (HS23, Konzeptfrage 1.4(a), 1 point, original)

Two spring pendulums with balls of equal mass (coordinates $x_1$, $x_2$) are coupled by a massless spring. Which normal coordinate gives the oscillation frequency of the centre of mass in the $x$ direction? (A) $x_1+x_2$ (B) $x_1-x_2$ (C) $x_1\cdot x_2$ (D) $x_1/x_2$

Solution

A. For equal masses the centre of mass is $\frac12(x_1+x_2)$. The coupling spring cancels in the sum of the equations, so $x_1+x_2$ oscillates with the uncoupled frequency.

Exercise 7 (HS25, problem 4, “Schwingungsisolierung”, 12 points, original)

A pump of mass $m$ is bolted onto a concrete block of mass $M$, which sits on a spring $k$ on the floor; only motion in $x$ counts. (a) The pump is off: set up the equation of motion. (b) Find the natural frequency $\omega_0$. (c) The pump runs and acts with $F(t)=F_0\cos\Omega t$: add this to (a). (d) For $\Omega>\omega_0$, find a solution with the ansatz $x=x_0\cos\Omega t$, using $\eta=\Omega/\omega_0$. (e) Give the force $F_B(t)$ passed on to the floor. (f) In which range must $\omega_0$ lie compared with $\Omega$ for less than $10\,\%$ of the force to reach the floor? A damper with damping coefficient $\rho$ is added beside the spring. (g) Adapt the equation from (c). (h) It is solved by $x_D=x_{D,0}\cos(\Omega t+\phi)$ with $x_{D,0}=\frac{F_0/k}{\sqrt{(1-\eta^2)^2+4\frac{\rho^2}{\omega_0^2}\eta^2}}$. Give the force $F_{B,D}(t)$ on the floor and say how you would choose $\rho$. (i) Only $\rho\approx\omega_0$ is possible. Compare with the undamped case for A) $\Omega\ll\omega_0$, B) $\Omega\approx\omega_0$, C) $\Omega\gg\omega_0$: where is the isolation better?

Solution

(a) Pump and block move together: $(m+M)\ddot x=-kx$, so $\ddot x+\frac k{m+M}x=0$.

(b) $\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac k{m+M}}$.

(c) $(m+M)\ddot x+kx=F_0\cos\Omega t$.

(d) Insert: $x_0\left(k-(m+M)\Omega^2\right)=F_0$, so $x_0=\frac{F_0/k}{1-\eta^2}$. For $\Omega>\omega_0$ this is negative: the block moves opposite to the force (phase $-\pi$, the fast limit).

(e) The floor feels the spring: $F_B(t)=kx(t)=\frac{F_0}{1-\eta^2}\cos\Omega t$.

(f) $\frac{|F_B|}{F_0}=\frac1{\eta^2-1}<\frac1{10}$ means $\eta^2>11$: $\omega_0<\frac\Omega{\sqrt{11}}\approx0.3\,\Omega$. A soft spring and a heavy block.

(g) $(m+M)\ddot x+2(m+M)\rho\dot x+kx$ $=F_0\cos\Omega t$, that is $\ddot x+2\rho\dot x+\omega_0^2x=\frac{F_0}{m+M}\cos\Omega t$.

(h) Through the spring: $F_{B,D}=kx_D$ $=\frac{F_0}{\sqrt{(1-\eta^2)^2+4\xi^2\eta^2}}\cos(\Omega t+\phi)$ with $\xi=\frac\rho{\omega_0}$. The larger $\rho$, the larger the root, the smaller the force: choose $\rho$ as large as possible.

(i) With $\xi=1$ the root is $\sqrt{(1-\eta^2)^2+4\eta^2}=1+\eta^2$, so $\frac{|F_{B,D}|}{F_0}=\frac1{1+\eta^2}$ against $\frac1{|1-\eta^2|}$ without damping. A) $\eta\to0$: both $\approx1$, the whole force goes through either way; no gain. B) $\eta\approx1$: undamped, resonance, the transmitted force blows up; damped, $\frac12$. Much better. C) $\eta\gg1$: both $\approx\frac1{\eta^2}$; about the same. So the damper helps only near resonance (for example while the pump speeds up through $\omega_0$).

Beyond the exam: a real damper also pushes on the floor with $2(m+M)\rho\dot x$. Counting that force too, damping makes the isolation worse at high frequencies. Engineers therefore use as little damping as they can get away with.

Exercise 8 (practice, beats, 2 points)

A lute string and a tuning fork of $440\,\mathrm{Hz}$ sound together; you hear the loudness swell 3 times per second. (a) What can the string's frequency be? (b) You tighten the string a little and the swells get faster. Which was it, and what must you do?

Solution

(a) You hear $|f_1-f_2|=3\,\mathrm{Hz}$ swells (twice the formula sheet's $f_s=1.5\,\mathrm{Hz}$), so $f=437$ or $443\,\mathrm{Hz}$.

(b) Tightening raises the frequency. The difference grew, so the string was already too high: $443\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Loosen it until the beats vanish.

Exercise 9 (practice, coupled pendulums, 4 points)

Two pendulums of length $\ell=1\,\mathrm m$ and mass $m=0.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ are coupled by a spring with $k=0.25\,\mathrm{N/m}$; take $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. (a) Give both normal frequencies. (b) Pendulum 1 is started, pendulum 2 is at rest. After what time is pendulum 1 still for the first time? Use the weak-coupling approximation.

Solution

(a) In phase: $\omega_2=\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}=\sqrt{10}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\approx3.16\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. In antiphase: $\omega_1=\sqrt{\omega_0^2+\frac{2k}m}=\sqrt{11}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\approx3.32\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

(b) $x_1\propto\cos\left(\frac{\omega_1-\omega_2}2t\right)$ vanishes first at $t=\frac\pi{\omega_1-\omega_2}$. Weak coupling: $\omega_1-\omega_2\approx\frac k{m\omega_0}$, so $t\approx\frac{\pi m\omega_0}k=\frac{\pi\cdot0.5\cdot3.16}{0.25}\,\mathrm s\approx20\,\mathrm s$. All the energy is then in pendulum 2.

Scoring yourself: the exercises make 25 points. With 17 you're done. Exercises 1–4 are the questions that come up most often: the phase and the peak of the resonance curve.

7. For wonder (optional, 5 min)

The bridge that wobbled. When London's Millennium Bridge opened in 2000, it began to sway sideways under the crowd. The sway made people adjust their steps to it, which pushed it in step, a resonance that fed itself. Engineers added 91 dampers, and it has been calm since. (The more famous Tacoma Narrows collapse of 1940 was not simple resonance but a self-excited flutter driven by the wind.)

How a radio picks one station. The tuning circuit in a radio is a damped oscillator (an electric one, script table 9.1). Of all the stations' frequencies hitting the antenna, it responds strongly only to those near its own $\omega_0$: a resonance curve with a high $Q$. Turning the dial changes $\omega_0$.
