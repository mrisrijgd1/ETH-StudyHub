1. Warm-up (5 min)

Three quick questions. Answer each in your head, then open it.

  1. What is the pointwise limit of $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1]$?

    Answer

    $f(x)=0$ for $0\le x<1$ (since $x^n\to0$ there) and $f(1)=1$ (since $1^n=1$). All $f_n$ are continuous; the limit is not.

  2. True or false: if $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $X$, then $f_n\to f$ pointwise on $X$.

    Answer

    True. Uniform convergence gives one $N$ that works for all $x$ at once, so in particular it works for each fixed $x$. The converse is false ($x^n$ on $[0,1]$).

  3. What is the radius of convergence of $\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{z^n}{n!}$?

    Answer

    $R=\infty$. Ratio formula: $\frac{|a_n|}{|a_{n+1}|}=\frac{(n+1)!}{n!}=n+1\to\infty$. This series is $e^z$.

2. Recap card: lesson 15 (10 min)

Read this once, slowly. Everything in bold must come out exactly in the exam.

Lesson 15: uniform convergence

  • Pointwise: $\forall x\in X\ \forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall n\ge N:\ |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$. Here $N$ may depend on $x$.
  • Uniform: $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall n\ge N\ \forall x\in X:\ |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$. One $N$ for all $x$. The only difference is where “$\forall x$” stands.
  • Sup test: $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $X$ exactly when $d_n=\sup_{x\in X}|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$.
  • The recipe: (1) pointwise limit first; (2) yes: a bound $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le r_n$ without $x$, with $r_n\to0$; (3) no: points $x_n$ with $|f_n(x_n)-f(x_n)|\ge c>0$, or a discontinuous limit of continuous $f_n$.
  • Uniform limits keep continuity (Proof 1) and integrals: $\int_a^bf_n\to\int_a^bf$ (Proof 2). Not derivatives: $\frac{\sin(nx)}n\to0$ uniformly, but $\cos(nx)$ doesn't converge.
  • Weierstrass M-test: if $|g_k(x)|\le M_k$ for all $x$ and $\sum M_k<\infty$, then $\sum g_k$ converges absolutely and uniformly.
  • Watch the domain: $x^n\to0$ uniformly on $[0,r]$ for every $r<1$, but not on $[0,1)$ (the sup there is still $1$).

3. Recap card: lesson 16 (10 min)

Lesson 16: power series, exp, sin, cos

  • Radius of convergence of $\sum a_nz^n$: $R=\frac1{\limsup\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}}$ (with $\frac10=\infty$, $\frac1\infty=0$). The series converges absolutely for $|z|<R$, diverges for $|z|>R$, and converges uniformly on every disc $|z|\le S$ with $S<R$.
  • Ratio formula: if $\lim\frac{|a_n|}{|a_{n+1}|}$ exists, it equals $R$. Use it for factorials; use the root formula for $n$-th powers.
  • The boundary $|z|=R$ is unpredictable: $\sum z^n$, $\sum\frac{z^n}n$, $\sum\frac{z^n}{n^2}$ all have $R=1$ and behave differently at $z=1$ and $z=-1$. Check it by hand.
  • $e^z=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{z^n}{n!}$, $R=\infty$, and $e^{z+w}=e^ze^w$ (Cauchy product plus the binomial theorem).
  • $\sin z=\sum\frac{(-1)^nz^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}$, $\cos z=\sum\frac{(-1)^nz^{2n}}{(2n)!}$, and Euler: $e^{iz}=\cos z+i\sin z$. From it: $\cos z=\frac{e^{iz}+e^{-iz}}2$, $\sin z=\frac{e^{iz}-e^{-iz}}{2i}$, and the addition formulas.
  • Term-by-term integration: $F(x)=\sum\frac{c_n}{n+1}x^{n+1}$ has the same radius as $f(x)=\sum c_nx^n$, and $\int_a^bf=F(b)-F(a)$ inside the radius (Proof 3). Example: $\log(1+x)=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n}{n+1}x^{n+1}$ for $|x|<1$.
  • Limits with exp and log: write $a_n^{b_n}=\exp(b_n\log a_n)$, find the limit of the exponent, use continuity of $\exp$.

5. Write it on paper: three exam proofs (30 min)

For each proof: read the claim and the frame, close your eyes for ten seconds, then write the whole proof on paper without looking at the lesson. Only then open the fold and mark yourself with the grader's list.

Proof 1: uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous (Satz 7.48, Felder HS22)

Claim. Let $D\subseteq\mathbb R$ (or $\mathbb C$) and let $f_n:D\to\mathbb R$ (or $\mathbb C$) be continuous. If $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $D$, then $f$ is continuous. (Lesson 15, Proof B. Felder HS22 asked exactly this, as part (a) of a 7-point question.)

What the definitions force

First lines: “Let $x_0\in D$ and $\varepsilon>0$.” Last line: “so $|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x\in D$ with $|x-x_0|<\delta$; $x_0$ was arbitrary.” The gap: $\delta$ can only come from the continuity of one $f_N$, so fix that $N$ first, then walk in three hops.

The proof and the grader's list

Let $x_0\in D$ and $\varepsilon>0$. By uniform convergence there is $N$ with $|f_N(x)-f(x)|<\frac\varepsilon3$ for all $x\in D$. Since $f_N$ is continuous at $x_0$, there is $\delta>0$ with $|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|<\frac\varepsilon3$ for all $x\in D$ with $|x-x_0|<\delta$. For such $x$, $$\begin{aligned}&|f(x)-f(x_0)|\\&\quad\le|f(x)-f_N(x)|\\&\qquad+|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|\\&\qquad+|f_N(x_0)-f(x_0)|\\&\quad<\tfrac\varepsilon3+\tfrac\varepsilon3+\tfrac\varepsilon3=\varepsilon.\end{aligned}$$ So $f$ is continuous at $x_0$, and since $x_0$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • one $N$ with $|f_N-f|<\frac\varepsilon3$ for all $x\in D$, said explicitly (this is where “uniform” is used);
  • $\delta$ taken from the continuity of that one $f_N$ at $x_0$, chosen after $N$;
  • the three-term triangle inequality, each term below $\frac\varepsilon3$, with the reason for each;
  • the conclusion for arbitrary $x_0$.

In short: “fix $N$, then $\delta$, then three thirds”.

Proof 2: limit and integral can be swapped (Satz 7.49)

Claim. Let $f_n:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ be Riemann integrable and $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $[a,b]$. Then $f$ is Riemann integrable and $\int_a^bf_n\,dx\to\int_a^bf\,dx$. (Lesson 15, Proof C.)

What the definitions force

Two goals. Integrability: by the $\varepsilon$-criterion, for every $\varepsilon>0$ find step functions $u\le f\le o$ with $\int_a^b(o-u)$ small. The integrals: for every $\varepsilon>0$ an $N$ with $\left|\int f_n-\int f\right|$ small for $n\ge N$. The gap: $f$ lies in a thin tube around one $f_N$, and $f_N$ already has good step functions.

The proof and the grader's list

Integrability. Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $|f_N(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x\in[a,b]$, and step functions $u\le f_N\le o$ with $\int_a^b(o-u)\,dx<\varepsilon$. Then $u-\varepsilon$ and $o+\varepsilon$ are step functions with $u-\varepsilon\le f\le o+\varepsilon$, and $$\begin{aligned}&\int_a^b\big((o+\varepsilon)-(u-\varepsilon)\big)dx\\&\quad<\varepsilon\big(1+2(b-a)\big).\end{aligned}$$ As $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, $f$ is integrable by the $\varepsilon$-criterion.

Integrals. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $f-\varepsilon\le f_n\le f+\varepsilon$ on $[a,b]$ for all $n\ge N$. Monotonicity of the integral gives $\left|\int_a^bf_n-\int_a^bf\right|\le\varepsilon(b-a)$ for $n\ge N$. Hence $\int_a^bf_n\to\int_a^bf$. ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • the tube: one $N$ with $f_N-\varepsilon\le f\le f_N+\varepsilon$ on all of $[a,b]$;
  • step functions $u\le f_N\le o$ with $\int(o-u)<\varepsilon$, shifted to $u-\varepsilon\le f\le o+\varepsilon$;
  • the gap $\varepsilon(1+2(b-a))$ and the $\varepsilon$-criterion named;
  • the integrals: monotonicity turns the tube into $\left|\int f_n-\int f\right|\le\varepsilon(b-a)$.

In short: “tube, squeeze $f_N$, shift by $\varepsilon$, then integrate the tube”. The last step alone is often worth half the points.

Proof 3: power series can be integrated term by term (Satz 7.85, winter 2019 exam)

Claim. Let $f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_nx^n$ have radius of convergence $R>0$. Then (a) $F(x)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{c_n}{n+1}x^{n+1}$ has the same radius $R$, and (b) $\int_a^bf\,dx=F(b)-F(a)$ for all $a,b\in(-R,R)$. (Lesson 16, Proof E. The winter 2019 exam asked exactly this: (a) for 2 points, (b) for 3.)

What the definitions force

(a) Let $S$ be the radius of $F$; show $S\ge R$ and $R\ge S$, each by comparing terms. (b) Last line: “$\int_a^bf=\lim_N\int_a^bf_N=\lim_N(F_N(b)-F_N(a))=F(b)-F(a)$.” The gap: why the first equality holds. It is lesson 15's swap (Proof 2), so you need uniform convergence on $[a,b]$.

The proof and the grader's list

(a) Radius. Let $S$ be the radius of $F$. If $|x|<R$, then $\left|\frac{c_n}{n+1}x^{n+1}\right|\le|x|\,|c_nx^n|$, so $F(x)$ converges absolutely by comparison: $S\ge R$. If $|x|<|y|<S$, the terms of $F(y)$ tend to $0$, so they are bounded by some $M$. With $q=\frac{|x|}{|y|}<1$: $$\begin{aligned}|c_nx^n|&=\left|\tfrac{c_n}{n+1}y^{n+1}\right|\frac{n+1}{|y|}\,q^n\\&\le\frac M{|y|}(n+1)q^n,\end{aligned}$$ and $\sum(n+1)q^n$ converges by the ratio test. So $f(x)$ converges absolutely: $R\ge S$.

(b) Integral. Let $a,b\in(-R,R)$ and $f_N=\sum_{n=0}^Nc_nx^n$. Since $\max\{|a|,|b|\}<R$, the partial sums converge uniformly to $f$ on $[a,b]$ (power series converge uniformly on every smaller disc). For polynomials, $\int_a^bf_N=F_N(b)-F_N(a)$. By the swap theorem (Proof 2), $$\begin{aligned}\int_a^bf\,dx&=\lim_N\int_a^bf_N\,dx\\&=\lim_N\bigl(F_N(b)-F_N(a)\bigr)\\&=F(b)-F(a).\end{aligned}$$ ∎

A grader gives the points for:

  • $S\ge R$: the term comparison $\left|\frac{c_n}{n+1}x^{n+1}\right|\le|x|\,|c_nx^n|$;
  • $R\ge S$: stepping out to $y$, bounded terms, the bound $\frac M{|y|}(n+1)q^n$ and the ratio test;
  • uniform convergence of the partial sums on $[a,b]$, with the reason $\max\{|a|,|b|\}<R$;
  • polynomials integrate term by term, then the swap theorem named.

In short: “compare, step out and bound, polynomials, uniform swap”.

6. Spot the mistake (5 min)

A student is asked whether $f_n(x)=x^n$ converges uniformly on $[0,1)$ and hands in this. The conclusion is wrong. Where exactly does it break?

“Solution.” For every $x\in[0,1)$ we have $x^n\to0$, so the pointwise limit is $f=0$, which is continuous. All $f_n$ are continuous and the limit is continuous, so by the theorem on uniform limits the convergence is uniform.

The mistake

The theorem (Proof 1) says: uniform convergence $\Rightarrow$ continuous limit. The student used the converse, which is false. A continuous limit proves nothing.

The right test is the sup: $\sup_{x\in[0,1)}|x^n-0|=1$ for every $n$ (take $x$ close to $1$), so $d_n=1\not\to0$ and the convergence is not uniform. On $[0,r]$ with $r<1$ it is uniform, since there $d_n=r^n\to0$. Exam lesson: Proof 1 can only ever rule uniform convergence out (when the limit jumps); to prove it, bound the sup.

7. The official Lernblatt (10 min)

Each week the course team publishes a Lernblatt: a short German summary of that week's script sections, with a list of quick exercises. It is official material, so if it and StudyHub ever disagree, the Lernblatt is right (and please tell me). This unit matches Lernblatt week 9 (sections 8 to 14) and Lernblatt week 10 (all of it).

How to use it: skim the PDF once (about 5 minutes; the German terms are the ones in the lessons' word tables). Then read the box below and answer the quick checks in your head.

Worth a second look

  • The ratio test with limsup and liminf: for $r_n=\frac{|a_{n+1}|}{|a_n|}$, $\limsup r_n<1$ gives absolute convergence and $\liminf r_n>1$ gives divergence. If $\liminf r_n\le1\le\limsup r_n$, the test says nothing; $\limsup r_n>1$ alone does not prove divergence.
  • The Cauchy product of two absolutely convergent series: $c_n=\sum_{k=0}^na_kb_{n-k}$ and $\sum c_n=\left(\sum a_n\right)\left(\sum b_n\right)$. Every term of one series meets every term of the other once; summing only $a_nb_n$ would leave most products out.
  • The order of the quantifiers is the whole difference: pointwise is $\forall x\ \forall\varepsilon\ \exists N$, uniform is $\forall\varepsilon\ \exists N\ \forall x$.
  • Abel’s theorem (new compared with lesson 16, where it was only stated): if the series converges at the boundary point $R$, then $\lim_{x\uparrow R}\sum a_nx^n=\sum a_nR^n$. The assumption is needed: $\sum(-1)^nx^n=\frac1{1+x}\to\frac12$ as $x\uparrow1$, but $1-1+1-\dots$ diverges.
  • $|e^z|=e^{\operatorname{Re}z}$, so $e^z$ is never $0$ and $|e^{iy}|=1$ for real $y$. Example: $|e^{-2+3i}|=e^{-2}$.
  • Polar form (lesson 16 skipped it; the Lernblatt has it): every $z\ne0$ is $z=re^{i\theta}=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ with $r=|z|$, and $\theta$ is fixed up to multiples of $2\pi$. Multiplying multiplies the moduli and adds the angles: $1+i=\sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}$, so $(1+i)^2=2e^{i\pi/2}=2i$.
  • The principal logarithm (also skipped in lesson 16): $\operatorname{Log}z=\log|z|+i\operatorname{Arg}z$ with $\operatorname{Arg}z\in(-\pi,\pi]$. So $\operatorname{Log}(-1)=i\pi$ and $\operatorname{Log}(-i)=-\frac{i\pi}2$. A unique log is only possible after choosing the angle range, because $e^{w+2\pi i}=e^w$.
  • Hyperbolic functions: $\cosh x=\frac{e^x+e^{-x}}2$, $\sinh x=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}2$, $\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1$, $\sinh^\prime=\cosh$, $\cosh^\prime=\sinh$.

Quick checks from the Lernblatt

Translated from the Lernblatt's exercise list. The Lernblatt has no official answers, so the answers here are StudyHub-written.

  1. Let $a_n=b_n=2^{-n}$ for $n\ge0$. Find $c_n$ in the Cauchy product and the value of $\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n$.

    Answer

    $c_n=\sum_{k=0}^n2^{-k}2^{-(n-k)}=(n+1)2^{-n}$. Both series are absolutely convergent with sum $2$, so $\sum c_n=2\cdot2=4$.

  2. Let $f_n(x)=x+\frac1n$ on $[0,1]$. Find the limit function and the sup error. May limit and integral be swapped here?

    Answer

    $f(x)=x$, and the error is $\frac1n$ at every point, so the sup error is $\frac1n\to0$: uniform. So yes (Proof 2): $\int_0^1f_n=\frac12+\frac1n\to\frac12=\int_0^1x\,dx$.

  3. The tents from the Lernblatt satisfy $f_n\to0$ pointwise and $\int_0^1f_n=\frac14$ for all $n$. Is the convergence uniform? May limit and integral be swapped?

    Answer

    No to both. If it were uniform, Proof 2 would give $\int f_n\to\int0=0$, but the integrals stay $\frac14$. So the convergence is not uniform, and $\lim\int f_n=\frac14\ne0=\int\lim f_n$.

  4. Does $x^n$ converge uniformly to $0$ on $[0,\frac12]$? Give the largest error.

    Answer

    Yes. The largest error is $\sup_{[0,1/2]}x^n=\left(\frac12\right)^n\to0$.

  5. Let $f_n,f$ be integrable on $[0,2]$ with $\sup_{[0,2]}|f_n-f|\le\frac1n$. How far apart can their integrals be?

    Answer

    At most $\frac2n$: $\left|\int_0^2f_n-\int_0^2f\right|\le\int_0^2|f_n-f|\le2\cdot\frac1n$.

  6. Find the radius $R$ and the sum of $\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(\frac z2\right)^n$ for $|z|<R$.

    Answer

    A geometric series with $q=\frac z2$: it converges exactly for $|z|<2$, so $R=2$, and the sum is $\frac1{1-z/2}=\frac2{2-z}$.

  7. $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{z^n}n$ has radius $1$. Does it converge at $z=1$? At $z=-1$?

    Answer

    At $z=1$ it is the harmonic series: diverges. At $z=-1$ it is $\sum\frac{(-1)^n}n$: converges by Leibniz. The boundary really is unpredictable.

  8. For $|x|<1$, $\sum_{n=0}^\infty(-1)^nx^n=\frac1{1+x}$. May you conclude from Abel’s theorem that $\sum_{n=0}^\infty(-1)^n=\frac12$?

    Answer

    No. Abel’s theorem needs the boundary series to converge, and $\sum(-1)^n$ diverges (its terms don’t tend to $0$).

  9. Compute $|e^{2+i}|$, $e^{i\pi}$ and $e^{i\pi/2}$.

    Answer

    $|e^{2+i}|=e^{\operatorname{Re}(2+i)}=e^2$. $e^{i\pi}=\cos\pi+i\sin\pi=-1$ and $e^{i\pi/2}=i$.

  10. Write $-4$ in polar form and find its two square roots. Then compute $\operatorname{Log}(-1)$ and $\operatorname{Log}(-i)$.

    Answer

    $-4=4e^{i\pi}$. Square roots: halve the angle, take the root of the modulus: $2e^{i\pi/2}=2i$ and $-2i$. $\operatorname{Log}(-1)=\log1+i\pi=i\pi$, $\operatorname{Log}(-i)=-\frac{i\pi}2$.

  11. Which series $F$ belongs to $f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n$ by term-by-term integration? What is its radius?

    Answer

    $F(x)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}=x+\frac{x^2}2+\frac{x^3}3+\dots$, radius $1$ (the same as $f$, by Proof 3). Since $f(x)=\frac1{1-x}$, $F(x)=\int_0^x\frac{dt}{1-t}=-\log(1-x)$.

  12. Compute $\sinh0$, $\cosh0$ and $\int_0^1\cosh x\,dx$.

    Answer

    $\sinh0=0$, $\cosh0=1$, and $\int_0^1\cosh x\,dx=\sinh1=\frac{e-e^{-1}}2$.
